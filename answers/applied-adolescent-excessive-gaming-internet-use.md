## Exam answer

**Case summary.** A 16-year-old boy has spent about a year on excessive computer and mobile use. He neglects important activities and responsibilities, shows little concern for family and social engagement, and his academic performance has declined significantly.

**Provisional diagnosis:** **Gaming disorder, predominantly online (ICD-11 6C51.0)**, if the content is gaming. Alternatives:
- **other specified disorder due to addictive behaviours** (problematic internet, social media or smartphone use);
- DSM-5-TR **Internet gaming disorder** (Section III).

The **12-month duration**, **increasing priority** over other activities, and **significant academic and social impairment** fit ICD-11 [1, 2]. **Comorbidity must be excluded actively**: ADHD, depression, social anxiety, ASD.

### (a) Further history and assessment
1. **The behaviour:**
   - what he does online: gaming (which games; multiplayer; loot boxes and purchases), social media, streaming, pornography, **online betting, fantasy sports or rummy**;
   - hours per day; night-time use;
   - onset and course; what triggered it (pandemic online schooling, school transition, bullying, breakup).
2. **Addiction components** [1]:
   - **salience** (preoccupation when offline);
   - mood modification (escape from stress or low mood);
   - **tolerance** (more time needed);
   - **withdrawal** (irritability or aggression when the device is removed);
   - conflict;
   - relapse after attempts to cut down;
   - **impaired control** (ICD-11).
3. **Impairment:**
   - school attendance and grades; dropout risk;
   - sleep (delayed phase, insomnia);
   - eating and hygiene;
   - peer relationships (online vs offline friends);
   - family conflict; violence.
4. **Comorbidity screening:**
   - **ADHD** (childhood inattention and hyperactivity; strongest association);
   - **depression** (anhedonia, withdrawal, suicidal ideation);
   - **social anxiety / avoidant traits** (online interaction as safety);
   - **ASD** (restricted interests, social difficulties);
   - substance use (tobacco, cannabis, alcohol);
   - specific learning disorder (academic failure that predates use);
   - OCD.
5. **Risk:**
   - self-harm and suicidality;
   - aggression;
   - **cyberbullying victimisation**; online sexual exploitation and grooming (POCSO);
   - financial losses (stolen money, parents' cards).
6. **Family:**
   - parenting style (permissive, authoritarian, inconsistent);
   - parental screen use;
   - marital conflict;
   - family history of addiction or mood disorder;
   - academic pressure.
7. **Developmental and personal history:** temperament, milestones, school history, hobbies before the problem.
8. **Mental state examination:** mood, anxiety, cognitive distortions about gaming, insight, motivation (stage of change).
9. **Rating scales:**
   - IGDS9-SF; Young's Internet Addiction Test; Smartphone Addiction Scale – Short Version;
   - Vanderbilt or Conners (ADHD); PHQ-A (depression); SCARED (anxiety);
   - teacher reports.
10. **Physical examination and investigations:** BMI; posture and eye strain; sleep diary. Investigations as clinically indicated (thyroid if fatigue).

### (b) Differential diagnosis
| Possibility | Distinguishing features |
| --- | --- |
| **Gaming disorder (ICD-11)** | Impaired control, priority, continuation despite harm, impairment ≥12 months |
| Problematic internet / social media use (not formally classified) | Similar features but non-gaming content |
| **High engagement (non-pathological)** | Lots of time but no loss of control, no impairment |
| **Depressive disorder** with secondary withdrawal into internet use | Pervasive low mood and anhedonia; gaming used to escape |
| **ADHD** | Childhood onset of inattention and hyperactivity across settings; gaming gives immediate rewards |
| Social anxiety disorder | Fear of evaluation; online interaction is safe |
| ASD | Lifelong social-communication deficits; gaming as a restricted interest |
| Gambling disorder (online) | Real-money stakes |
| Bipolar disorder | Episodic, with mood elevation and reduced need for sleep |
| Conduct or oppositional disorder; family dysfunction | Broader rule-breaking; gaming as one aspect |

### (c) Management
**Setting:** outpatient (with a specialised clinic such as NIMHANS SHUT if available). Admit only for severe depression, suicidality, aggression or treatment failure.

1. **Engagement and psychoeducation:**
   - non-judgemental stance;
   - explain the disorder and its link with mood and sleep;
   - set **reduction goals** (complete abstinence from devices is unrealistic).
2. **Motivational interviewing:** explore ambivalence; decisional balance (costs and benefits); link change to his own goals (exams, career, friends).
3. **CBT for internet and gaming addiction** (Young's CBT-IA: behaviour modification, cognitive restructuring, harm reduction):
   - self-monitoring diary;
   - identify triggers (boredom, stress, conflict);
   - challenge cognitions ("I'm only valued in the game");
   - **stimulus control:** devices out of the bedroom at night, scheduled use, app limits;
   - **alternative rewarding activities** (sports, hobbies, offline friends);
   - problem-solving; relapse prevention.
4. **Family intervention:**
   - **parent management training** principles: clear, consistent, negotiated rules; reward offline behaviour; avoid sudden total confiscation (which can provoke aggression);
   - a family media plan;
   - parental modelling;
   - reduce criticism and high expressed emotion;
   - address family conflict.
5. **School liaison:** gradual re-engagement; remedial support; counsellor involvement; check for bullying.
6. **Sleep restoration:** fixed wake time; no screens 1 hour before bed; morning light.
7. **Treat comorbidity:**
   - **ADHD:** methylphenidate (reduces gaming in comorbid ADHD) or atomoxetine;
   - **depression:** CBT with or without an SSRI (fluoxetine);
   - social anxiety: CBT.
8. **Pharmacotherapy for gaming disorder itself:** none approved. Bupropion has small trials [general knowledge].
9. **Group therapy and peer support; family support groups.**
10. **Follow-up:** relapse risk is high, especially during holidays and exams. Use booster sessions.

**Prognosis:** better with early intervention, family engagement, treated comorbidity and alternative activities. Many adolescents improve as they mature, but severe cases persist.

## In depth

### Background
- **ICD-11** included gaming disorder in 2019, the first non-gambling behavioural addiction in a major classification. DSM-5 placed internet gaming disorder in Section III (2013) [general knowledge; see also `behavioural-addictions`].
- **Griffiths' components model** (salience, mood modification, tolerance, withdrawal, conflict, relapse) is the basis of most scales [1].

### Epidemiology
- Gaming disorder about 2–3% of adolescents worldwide; male:female about 2.5:1.
- India: school-based studies report high rates of problematic internet use. Pandemic online schooling increased screen time markedly [Likely; check].

### Classification and criteria
**DSM-5-TR Internet gaming disorder (Section III)**: 5 or more of 9 in 12 months:
1. Preoccupation.
2. Withdrawal when gaming is taken away.
3. Tolerance.
4. Unsuccessful attempts to control.
5. Loss of interest in other hobbies.
6. Continued excessive use despite problems.
7. Deceiving family about gaming.
8. Use to escape or relieve negative mood.
9. Jeopardised or lost relationships, education or job.

**ICD-11 6C51:** impaired control; increasing priority; continuation despite harm; significant impairment; usually 12 months. Subtypes: predominantly online or offline. Hazardous gaming (QE22) is a pre-disorder category.

### Mechanism in detail
- **Dual-systems model:** the adolescent reward system (ventral striatum) matures before prefrontal control.
- **Game design:**
  - variable-ratio rewards (loot boxes);
  - progression systems;
  - social obligation (guilds, raids);
  - fear of missing out;
  - no natural stopping points.
- **Neuroimaging:** altered cue reactivity and reduced inhibitory control, similar to SUD (general knowledge).
- **I-PACE model:** person factors plus affective and cognitive responses plus executive control [general knowledge].

### Clinical features in detail
- Night-long gaming, daytime sleep, school refusal.
- **Aggression** when devices are removed (a common reason for presentation).
- Neglect of hygiene and meals.
- Lying about use; stealing money for in-game purchases.
- Social withdrawal from offline peers (hikikomori-like in severe cases).

### Assessment in detail
- Use **multiple informants**: adolescents often minimise.
- **Sleep diary and device usage logs** (screen-time reports) provide objective data.
- Screen for **online risks**: grooming, sextortion, cyberbullying. If sexual exploitation is disclosed, POCSO reporting applies.

### Management details
- **Evidence:**
  - CBT has the best evidence (meta-analyses show reduced symptoms and time spent; small studies);
  - family therapy (multi-family group therapy in Korea);
  - **PIPATIC** (Spanish adolescent programme combining CBT and family work) [general knowledge].
- **Residential "digital detox" camps** (South Korea, China) lack robust evidence. Some coercive "boot camps" have caused harm.
- **Pharmacological:** bupropion (Han 2010) and methylphenidate in ADHD comorbidity reduce craving and use (small trials) [general knowledge].

### Evidence table
| Study | Finding |
| --- | --- |
| Griffiths 1996 | Components model; adolescent fruit-machine addiction [1] |
| Hodgins 2011, *Lancet* | CBT and MI effective for gambling (model for gaming) [2] |
| Stevens 2021 meta-analysis (general knowledge) | Gaming disorder about 3% |
| Han 2009/2010 (general knowledge) | Methylphenidate and bupropion reduce gaming in ADHD/MDD comorbidity |
| King 2017 systematic review (general knowledge) | Treatment evidence weak; CBT most promising |

### Critical appraisal
- **Risk of over-diagnosis:** time spent is not equal to disorder; impairment is the key.
- **Comorbidity confound:** treating ADHD or depression may resolve the "addiction".
- **Cultural factors:** Indian academic pressure; family structure; limited outdoor spaces in cities.

### Indian context
- **NIMHANS SHUT clinic** (2014): assessment, CBT and family therapy for technology addiction [Likely].
- **Regulation:**
  - 2025 national ban on real-money online gaming [general knowledge; check];
  - Ministry of Education guidelines on screen time for online classes (PRAGYATA, 2020) [Likely].
- **Tele-MANAS** can be a first contact.

### Recent advances
- App-based monitoring and just-in-time interventions.
- rTMS trials for gaming craving.
- **Design regulation:** loot-box bans; age-appropriate design codes.
