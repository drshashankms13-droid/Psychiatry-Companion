## Exam answer

**Definition of the debate.** Lithium (Cade 1949; Schou 1954) is called the **gold standard** mood stabiliser. Second-generation antipsychotics (SGAs: quetiapine, olanzapine, aripiprazole, risperidone/paliperidone LAI, asenapine, cariprazine, lurasidone) now dominate prescribing. Three related statements are examined here: "lithium is the most effective medication", "lithium is the ideal medication", and "atypicals are better mood stabilisers than lithium". The evidence supports lithium as **the best single maintenance agent overall**, with unique anti-suicidal and possibly neuroprotective effects. SGAs are **faster and broader in acute phases** (especially mania and bipolar depression) and easier to start. Neither is "ideal" for every patient [1].

A **mood stabiliser** is a drug effective in the treatment of mania and depression and in preventing both, without inducing switch (Bauer & Mitchner 2004). Lithium meets most of these criteria; quetiapine and olanzapine meet several.

### Mechanism
- **Lithium:** GSK-3β inhibition, inositol depletion (IMPase), modulation of CREB, Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase and calcium signalling → neuroprotection, hippocampal neurogenesis, BDNF and Bcl-2 increase, circadian stabilisation, anti-suicidal effect (reduced impulsivity and aggression) [1].
- **SGAs:** D2 blockade or partial agonism + 5-HT2A antagonism → rapid antimanic effect. Antidepressant effect comes from non-D2 actions (norquetiapine NET inhibition, 5-HT7, 5-HT1A) [1].

### Head-to-head comparison

| Domain | Lithium | Atypical antipsychotics |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Acute mania | Effective (level 0.8–1.0); slower (7–14 days); less so in mixed, psychotic or rapid-cycling mania [1] | Faster; effective in mixed and psychotic mania; olanzapine, risperidone and haloperidol rank highest in network meta-analysis (Cipriani 2011) |
| Bipolar depression (acute) | Modest, methodologically weak evidence [1] | Quetiapine, lurasidone, olanzapine–fluoxetine, cariprazine, lumateperone effective [1] |
| Prevention of mania | Strong | Strong (olanzapine, quetiapine, aripiprazole, risperidone LAI) [1] |
| Prevention of depression | Moderate–strong | Quetiapine strong; aripiprazole and risperidone mostly prevent mania only [1] |
| Overall maintenance | **Best-performing mood stabiliser in practice**; effectiveness similar to LAIs (Lähteenvuo 2018) [1] | Quetiapine comparable to lithium in trials (Weisler 2011) |
| Suicide | **Reduces attempted and completed suicide by ~80%** (Cipriani 2005; Baldessarini) [1] | No proven anti-suicidal effect (except clozapine in schizophrenia) |
| Neuroprotection / dementia | Possible protection (lower dementia rates; mixed data) [1] | None shown; possible grey matter loss |
| Adverse effects | Narrow therapeutic index; toxicity; renal (NDI, CKD after more than 15 years), thyroid (up to 20% of middle-aged women), hyperparathyroidism (~4%), tremor, weight gain, teratogenicity (small Ebstein risk) [1] | Metabolic syndrome, weight gain, EPS and akathisia, prolactin, sedation, TD [1] |
| Monitoring | Levels, eGFR, TFTs, calcium every 6 months [1] | Metabolic monitoring |
| Rebound | Abrupt stopping → manic relapse [1] | Withdrawal and supersensitivity |
| Cost | Very cheap | Cheap (generics) to expensive (newer) |
| Predictors of response | Classic euphoric mania, episodic course with full interepisode recovery, family history of lithium response, mania–depression–interval sequence | Broader range including mixed and psychotic presentations |

### Assessment (choosing between them)
- Polarity predominance (manic vs depressive).
- Episode pattern (classic vs rapid cycling/mixed).
- Psychotic features; suicide history (favours lithium).
- Renal, thyroid and cardiac status; metabolic risk; pregnancy plans.
- Adherence and capacity to attend for monitoring.
- Family history of lithium response.

### Management principles
1. **Acute mania:** SGA (or haloperidol) ± lithium/valproate for speed; lithium monotherapy in classic non-psychotic mania if waiting is acceptable [1].
2. **Bipolar depression:** quetiapine, lurasidone, olanzapine–fluoxetine, lamotrigine; lithium for suicidality [1].
3. **Maintenance:** lithium first line (BAP, NICE, CANMAT); quetiapine, aripiprazole/LAIs or lamotrigine as alternatives or combinations. Lithium + quetiapine/valproate for breakthrough episodes [1].
4. **Target level:** 0.6–0.8 mmol/L for maintenance (0.4–0.6 if response is good but tolerance poor; 0.8–1.0 if response is insufficient and tolerance good) [1].

### Key evidence
- **BALANCE (Geddes 2010, n=330):** lithium alone or lithium + valproate better than valproate alone for relapse prevention.
- **Weisler 2011 (n=1,226):** quetiapine and lithium both superior to placebo in maintenance (quetiapine continuation vs switch).
- **Cipriani 2011 network meta-analysis (mania):** antipsychotics more effective than mood stabilisers in acute mania.
- **Cipriani 2005 / 2013 meta-analyses:** lithium reduces suicide (OR about 0.13 for completed suicide).
- **Lähteenvuo 2018 (Finnish cohort, n=18,018):** lithium the most effective mood stabiliser in preventing rehospitalisation; comparable to LAIs [1].
- **Severus 2014:** lithium prevents manic more than depressive relapses.

**Conclusion.** "Lithium is the most effective medication" is **largely correct for long-term prophylaxis and suicide prevention**, the two outcomes that matter most over a lifetime. It is less true for acute mania speed and acute bipolar depression, where SGAs do better. "Atypicals are better mood stabilisers than lithium" is **not supported** as a general claim; they are better in particular phases and in some patients. Lithium is not "ideal" (narrow therapeutic index, organ toxicity), but it remains the gold standard against which others are measured and is badly under-used.

## In depth

### Background
- **1871:** Hammond used lithium bromide for mania. **1949:** John Cade (Australia) reported lithium in 10 manic patients.
- **1954:** Mogens Schou's placebo-controlled trial. **1967–70:** prophylaxis studies (Baastrup & Schou) and the "lithium controversy" (Blackwell & Shepherd). FDA approval in 1970.
- **1990s–2000s:** valproate and SGAs gained ground, helped by marketing. Lithium prescriptions fell in the US.
- **2010s–2020s:** a "lithium renaissance" driven by BALANCE, registry studies, suicide evidence and neuroprotection data [1].

### Epidemiology
- Bipolar I lifetime prevalence about 1%; bipolar spectrum up to about 6% [2].
- Suicide in about 15% [1]; suicide rates are 20–30 times the general population.
- Lithium use: 1 in 10 long-term patients have sub-therapeutic levels; adherence is poor (only half take almost all doses; a third take lithium for under 6 months) [1].
- Hypothyroidism on lithium: up to 20% of middle-aged women. Hyperparathyroidism about 4%. CKD risk rises with level above 0.8 and duration over 15 years [1].

### Classification and criteria
- **Mood stabiliser definitions:**
  - *Narrow:* efficacy in both acute mania and acute depression and in prophylaxis of both.
  - *Broad:* effective in one phase without worsening the other.
- **Lithium response (Alda scale):**
  - Criterion A: 0–10, degree of improvement.
  - Criterion B: confounders (duration, adherence, co-medication).
  - Total score ≥7 is generally taken as a good responder.
- About 30% are excellent lithium responders.

### Mechanism in detail

| Lithium target | Consequence |
| --- | --- |
| GSK-3β inhibition | Neuroprotection, circadian rhythm (CLOCK genes), Wnt signalling |
| IMPase inhibition → inositol depletion | Dampening of overactive phosphoinositide signalling |
| Increased BDNF, Bcl-2 | Neurogenesis, cell survival; grey matter increases on MRI |
| Reduced PKC and MARCKS | Antimanic (shared with valproate) |
| Glutamate (NMDA) modulation | Neuroprotection |
| Serotonin enhancement | Anti-suicidal, anti-aggressive |
| Renal: blocks ADH-dependent aquaporin-2 (via ENaC entry into collecting duct) | Nephrogenic diabetes insipidus |
| Thyroid: inhibits iodine uptake and hormone release | Hypothyroidism, goitre |

**Genetics:** lithium response is familial; GWAS (ConLiGen) implicates chromosome 21 lncRNAs. A higher schizophrenia polygenic score predicts poorer lithium response.

### Clinical features in detail — who responds to what

| Lithium responders | SGA-favoured |
| --- | --- |
| Classic euphoric mania; M-D-I course | Mixed features; dysphoric mania |
| Full interepisode recovery | Psychotic features, incomplete recovery |
| Family history of bipolar disorder and lithium response | Rapid cycling (quetiapine; lithium less effective) |
| Few episodes, no substance misuse | Comorbid substance use, anxiety |
| High suicide risk | Need for rapid control |

### Assessment in detail
- **Pre-lithium:** eGFR, U&Es, calcium, TFTs (± thyroid antibodies in women), weight, ECG if cardiac risk, pregnancy test [1].
- **Level:** 12 hours post-dose; after 7 days, and 7 days after each change [1].
- **Every 6 months:** level, eGFR, U&Es, TFTs, calcium, weight; more often in the elderly, CKD, or with interacting drugs [1].
- **SGA monitoring:** weight, waist, BP, glucose/HbA1c, lipids, prolactin, ECG.

### Differential — situations where neither is ideal

| Situation | Preferred |
| --- | --- |
| Predominant depressive polarity | Lamotrigine, quetiapine, lurasidone |
| Women of childbearing age | Avoid valproate; lithium with planning; lamotrigine or quetiapine |
| CKD (eGFR <30) | Avoid lithium; SGA or lamotrigine |
| Metabolic syndrome | Lithium, lamotrigine, aripiprazole, lurasidone |
| Poor adherence | LAI (aripiprazole, risperidone) [1] |
| Treatment-resistant | Clozapine (strong use in South-East Asia), ECT [1] |

### Management details
- **Lithium dosing:** start 400 mg at night (200 mg in the elderly); single daily dose of a prolonged-release preparation reduces polyuria [1].
- **Interactions:** NSAIDs, ACE inhibitors and ARBs (up to 4× level increase; 7× risk of toxicity admission in the elderly), thiazides, dehydration, low-salt diets. Theophylline and acetazolamide lower levels [1].
- **Toxicity:** >1.5 mmol/L (GI, coarse tremor, ataxia, confusion); >2.0 (seizures, coma); >3.0 often needs dialysis. Neurotoxicity can occur at normal levels [1].
- **Discontinuation:** taper over ≥1 month (preferably 3); avoid level drops >0.2 mmol/L. Abrupt stopping causes rebound mania even after 5 years of stability [1].
- **Pregnancy:** Ebstein's anomaly risk about 1 in 1,000–2,000 (much lower than once thought); level monitoring each trimester and postpartum; lithium is often continued in high-risk women.
- **Combination:** lithium + quetiapine or lithium + valproate (men) are common for breakthrough episodes.

### Critical appraisal
- **Industry bias:** many SGA maintenance trials used **enriched randomised-withdrawal designs** (responders to the SGA randomised to continue vs placebo). This favours the study drug and inflates its apparent prophylactic effect.
- **Lithium trials** are older and smaller, with abrupt-withdrawal designs and rebound-mania confounds.
- **Real-world data** (registries) favour lithium, but there is confounding by indication [1].
- **Acute mania speed:** antipsychotics are faster, which matters in emergencies; lithium needs levels and time.
- **Toxicity and monitoring burden:** lithium needs infrastructure. In low-resource settings, poor monitoring can make it dangerous.
- **Under-use of lithium:** marketing, fear of toxicity and lack of training. "The tragedy of lithium" (Malhi).
- **Dichotomy is false:** many patients need combinations. The question should be "lithium **and/or** an SGA, for whom?"

### Indian context
- Lithium is cheap and available, but monitoring (levels, renal and thyroid tests) is limited in rural areas and at DMHP level. Heat and dehydration in Indian summers raise the risk of toxicity, so counsel patients about fluids and salt.
- **IPS CPG (Shah, Grover, Rao 2017):** lithium is a first-line option for maintenance; SGAs are widely used.
- In practice, Indian psychiatrists frequently use olanzapine or valproate. Under-use of lithium mirrors global trends.
- Lithium in pregnancy: many Indian women of childbearing age are on valproate, which carries a high teratogenic risk; lithium or lamotrigine may be preferable.

### Recent advances
- **Established:** guidance on lithium level targets (0.6–0.8 maintenance) [1]; once-daily prolonged-release dosing.
- **Emerging:**
  - **Lithium response prediction** with genetics (ConLiGen polygenic score), iPSC neurons and the Alda scale.
  - Low-dose lithium for dementia prevention (mixed results) [1].
  - Lithium orotate (not recommended).
- **Investigational:** lithium nanoformulations; GSK-3 inhibitors without toxicity; SGA LAIs in bipolar disorder (aripiprazole 2-monthly) [1].
