## Exam answer

**Introduction.** ICD-11 replaced the ten specific personality disorder (PD) types of ICD-10 with a **single diagnosis of personality disorder rated by severity**, which can be further described by **five trait domain qualifiers** and an optional **borderline pattern** qualifier [1]. It is the most clearly dimensional part of the ICD-11 mental disorders chapter [1].

### Why change? [1]

- Gross **under-diagnosis** compared with the true prevalence among patients.
- Only **two** ICD-10 types (emotionally unstable–borderline and dissocial) were recorded with any frequency in public databases.
- Extremely high **co-occurrence** — most patients with severe disorder met criteria for several types.
- Categories had poor reliability and little treatment relevance.

### Step-by-step diagnosis [1, 2]

**Step 1 — General requirements for PD**
An enduring disturbance (typically **2 years or more**) in:
- **Self-functioning** — identity, self-worth, accuracy of self-view, self-direction; and/or
- **Interpersonal functioning** — understanding others' perspectives, developing and maintaining close relationships, managing conflict;

manifest in patterns of cognition, emotion and behaviour that are maladaptive, pervasive across situations, not developmentally appropriate, not explained by another disorder or substance, and associated with distress or impairment (author's summary of ICD-11 general requirements).

**Step 2 — Severity**, based on (a) degree and pervasiveness of self-dysfunction, (b) interpersonal dysfunction, (c) pervasiveness, severity and chronicity of emotional, cognitive and behavioural manifestations, and (d) distress and psychosocial impairment [1]:

| Level | Code | Description (summary) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Personality difficulty | QE50.7 | Pronounced traits affecting care, **not a mental disorder** |
| Mild PD | 6D10.0 | Some areas affected; relationships strained but maintained; usually no substantial harm |
| Moderate PD | 6D10.1 | Multiple areas; marked problems in most relationships; often some risk of harm |
| Severe PD | 6D10.2 | Severe disturbance in almost all areas; frequent harm to self or others |

**Step 3 — Trait domain qualifiers** (as many as are prominent) [1, 2]:

| Domain | Core tendency | Roughly corresponds to |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Negative affectivity** | Broad range of negative emotions; low self-esteem; mistrust | Anxious, dependent, borderline features |
| **Detachment** | Social and emotional distance | Schizoid, avoidant |
| **Dissociality** | Disregard for others' rights and feelings; self-centredness; lack of empathy | Dissocial, narcissistic |
| **Disinhibition** | Impulsive action without regard to consequences | Borderline, histrionic, impulsive |
| **Anankastia** | Rigid perfectionism; control of self and others | Obsessive-compulsive (anankastic) |

**Step 4 — Borderline pattern qualifier (optional)** — retained for continuity of care and to identify patients who may benefit from specific psychotherapies (DBT, MBT) [1].

**Example diagnosis:** *Moderate personality disorder, with prominent negative affectivity and disinhibition; borderline pattern.*

### Advantages [1]

- **Simplicity and clinical utility**, especially in non-specialist settings.
- Severity distinguishes patients needing **complex vs simpler treatment** and tracks **change over time**.
- Removes **artificial comorbidity** and "unspecified PD" diagnoses.
- Traits guide individual intervention strategies.
- Consistent with research showing personality pathology is dimensional.
- **Schizotypal disorder** stays with psychotic disorders, not PD [1].

### Criticisms

- Loss of familiar categories and decades of research on specific types (author's knowledge).
- Severity ratings require training; reliability in routine practice needs evidence.
- Unlike the DSM-5 Alternative Model, there is **no psychoticism domain** [3].
- Debate about whether the borderline pattern adds information beyond the traits [1].

**Conclusion.** ICD-11 diagnoses personality disorder by first confirming general requirements, then rating **severity (mild, moderate, severe)**, then describing prominent **trait domains** and, optionally, a **borderline pattern**. This severity-first, dimensional model is simpler and more clinically useful than ICD-10's ten types, though it sacrifices continuity with older research.

## In depth

### Background

- ICD-9 relied on Kurt Schneider's ten "psychopathic personalities"; ICD-10 aligned closely with DSM categories [3].
- Cloninger (2000) proposed a two-step approach — rate presence and severity of PD by adaptive functioning (self-directedness, cooperativeness), then describe traits — which both ICD-11 and the DSM-5 Alternative Model adopted in different forms [3].
- Early ICD-11 drafts used the "four As" (asthenic/anxious, adventurous/antisocial, asocial, anankastic) and proposed dropping borderline PD; the final version used five domains and added the borderline pattern [1, 3].
- The ICD-11 personality disorders working group was chaired by **Peter Tyrer** (author's knowledge); he is a co-author of [1].

### Comparison with other systems

| Feature | ICD-10 | DSM-5 Section II | DSM-5 Alternative Model (AMPD) | ICD-11 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Approach | 10 categories | 10 categories in 3 clusters | Level of functioning + 5 trait domains + 6 types | Severity + 5 trait domains |
| Severity | No | No | Level of Personality Functioning Scale (0–4) | Mild/moderate/severe; personality difficulty |
| Traits | — | — | Negative affectivity, detachment, antagonism, disinhibition, **psychoticism** | Negative affectivity, detachment, dissociality, disinhibition, **anankastia** |
| Borderline | Emotionally unstable, borderline type | BPD | One of six types | Optional pattern qualifier |
| Schizotypal | With schizophrenia | PD | PD | With psychotic disorders |

### Mechanism and psychobiological basis

- K&S argues that trait models should rest on neurobiology and genetics, not factor analysis alone; ICD-11's domains resemble Cloninger's temperament dimensions (harm avoidance → negative affectivity; novelty seeking → disinhibition/dissociality; low reward dependence → detachment; persistence → anankastia) [3].
- Factor analysis is statistically indeterminate, so different trait models overlap heavily [3].

### Assessment tools

- **Standardised Assessment of Severity of Personality Disorder (SASPD)** and **Personality Inventory for ICD-11 (PiCD)** were developed for the new system (author's knowledge).
- Clinical interview with collateral remains essential.

### Critical appraisal

- Severity is a strong predictor of outcome and service need, supporting the model [1].
- Clinicians accustomed to categories may under-use trait qualifiers, losing information.
- Personality difficulty, coded outside mental disorders, may help recognise subthreshold problems without stigma [1].
- Research continuity: decades of trials for BPD and antisocial PD were based on categories; the borderline pattern partly bridges this [1].

### Indian context

- Personality disorders are rarely diagnosed in Indian clinical practice; a severity-based approach may increase recognition among generalists.
- Cultural norms (family interdependence, deference, emotional expression) must be considered in judging self and interpersonal dysfunction [3].
- Training and adaptation of ICD-11 PD tools in Indian languages are needed.

### Recent advances

> From the author's knowledge — check before marking `reviewed`.

- Validation studies of ICD-11 PD severity and traits (PiCD, SASPD) across countries show reasonable reliability and structure.
- ICD-11 CDDR (2024) provides behavioural descriptions for each severity level.
