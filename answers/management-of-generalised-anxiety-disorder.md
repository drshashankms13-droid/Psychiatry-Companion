## Exam answer

**Definition.** Generalised anxiety disorder (GAD; ICD-11 6B00) is marked anxiety lasting **at least several months**, present on more days than not. It shows as either **general apprehension** ("free-floating" anxiety) or **excessive worry** about multiple everyday events (family, health, finances, work). It is accompanied by muscle tension, motor restlessness, sympathetic overactivity, nervousness, poor concentration, irritability and sleep disturbance, and causes significant distress or impairment. DSM-5-TR requires 6 months of excessive, hard-to-control worry with 3 of 6 symptoms (restlessness, fatigue, poor concentration, irritability, muscle tension, sleep disturbance).

### Mechanism
Genetic vulnerability (heritability ~30%) + temperament (neuroticism, behavioural inhibition, intolerance of uncertainty) + adversity → amygdala hyper-reactivity and weak ventromedial prefrontal regulation, HPA dysregulation, low GABAergic and serotonergic tone → threat bias → **worry as cognitive avoidance** (Borkovec), which suppresses autonomic arousal and is negatively reinforced → "worry about worry" (Wells' metacognitive model) → chronic course [1].

### Clinical course (why management is long-term)
GAD is chronic and fluctuating. Prospective remission is only about 38% at 5 years; relapse is common after partial remission. 91% have at least one comorbid disorder (depression, panic), which lowers remission. A personality disorder (avoidant, dependent) reduces the likelihood of remission by 30%. There are onset peaks in the 2nd and 5th decades [2].

### Assessment
- History of worry themes, duration, autonomic symptoms, avoidance, safety behaviours, function.
- **Comorbidity:** depression, panic, social anxiety, alcohol and benzodiazepine misuse, suicidality.
- **Physical causes:** thyrotoxicosis, anaemia, hypoglycaemia, arrhythmia, phaeochromocytoma, caffeine, stimulants, drug withdrawal [3].
- **Scales:** GAD-7 (≥10 = probable GAD), HAM-A, Penn State Worry Questionnaire.
- **Investigations** as indicated: TSH, CBC, glucose, ECG.

### Management — stepped care (NICE CG113; Maudsley)

| Step | Intervention |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | Identification, assessment, psychoeducation, active monitoring |
| 2 | Low-intensity: guided or unguided CBT-based self-help, psychoeducational groups, digital CBT |
| 3 | High-intensity **CBT** (12–15 sessions) or **applied relaxation**; **or** drug treatment |
| 4 | Complex or refractory: combination, specialist services, drug switching or augmentation |

**Pharmacotherapy** [3]
- **First line: SSRI** (sertraline preferred; escitalopram, paroxetine, fluoxetine). Start at **half the usual dose** because of initial jitteriness, then titrate. Benefit by 6 weeks, increasing over time.
- **Second line:** SNRI (venlafaxine 75–225 mg; duloxetine 60–120 mg).
- **Third line:** pregabalin 150–600 mg/day (fast onset; misuse risk; withdrawal seizures).
- **Others:** agomelatine, buspirone (delayed onset), quetiapine monotherapy (not adjunct), imipramine, hydroxyzine, mirtazapine; propranolol for somatic symptoms only.
- **Benzodiazepines:** crisis use only, maximum 2–4 weeks.
- **NICE:** do not offer antipsychotics in primary care.
- **Duration:** at least 1 year after response; taper slowly. Relapse within 3–6 months of stopping medication in one-third to one-half of patients [1,3].

**Psychological:** CBT (psychoeducation, worry exposure, cognitive restructuring of probability and catastrophising, intolerance-of-uncertainty work, problem-solving, relaxation, behavioural experiments to drop safety behaviours); applied relaxation; metacognitive therapy. Post-treatment response rates are 45–55% [1].

**Lifestyle:** exercise, sleep, reducing caffeine and alcohol, mindfulness.

### Key evidence
- **Hidalgo 2007; Baldwin 2011** network meta-analyses: SSRIs, SNRIs and pregabalin are all superior to placebo; fluoxetine most effective and sertraline best tolerated (early NMA). Later NMAs favour bupropion or agomelatine [3].
- **Slee 2019 (Lancet)** network meta-analysis (89 RCTs): duloxetine, pregabalin, venlafaxine and escitalopram effective and tolerable; quetiapine effective but poorly tolerated.
- **Cuijpers 2014:** CBT effect size ~0.8 vs waiting list; smaller vs active controls [1].
- **Relapse-prevention RCTs:** continuing SSRI/SNRI or pregabalin halves relapse risk over 6–12 months.

**Conclusion.** GAD is chronic and comorbid, so management needs a stepped, long-term plan. CBT and SSRIs (sertraline) are equally first-line and are often combined. SNRIs and pregabalin come next, benzodiazepines are for crises only, and treatment should last at least a year with relapse-prevention skills.

## In depth

### Background
Freud's "anxiety neurosis" (1895) was separated from neurasthenia. DSM-III (1980) created GAD as a residual category (1-month duration). DSM-III-R (1987) made worry central and extended the duration to 6 months. ICD-11 (2022) moved GAD into "Anxiety or fear-related disorders" and allowed "several months" duration. Borkovec's avoidance theory (1990s) and Wells' metacognitive model shaped CBT.

### Epidemiology
- Lifetime prevalence 4–6% (US NCS-R 5.7%); 12-month about 2–3%. Women are affected twice as often [1,2].
- Global 12-month prevalence of all anxiety disorders is about 14% (Europe) [1].
- NMHS India 2016: GAD 0.6% current; neurotic and stress-related disorders 3.5%.
- More than 90% have comorbidity [2]. GAD is a frequent presentation in primary care (somatic complaints, insomnia).

### Classification and criteria

| Feature | ICD-11 6B00 | DSM-5-TR |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Duration | Several months | ≥6 months |
| Core | General apprehension **or** excessive worry | Excessive, hard-to-control worry |
| Associated symptoms | Muscle tension, restlessness, sympathetic overactivity, nervousness, concentration, irritability, sleep | 3 of 6 |
| Exclusions | Another disorder, substance or medical cause | Same |

### Mechanism in detail
- **Neural:** amygdala and insula hyperactivity; reduced vmPFC/ACC regulation; altered amygdala–prefrontal connectivity; the bed nucleus of the stria terminalis (BNST) in sustained anxiety [1].
- **Neurochemistry:** reduced GABA-A benzodiazepine-site binding; serotonergic dysfunction; noradrenergic hyperactivity; HPA axis.
- **Genetics:** heritability ~30%, shared with depression (neuroticism) [1].
- **Psychological models:**
  - Borkovec's avoidance model (worry suppresses somatic arousal).
  - Dugas' intolerance-of-uncertainty model.
  - Wells' metacognitive model (Type 1 worry about events; Type 2 worry about worry).
  - Mennin's emotion dysregulation model.

### Clinical features in detail
Worries are multiple, shifting, and feel uncontrollable. Somatic complaints are common (headache, muscle aches, GI upset, palpitations). Patients may present to physicians repeatedly. Sleep-onset insomnia; irritability; fatigue; avoidance of uncertainty (reassurance-seeking, over-preparation, procrastination). Older adults: more somatic and health-focused worry; higher rates of persistent symptoms [2].

### Assessment in detail

| Tool | Use |
| --- | --- |
| GAD-7 | Screening and monitoring; cut-off 10 |
| HAM-A | Clinician-rated severity |
| PSWQ | Trait worry |
| Intolerance of Uncertainty Scale | CBT target |
| PHQ-9 | Comorbid depression |
| AUDIT | Alcohol |
| Investigations | TSH, CBC (anaemia), glucose, ECG (palpitations), urine drug screen if indicated |

### Differential diagnosis

| Condition | Distinguishing feature |
| --- | --- |
| Depression | Pervasive low mood, anhedonia; worry is ruminative and past-focused |
| Panic disorder | Discrete unexpected attacks, fear of attacks |
| Social anxiety disorder | Worry limited to social evaluation |
| OCD | Intrusive ego-dystonic obsessions with neutralising compulsions |
| Illness anxiety disorder | Worry focused on having a serious illness |
| PTSD | Trauma-linked re-experiencing |
| Adjustment disorder | Clear stressor, under 6 months |
| Hyperthyroidism, caffeine, stimulants, withdrawal | Physical signs, history, labs |
| Avoidant personality disorder | Lifelong, pervasive, ego-syntonic [2] |

### Management details
- **SSRI tips:** sertraline 25 mg → 50–200 mg; escitalopram 5 mg → 10–20 mg. Warn about early activation; review at 2–4 weeks; full trial 8–12 weeks [3].
- **Pregabalin:** schedule H1 in India; check for opioid co-use; taper over at least 1 week [3].
- **Benzodiazepines:** clonazepam or lorazepam short-term while the SSRI starts; avoid in substance misuse; the elderly are at risk of falls [1,3].
- **Treatment-resistant GAD:** confirm diagnosis and adherence; switch class (SSRI → SNRI → pregabalin); augment SSRI with pregabalin; quetiapine monotherapy (weigh metabolic effects); add CBT [3].
- **Special populations:**
  - Pregnancy: CBT first; sertraline if needed [1].
  - Elderly: lower doses; avoid benzodiazepines; watch for hyponatraemia with SSRIs.
  - Children: CBT with parents; SSRIs (sertraline, duloxetine is FDA-approved from age 7) [1].
- **Comorbid depression:** an SSRI/SNRI treats both. **Comorbid alcohol use:** treat both and avoid benzodiazepines.

### Critical appraisal
- **Comparisons of CBT and medication** are confounded by different control conditions. Direct comparisons show broadly similar efficacy; combination evidence is mixed [1,3].
- **Pregabalin misuse** has become a global problem; this shifts the risk–benefit balance [3].
- **Benzodiazepines:** possibly the most effective acute treatment, but dependence limits their role. Some argue the risks are overstated [3].
- **Access to CBT** is poor in LMICs; task-shifting and digital CBT are partial solutions [1].
- **Natural course data** are limited; long-term studies are needed [2].

### Indian context
- High somatic presentation ("gas", "weakness", headache); patients present to physicians and are often treated with benzodiazepines or alprazolam long-term, leading to dependence.
- Over-the-counter benzodiazepine access is a problem.
- Culturally adapted CBT and yoga/meditation-based interventions have Indian evidence. Tele-MANAS (14416) provides counselling.
- IPS CPG for anxiety disorders (Gautam et al. 2017): SSRIs first-line; benzodiazepines short-term; CBT.

### Recent advances
- **Established:** digital CBT (internet-delivered, guided) [1].
- **Emerging:** silexan (oral lavender oil, 80–160 mg) with positive RCTs [3]; agomelatine; vortioxetine (mixed) [3].
- **Investigational:**
  - Ketamine [3].
  - Cannabidiol [3].
  - Psilocybin (MM-120/LSD phase 2 positive in GAD, 2024: check status).
  - Neuromodulation: rTMS low-frequency right DLPFC has moderate evidence in the IPS CPG [4].
