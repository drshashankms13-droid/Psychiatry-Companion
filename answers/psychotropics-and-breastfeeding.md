## Exam answer

**Introduction.** Breastfeeding brings well-established physical and cognitive benefits to the child and benefits to the mother. Exclusive breastfeeding is recommended for 6 months. The **postpartum period is the peak risk time for maternal psychiatric illness**. Maudsley's starting position [1]:
- **most psychotropics should be continued** in breastfeeding women, because the benefits are clear and harm is unproven for most drugs;
- for **a few drugs (lithium, clozapine)** the mother should be advised **not to breastfeed** if that drug is her best option;
- **maternal treatment is the priority**.

The data come mostly from **small studies, case reports and series**, with short-term outcomes only, so **long-term safety cannot be guaranteed**.

### Measuring infant exposure [1]
- All psychotropics pass into milk to some degree.
- **Infant plasma level** is the best measure but rarely available.
- **Relative infant dose (RID)** = infant dose per kg per day (milk concentration × 150 mL/kg/day) ÷ maternal dose per kg per day.
  - **RID < 10% is usually regarded as safe.**
  - Infant plasma level below 10% of maternal level is also proposed as safe.
- Drug factors that increase milk transfer:
  - low protein binding;
  - lipid solubility;
  - low molecular weight;
  - **long half-life** (accumulation);
  - active metabolites (e.g., norfluoxetine).
- **Infant factors** increase risk:
  - **prematurity**;
  - neonatal age (immature hepatic and renal clearance);
  - renal, hepatic, cardiac or neurological disease.

### Two clinical scenarios [1]
1. **Already on the drug during pregnancy:** **continue it.** This also reduces neonatal withdrawal. **Do not switch at the end of pregnancy or just after birth** (high relapse risk). Clozapine and lithium are the exceptions.
2. **Starting a drug postpartum:**
   - consider previous response;
   - choose low RID and low infant levels;
   - prefer short half-life;
   - avoid high-RID drugs.

### Practical principles [1]
- **Discuss breastfeeding before conception or early in pregnancy**, as part of drug choice for pregnancy.
- Use the **lowest effective dose**, **monotherapy**, and continue the pregnancy regimen.
- Consider **feeding just before a dose** (the trough in milk) for short-half-life drugs, and giving a sedative dose at night [general knowledge].
- **Monitor the infant:**
  - sedation;
  - feeding;
  - weight gain;
  - irritability;
  - development.
- **Measure infant plasma levels** if adverse effects or toxicity are suspected.
- **Sedated mothers should not breastfeed in bed or co-sleep**, because of the risk of overlying and hypoxia. Monitor sedation's effect on mother–infant interaction.
- **Premature or sick infants:** use extra caution; consult the paediatric team.
- **Never withhold necessary treatment.** If a drug is absolutely contraindicated, treat the mother and advise formula feeding. Stopping breastfeeding is better than stopping treatment.
- **Sleep:** night feeds disrupt sleep, which is a trigger in bipolar disorder. Consider expressed milk or formula for night feeds by a partner.

**Conclusion.** Breastfeeding is **compatible with most psychotropics**:
- **sertraline and paroxetine** among antidepressants;
- **olanzapine and quetiapine** among antipsychotics;
- with infant monitoring.

**Lithium and clozapine** require formula feeding. **Lamotrigine** and **fluoxetine** need caution. Decisions should be planned before delivery, individualised (illness severity, previous response, infant health), and documented.

## In depth

### Background
- Breastfeeding benefits:
  - lower infant infection rates, SIDS and later obesity;
  - higher cognitive scores;
  - lower maternal breast and ovarian cancer risk.
- Women with SMI breastfeed less often, partly because of **unnecessary advice to stop**.
- The **LactMed** database (US NLM) and the Hale lactation risk categories are common references [general knowledge].

### Epidemiology
- Postpartum depression about 10–15%; postpartum psychosis 1–2 per 1,000.
- Bipolar disorder postpartum relapse about 20% (higher if untreated).
- Many women on psychotropics are advised (often wrongly) to stop breastfeeding [general knowledge].

### Classification of lactation risk (general knowledge)
- **RID thresholds:**
  - below 10%: generally acceptable;
  - 10–25%: caution;
  - above 25%: avoid.
- **Hale's L1–L5 categories:** L1 safest, L5 contraindicated. These were common but are being replaced by narrative assessment.

### Mechanism: pharmacokinetics of milk transfer
- **Passive diffusion** of free, un-ionised drug from plasma to milk.
- **Milk is slightly acidic (pH about 7.0–7.2)**, so weak bases (most psychotropics) are "trapped" in milk.
- **Factors:**
  - protein binding (highly bound drugs such as sertraline transfer little);
  - lipid solubility;
  - molecular weight (lithium is a small ion and transfers freely);
  - maternal plasma level.
- **Infant handling:**
  - neonatal CYP activity is low and glucuronidation is immature;
  - renal clearance is about 30% of adult values at birth.

  These explain accumulation of long half-life drugs (fluoxetine, diazepam) and lithium.

### Clinical features of infant toxicity
- **Sedation**, poor suck, poor weight gain (sedatives, antipsychotics, TCAs).
- **Irritability**, colic, insomnia (SSRIs, particularly fluoxetine and citalopram).
- **Respiratory depression or apnoea** (benzodiazepines, doxepin, lamotrigine).
- **Haematological effects:**
  - agranulocytosis (clozapine);
  - thrombocytopenia and anaemia (valproate);
  - thrombocytosis (lamotrigine).
- **Hepatic effects:** cholestatic hepatitis (carbamazepine).
- **Rash** (lamotrigine): consider SJS.
- **Lithium toxicity:** hypotonia, lethargy, cyanosis, feeding difficulty, raised urea and creatinine, raised TSH.

### Assessment before prescribing
- Maternal illness severity and relapse risk; previous response.
- Infant:
  - gestational age;
  - weight;
  - jaundice;
  - renal, hepatic and cardiac status;
  - feeding pattern.
- Mother's wishes about breastfeeding; support for night feeds.
- Co-sleeping arrangements; sedation level.

### Differential: is the infant's symptom drug-related?
- Normal newborn variation (colic, sleep variability).
- Neonatal withdrawal from in-utero exposure (first 1–2 weeks).
- Infection, hypoglycaemia, jaundice, feeding problems.
- **Use the temporal relationship**, dose changes, and infant plasma levels.

### Management: worked examples
1. **Postpartum depression, drug-naïve, breastfeeding:** **sertraline** 25–50 mg, titrating; infant check at 1–2 weeks.
2. **Bipolar disorder on lithium through pregnancy:**
   - continue lithium (postpartum relapse risk is high);
   - advise **formula feeding**;
   - if the mother insists on breastfeeding after informed discussion:
     - paediatric team involvement;
     - infant lithium levels, TFT and creatinine;
     - monitor for dehydration (risk of toxicity) [1, plus BAP general knowledge].
3. **Schizophrenia on clozapine:** continue clozapine and formula feed. If clozapine was started postpartum, consider a different drug if a breastfeeding-compatible alternative works.
4. **Bipolar disorder on lamotrigine:**
   - can breastfeed;
   - **reduce dose back toward pre-pregnancy levels** over the first 2–3 weeks postpartum (clearance falls), guided by levels;
   - monitor the infant for apnoea and rash.
5. **Insomnia or anxiety:** CBT-I and sleep hygiene first. If medication is needed, a short course of a short half-life agent (lorazepam, zolpidem).

### Critical appraisal
- **The evidence is weak:** case reports (publication bias toward adverse events); small series; no long-term outcomes.
- **RID** varies widely between studies and does not capture infant metabolism.
- **Confounding:** in-utero exposure vs milk exposure are hard to separate, because most infants were exposed in pregnancy.
- **Conservative advice may cause harm:**
  - infants lose breastfeeding benefits;
  - mothers lose a protective bonding activity;
  - abrupt stopping can trigger relapse.
- **The lithium position is contested.** Some centres (e.g., in the Netherlands) support breastfeeding on lithium with close infant monitoring [general knowledge; Imaz 2019].

### Indian context
- Breastfeeding rates in India are high and culturally valued (NFHS-5: about 64% exclusive breastfeeding under 6 months) [general knowledge; check]. Formula feeding has cost and hygiene implications.
- The threshold for advising against breastfeeding should therefore be higher in Indian practice. Weigh the practicality of infant monitoring (lithium levels, CBC for clozapine).
- **MHCA 2017 Section 21(2):** a mother should not be separated from her child under 3 during admission. Mother–baby units (NIMHANS) allow supervised breastfeeding [Likely].

### Recent advances
- **Brexanolone** (RID 1–2%) [1]. **Zuranolone** (oral, 14-day course; FDA 2023) for postpartum depression. Breastfeeding data for zuranolone are limited [general knowledge].
- **Dried blood-spot infant monitoring** for drug levels [Guessing].
- **Pharmacovigilance networks** for lactation (InfantRisk Center, LactMed).
