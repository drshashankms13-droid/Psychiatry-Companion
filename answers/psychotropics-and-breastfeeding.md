## Exam answer

### Principles [1]
- Breastfeeding has well-established benefits for the child's physical health and cognition; **at least 6 months** is encouraged.
- **Most psychotropics should be continued** in breastfeeding women: the benefits are real and harm is unproven for most drugs. A **few drugs require avoiding breastfeeding**.
- **Evidence is weak:** small studies and case reports; mostly **short-term acute effects** only. **Long-term safety cannot be guaranteed.**

**Measuring infant exposure:**
- All psychotropics pass into milk.
- **Infant plasma level** is the most direct measure but is rarely available.
- **Relative infant dose (RID)** = infant weight-adjusted daily dose (assuming **150 mL/kg/day** of milk) as a % of the maternal weight-adjusted dose:
  - **RID under 10%** is usually regarded as safe;
  - **infant plasma level under 10% of the maternal level** is also proposed as safe.
- The RID is a guide only; estimates vary widely.

**Two scenarios:**
1. **Drug taken through pregnancy:** **continue the same drug** while breastfeeding. This also reduces **neonatal withdrawal**. Do not switch at the end of pregnancy or just after birth (**high relapse risk**).
2. **New prescription postpartum:** consider **previous response**; avoid drugs with **high infant levels or RID**, and **long half-lives** (accumulation).

**Further points:**
- Discuss breastfeeding **before conception or early in pregnancy**, as part of the pregnancy drug plan.
- Weigh the benefits of breastfeeding against exposure. Consider **prematurity** and infant **renal, hepatic, cardiac or neurological impairment**: neonates clear drugs poorly.
- **Do not stop maternal treatment.** If a drug is absolutely contraindicated in breastfeeding, **treat the mother and advise formula feeding**.
- **Monitor the infant:** feeding, growth, development, specific adverse effects. Measure infant plasma levels if toxicity is suspected.
- **Sedating drugs:**
  - **do not breastfeed in bed** (falling asleep and overlying the baby, with a risk of hypoxia);
  - watch for sedation impairing mother–infant interaction.
- **Lowest effective dose; avoid polypharmacy; continue the pregnancy regimen.**

### Summary recommendations (Table 7.1) [1]

| Group | Recommendation |
| --- | --- |
| **Antidepressants** | Continue the pregnancy drug. If starting, **sertraline** may be considered |
| **Antipsychotics** | **Clozapine: continue, but advise against breastfeeding.** If starting, **olanzapine or quetiapine** |
| **Mood stabilisers** | **Lithium: continue, but advise against breastfeeding.** If starting, a mood-stabilising antipsychotic (**olanzapine or quetiapine**) |
| **Sedatives** | **Best avoided**; if needed, use a **short half-life** drug |

### Individual drugs (Tables 7.2–7.6) [1]

| Drug | Infant exposure | Notable infant effects |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Sertraline** | Undetectable or low; **RID 0.5–3%** | Rare serotonergic overstimulation; withdrawal after abrupt maternal stopping |
| **Paroxetine** | Low; RID 0.5–2.8% | Isolated vomiting and irritability (hyponatraemia) |
| **Mirtazapine** | Low; RID 0.5–4.4% | Breastfeeding **reduced poor neonatal adaptation** after in-utero exposure |
| **Citalopram / escitalopram** | RID about 3–8% | Sleep disturbance, colic, irritability; one case of necrotising enterocolitis (escitalopram, also exposed in utero) |
| **Fluoxetine** | **Highest of the SSRIs; can exceed 10%** (RID 1.6–14.6%) | Colic, crying, poor sleep and feeding; one case of serotonin syndrome (60 mg) |
| **Venlafaxine** | Up to 37% of maternal plasma; RID 6–9% | Lethargy, poor suckling (also exposed in utero) |
| **Duloxetine** | Under 1% | Dizziness, nausea, fatigue reported |
| **Quetiapine** | **Undetectable; RID about 0.1%** | Excess sleep (with co-medication) |
| **Olanzapine** | Low; RID 1–1.6% | Somnolence, irritability, poor suckling |
| **Aripiprazole** | RID 0.9–8.3% | **May reduce milk supply** |
| **Risperidone** | RID about 3–9% | One case of hypersomnia and poor feeding |
| **Clozapine** | 6.5% of maternal plasma | **Avoid**: sedation, **agranulocytosis**, seizures, cardiovascular instability, poor sucking |
| **Lithium** | **Up to 57% of maternal plasma; RID 12–30%** | **Avoid**: feeding problems, raised urea and creatinine, toxicity, raised TSH |
| **Lamotrigine** | **Up to 48% of maternal plasma; RID 9–18%** | Thrombocytosis; one severe cyanotic apnoea episode |
| **Valproate** | Under 2%; RID about 1.5% | Thrombocytopenia and anaemia (reversed on stopping breastfeeding) |
| **Carbamazepine** | Generally low; RID 1–7% | Cholestatic hepatitis, transient hepatic dysfunction |
| **Benzodiazepines** | Clonazepam RID 2.8%; diazepam up to 7%; **oxazepam 0.3–1%** | Sedation, lethargy, weight loss; persistent apnoea (clonazepam) |
| **Z-drugs** | Zolpidem RID under 0.2%; zopiclone 1.5% | None reported |
| **Methylphenidate** | Undetectable; RID under 1% | None reported |

**Neurodevelopment:** anticonvulsant exposure through breastfeeding (after in-utero exposure) was **not associated with adverse development or lower IQ** at 3 years [1].

**Conclusion.** Breastfeeding should usually continue, along with the drug the mother took in pregnancy. RID under 10% is generally regarded as safe. Sertraline (antidepressant) and olanzapine or quetiapine (antipsychotic/mood stabiliser) are preferred when starting a drug postpartum. **Lithium and clozapine** are the important exceptions: continue them, but advise formula feeding. Fluoxetine, venlafaxine and lamotrigine give relatively high infant exposure. Sedatives should be avoided or short-acting. Monitor the infant for sedation, feeding and growth, and never breastfeed in bed while on sedating medication.
