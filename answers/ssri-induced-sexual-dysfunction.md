## Exam answer

**Definition.** SSRI-induced (treatment-emergent) sexual dysfunction is new or worsened impairment of sexual **desire, arousal (erection, lubrication), orgasm or ejaculation** that is temporally linked to starting or increasing an SSRI and not better explained by depression, physical illness or other drugs. DSM-5-TR calls it **substance/medication-induced sexual dysfunction**. It is common, under-reported, a major cause of **non-adherence**, and usually dose-dependent and reversible. Rare persistent cases are called **post-SSRI sexual dysfunction (PSSD)** [1].

### Mechanism
- ↑5-HT at **5-HT2A/2C** receptors (spinal and brainstem inhibition of ejaculation and orgasm; reduced mesolimbic dopamine) → **delayed orgasm or ejaculation, anorgasmia, reduced libido**.
- ↓dopamine and noradrenaline → reduced desire and arousal.
- **Nitric oxide synthase inhibition** (paroxetine) → erectile dysfunction.
- **Hyperprolactinaemia** → reduced libido.
- Sedation; peripheral serotonergic effects (genital anaesthesia).
- Anticholinergic effects (paroxetine, TCAs) → arousal problems [1].

### Frequency and comparative risk

| Antidepressant | Desire | Arousal | Orgasm | Comment |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| SSRIs (paroxetine highest; fluvoxamine less anorgasmia) | ++ | ++ | ++ | Rates 25–80% when asked directly |
| Venlafaxine, duloxetine | ++ | ++ | ++ | Similar to SSRIs |
| TCAs (clomipramine worst) | ++ | ++ | ++ | — |
| Mirtazapine | + | – | – | Less than SSRIs |
| **Bupropion** | – | +/– | – | At or below placebo |
| **Agomelatine** | – | – | – | Similar to placebo |
| **Vortioxetine** (≤10 mg) | – | + | + | Lower than paroxetine or duloxetine |
| Moclobemide | – | – | – | Low |
| Trazodone | – | + | + | Priapism (rare) |

Source: Maudsley Table 3.17 [1].

**Depression itself:** 40–50% have reduced libido or arousal before treatment, but only 15–20% have orgasm problems. Orgasm and ejaculation delay therefore points to the drug [1]. In STAR*D, sexual dysfunction was a problem in 21% of remitters vs 61% of non-remitters, so treating depression can improve sexual function [1].

### Assessment
- Document **baseline sexual function before starting** (ASEX, CSFQ-14, PRSexDQ-SALSEX). Ask directly; spontaneous reporting greatly underestimates the problem [1].
- Domain affected; timing; partner factors; relationship.
- Other causes: diabetes, hypertension, atherosclerosis, hypogonadism, hyperprolactinaemia, alcohol, smoking, other drugs (antihypertensives, beta-blockers, antipsychotics) [1].
- Severity of depression and residual symptoms.

### Management (Maudsley Table 3.18; Indian CPG)
1. **Assess and educate.** Exclude other causes. Reassure that it is usually reversible [1,2].
2. **Wait** for tolerance: least effective. Only 5–10% remit spontaneously, and it may take up to 6 months [1].
3. **Dose reduction** if the patient is in remission (risk of relapse).
4. **Drug holidays** (e.g., skip sertraline or paroxetine on Friday–Sunday; not fluoxetine, whose half-life is too long). Risk of discontinuation symptoms and non-adherence.
5. **Timing:** take the dose after sexual activity (short half-life drugs).
6. **Switch** to an antidepressant with lower liability: **bupropion, agomelatine, vortioxetine, mirtazapine, moclobemide** [1].
7. **Add an antidote:**
   - **PDE-5 inhibitors** (sildenafil 50–100 mg, tadalafil 10–20 mg) for erectile dysfunction in men; best evidence (sildenafil). Limited evidence in women [1].
   - **Bupropion** 300 mg/day (women; low doses ineffective) [1].
   - Transdermal **testosterone** (women with SSRI-induced low libido; men with low or low-normal testosterone) [1].
   - Others with weak evidence: mirtazapine, buspirone, cyproheptadine (may reverse the antidepressant effect), amantadine, yohimbine, aripiprazole (in women) [1].
8. **Psychosexual therapy:** sensate focus, couple communication, exercise before sex [2].

### Key evidence
- **Montejo 2001** (Spanish prospective study, n=1,022): overall incidence 59%, higher with paroxetine (70.7%), citalopram and venlafaxine; mirtazapine 24%; nefazodone, moclobemide low.
- **Serretti & Chiesa 2009 (meta-analysis):** SSRIs and venlafaxine highest; bupropion, agomelatine, moclobemide and mirtazapine lowest.
- **Nurnberg 2003 (JAMA, n=90):** sildenafil improved SSRI-associated ED (response 55% vs 4.4%).
- **Cochrane (Taylor 2013):** sildenafil and tadalafil effective in men; bupropion at high dose promising in women.

**Conclusion.** SSRI-induced sexual dysfunction is common, under-detected and a leading cause of stopping treatment. Ask before and during treatment. Choose low-risk antidepressants for patients for whom sexual function matters most. Manage with switching (bupropion, agomelatine, vortioxetine, mirtazapine) or antidotes (PDE-5 inhibitors in men; high-dose bupropion or testosterone in some women), while keeping depression in remission.

## In depth

### Background
Early SSRI trials relied on spontaneous reports, which suggested rates under 10%. Studies using direct questioning (Montejo, 1997–2001) revealed 50–70%. Serotonergic delay of ejaculation was later used therapeutically (dapoxetine for premature ejaculation). PSSD was recognised in the 2010s; the EMA added it to SSRI labels in 2019, and Healy proposed diagnostic criteria (2022) [1].

### Epidemiology
- Incidence 25–80% depending on assessment method; paroxetine, venlafaxine and citalopram highest.
- About one-third of patients stop antidepressants because of sexual effects.
- PSSD risk estimated at about 1 in 216 treated patients (uncertain) [1].
- Baseline sexual dysfunction is common in depression (40–50% desire and arousal) [1].

### Classification and criteria
- **DSM-5-TR:** substance/medication-induced sexual dysfunction (onset during or soon after exposure; the drug is capable of producing the symptoms; not better explained otherwise).
- **ICD-11:** HA00–HA03 sexual dysfunctions with aetiological qualifier "associated with medications or substances" (in the separate Conditions related to sexual health chapter, which reflects a non-dualistic approach) [2].
- Subtypes: desire, arousal, orgasm, ejaculatory, pain; PSSD (persistent after discontinuation).

### Mechanism in detail
- **Serotonin:** 5-HT2C and 5-HT1A interplay. 5-HT1A agonism facilitates ejaculation; 5-HT2C stimulation delays it. The dorsal raphe inhibits the spinal ejaculation generator (lumbar spinothalamic cells).
- **Dopamine:** mesolimbic dopamine drives desire. SSRIs reduce dopamine release (5-HT2C on VTA); bupropion increases it.
- **NO:** paroxetine inhibits NOS, which impairs erection.
- **Prolactin:** some SSRIs raise prolactin.
- **Genetics:** 5-HT2A, 5-HTTLPR and GNB3 variants affect susceptibility [1].
- **PSSD hypotheses:** epigenetic changes, receptor down-regulation, peripheral small-fibre and genital nerve effects; uncertain [1].

### Clinical features in detail
- **Men:** delayed or absent ejaculation (most specific), erectile dysfunction, reduced libido, anhedonic orgasm, genital anaesthesia.
- **Women:** reduced libido, reduced lubrication and arousal, delayed or absent orgasm.
- **Other:** emotional blunting affecting intimacy; relationship strain.
- **PSSD:** persistent genital numbness, pleasureless orgasm, low libido, ED, sometimes premature ejaculation [1].

### Differential diagnosis

| Cause | Clue |
| --- | --- |
| Depression-related | Present before treatment; desire and arousal more than orgasm [1] |
| Residual depression | Improves with remission |
| Antipsychotics, beta-blockers, thiazides, finasteride | Drug history |
| Hypogonadism, hyperprolactinaemia | Labs |
| Diabetes, vascular disease | Risk factors, gradual ED |
| Relationship or performance anxiety | Situational, partner-specific |
| Alcohol and substance misuse | History |

### Management details
- **Bupropion switch:** 150–300 mg; avoid in seizure risk and eating disorders.
- **Agomelatine:** 25–50 mg; check LFTs.
- **Vortioxetine:** 10–20 mg (lower sexual dysfunction at ≤10 mg) [1].
- **Mirtazapine:** 15–45 mg; weight gain and sedation [1].
- **Sildenafil:** 50–100 mg 1 hour before sex; contraindicated with nitrates. **Tadalafil:** 10–20 mg on demand or 5 mg daily.
- **Bupropion augmentation:** 150 mg twice daily (women) [1].
- **Testosterone:** transdermal in women (off-label); in men with low testosterone [1].
- **Special groups:** adolescents (counsel); pregnancy (avoid PDE-5 data gaps); men planning conception (SSRIs may affect sperm DNA — limited data).
- **Shared decision-making:** some patients prefer to accept sexual side effects for antidepressant benefit; others prioritise sexual function.

### Critical appraisal
- Data rely on observational studies; early RCTs used spontaneous reporting [1].
- Antidote trials are small and heterogeneous; some are retracted (bupropion in men) [1].
- Separating drug effect from depression requires baseline assessment [1].
- PSSD: prevalence and mechanism uncertain; it may be under-recognised or over-attributed [1].

### Indian context
- Sexual concerns are rarely raised spontaneously because of stigma, so direct, sensitive questioning is essential, as the IPS CPG on sexual dysfunction emphasises [2].
- Men often stop SSRIs silently because of ejaculatory delay or ED; this drives relapse.
- Dhat syndrome and performance anxiety may coexist [2].
- PDE-5 inhibitors are widely available and cheap; bupropion, vortioxetine and agomelatine are available.

### Recent advances
- **Established:** vortioxetine, agomelatine and bupropion as low-risk options [1].
- **Emerging:** recognition of PSSD (EMA labelling) [1]; adjunct pimavanserin and brexpiprazole improving sexual function in secondary analyses [1].
- **Investigational:** flibanserin and bremelanotide (approved for hypoactive sexual desire disorder, not antidepressant-induced) [1]; gepirone (5-HT1A agonist antidepressant, FDA 2023) with low sexual dysfunction.
