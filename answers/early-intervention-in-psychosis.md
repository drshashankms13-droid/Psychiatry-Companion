## Exam answer

**Definition.** Early intervention in psychosis (EIP) is a service model and treatment philosophy aimed at two goals:
1. **Early detection:** identifying people at clinical high risk, and those with first-episode psychosis (FEP) as soon as possible, to shorten the **duration of untreated psychosis (DUP)**.
2. **Intensive, phase-specific treatment** during the **critical period** (first 3–5 years), when most deterioration, relapse and suicide occur.

It is delivered by specialised, youth-friendly multidisciplinary teams (McGorry, EPPIC Melbourne 1992; Birchwood's critical-period hypothesis 1998).

### Mechanism (rationale)
Long DUP → psychosocial damage (loss of education, job and relationships) + possible biological "toxicity" (sensitisation, neuroprogression) → poorer response and outcome. Earlier and intensive treatment in the critical period → better remission, fewer relapses, preserved social roles → better long-term trajectory [1,2].

### Components of EIP

| Stage | Interventions |
| --- | --- |
| Community detection | Public awareness, GP/school training, rapid-access pathways (TIPS Norway reduced DUP from 16 to 5 weeks) |
| Clinical high risk | Monitoring, CBT, treatment of comorbidity; antipsychotics not first-line [3] |
| First episode | Low-dose second-generation antipsychotic ("start low, go slow"), clozapine early if two failures; CBTp; family psychoeducation; IPS supported employment/education; case management (caseload ~1:15); physical health and substance use care |
| Critical period (2–5 years) | Relapse prevention, LAI option, suicide prevention, recovery and transition planning |

### Assessment
DUP (time from first psychotic symptom to adequate treatment); pathways to care; CAARMS/SIPS for high risk; PANSS; function; suicide risk; substance use; baseline metabolic profile; organic screen (thyroid, drugs, MRI or EEG if atypical); family burden.

### Evidence that early detection and treatment improve outcome

| Evidence | Finding |
| --- | --- |
| DUP and outcome (Marshall 2005; Perkins 2005; Penttilä 2014) | Longer DUP → poorer symptoms, function and response; correlation r ≈ 0.2–0.3 [2] |
| TIPS (Norway; Melle 2008; Hegelstad 2012) | Early detection reduced DUP; fewer negative symptoms and better recovery at 10 years (31% vs 15%) |
| OPUS (Denmark; Petersen 2005) | Better outcomes at 2 years; **benefits lost by 5 and 10 years** after transfer to standard care |
| Lambeth Early Onset (LEO; Craig 2004) | Fewer relapses and admissions at 18 months |
| Correll 2018 meta-analysis (10 RCTs, n=2,176) | EIP superior to usual care on discontinuation, hospitalisation, symptoms, function, school/work |
| RAISE-ETP (Kane 2016) | NAVIGATE better quality of life, especially with DUP under 74 weeks |

### Management principles
Rapid assessment (target ≤2 weeks) → engagement → lowest effective dose → psychosocial package → family work → supported employment/education → relapse prevention → extended EIP (5 years) or a planned step-down.

**Conclusion.** The first statement, "early detection and treatment has significantly improved outcome", is **partly true**. EIP clearly improves short- and medium-term outcomes and reduced DUP is associated with better outcome. But many gains fade when care stops at 2 years, and the causal role of DUP is still debated. In India, the concept is valid and urgently needed because DUP is long, but it must be adapted rather than copied: community- and family-based detection, task-sharing and integration with the DMHP are more feasible than Western standalone teams.

## In depth

### Background
- Sullivan (1927) argued that schizophrenia should be treated at its earliest stage [3].
- Falloon's Buckingham project (1980s) used GP-based detection.
- **1992:** EPPIC in Melbourne. **1994:** the PACE clinic for UHR [3].
- **1998:** Birchwood's critical-period hypothesis.
- 2000s: national rollouts (UK NHS Plan 2000; 2016 NHS waiting standard of treatment within 2 weeks, including ARMS) [3].
- **2005:** WHO and the International Early Psychosis Association (IEPA) Declaration.

### Epidemiology
- Mean DUP is about 1–2 years in high-income countries, with a median of about 3–6 months. It is often longer in LMICs: Indian studies report mean DUPs of 6–30 months, depending on setting.
- The first 5 years carry the highest suicide risk. In the Madras cohort, 7 of 16 deaths over 20 years were suicides, all in people under 35 [1].
- Over 80% relapse within 5 years of a first episode [4].
- **Indian pathway-to-care data:** most families first consult faith healers or general practitioners. In the Madras cohort, insidious onset (54.5%) was common [1].

### Classification and criteria
- **First-episode psychosis:** first presentation with full-threshold psychotic symptoms for over 1 week, regardless of eventual diagnosis (schizophrenia, schizophreniform, ATPD, affective psychosis).
- **DUP:** onset of first positive symptom to start of adequate antipsychotic treatment. **DUI** (duration of untreated illness) includes the prodrome.
- **Clinical staging (McGorry):** 0 (at risk, asymptomatic), 1a (non-specific distress), 1b (UHR), 2 (FEP), 3 (incomplete remission or relapse), 4 (persistent, unremitting).

### Mechanism in detail — why DUP might matter
1. **Neurotoxicity hypothesis:** glutamatergic excitotoxicity and oxidative stress during active psychosis cause progressive change. Gupta & Kulhara cite longer DUP predicting worse response and higher relapse as evidence of an active pathological process [2].
2. **Psychosocial hypothesis:** untreated psychosis disrupts development (education, work, relationships) and family relationships (expressed emotion), and leads to trauma (coercive admission).
3. **Confound hypothesis:** insidious onset and negative symptoms cause both long DUP and poor outcome. Long DUP may be a marker rather than a cause.

The truth is likely a mix of the three.

### Clinical features in detail — the critical period
- Most functional decline happens in the first 2–5 years, then plateaus.
- The risk of relapse after stopping medication is highest early. Maudsley notes that withdrawing antipsychotics after 1–2 years led to almost 80% relapse at 1 year [4].
- Suicide, violence and substance misuse cluster here.
- Developmental tasks (completing education, starting work) are at stake.

### Assessment in detail

| Domain | Tool |
| --- | --- |
| DUP | Nottingham Onset Schedule; Interview for Retrospective Assessment of Onset of Schizophrenia (IRAOS) |
| Pathways to care | WHO Encounter Form |
| High risk | CAARMS, SIPS [3] |
| Symptoms | PANSS, CDSS (depression) |
| Function | SOFAS, PSP |
| Cognition | Brief Assessment of Cognition in Schizophrenia (BACS) |
| Physical | Weight, waist, BP, glucose, lipids, ECG, prolactin |

### Differential (in first-episode work)
Substance-induced psychosis, ATPD (good prognosis; avoid premature schizophrenia label), affective psychosis (bipolar disorder in youth), organic psychosis (autoimmune encephalitis, epilepsy, HIV, thyroid, Wilson's), autism with stress-related psychotic features, and trauma-related dissociation.

### Management details
- **Antipsychotics:** first-episode patients respond to lower doses and are more sensitive to side effects. Prefer aripiprazole, risperidone (low dose) or amisulpride; avoid olanzapine as first choice in young people where possible because of weight gain. Start metformin early if weight rises [4].
- **Clozapine early:** after two adequate trials; delays in clozapine use are associated with poorer response.
- **LAIs in first episode:** large benefit over oral risperidone in first episode [4].
- **Duration:** at least 1–2 years; dose reduction only with care and monitoring [4].
- **Psychosocial:** CBTp, family psychoeducation, IPS, cognitive remediation, peer support.
- **Physical health:** smoking cessation, exercise; prevention of metabolic syndrome from the start.
- **Special groups:** adolescents (Maudsley Chapter 5), pregnancy, and comorbid cannabis use (the strongest predictor of relapse after discontinuation) [4].

### Evidence table

| Study | Design | n | Main result |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Marshall et al., 2005 | Systematic review of DUP | 26 studies | Longer DUP → worse outcome at 6 and 12 months |
| Perkins et al., 2005 [2] | Meta-analysis | 43 studies | Longer DUP → poorer response |
| Melle et al., 2008; Hegelstad 2012 (TIPS) | Quasi-experimental | 281 | Shorter DUP; better 10-year recovery |
| Petersen et al., 2005; Secher 2015 (OPUS) | RCT | 547 | 2-year benefit lost at 5/10 years |
| Craig et al., 2004 (LEO) | RCT | 144 | Fewer relapses and admissions |
| Kane et al., 2016 (RAISE-ETP) | Cluster RCT | 404 | Better QoL; larger effect with short DUP |
| Correll et al., 2018 | Meta-analysis | 10 RCTs, 2,176 | EIP superior on all main outcomes |
| Chang et al., 2015 (Hong Kong EASY) | RCT extended care | 160 | Extending EIP to 3 years kept gains |
| Thara, 2004 (Madras) [1] | Cohort | 90 | Relapse common but good functional outcome in an LMIC setting |

### Critical appraisal
**For the statement "early detection and treatment have significantly improved outcome":**
- RCT and meta-analytic evidence of better short-term outcomes (Correll 2018).
- Shorter DUP linked to better response.
- Cost-effective: savings from fewer admissions; UHR services were cost-effective in the UK [3].
- Hope and engagement for young people and families.

**Against, or limitations:**
- **Durability:** OPUS showed benefits fade after care ends. Outcomes depend on continued care, not on "early" alone.
- **DUP causality** is not established; lead-time bias and confounding by mode of onset are possible.
- **Population-level outcomes** (mortality, long-term recovery rates) have not clearly changed. Jääskeläinen 2013 found no improvement in recovery rates over decades.
- High-risk detection has low PPV, and antipsychotic prevention is not supported [3].
- Most evidence comes from high-income countries with resources that do not exist in most LMICs.

**Verdict:** the statement holds for short- and medium-term outcomes and for service experience. It is overstated for long-term outcomes unless EIP is sustained.

### Indian context (critical evaluation)
**Why EIP is needed in India**
- DUP is long: delayed help-seeking, first contact with faith healers, stigma, and the distance to services.
- Psychiatrists are scarce (about 0.75 per 100,000), and the treatment gap for psychosis is high (NMHS 2016: about 75%).
- Young population; suicide is a major cause of death in youth.

**What is different**
- Better natural outcomes in some Indian cohorts (WHO IPSS/DOSMeD; Madras: 76% of men employed, 73.7% married, only 5 of 61 continuously ill), so the added value of Western-style EIP may be smaller here, or differently located [1].
- Strong family involvement can serve as a resource for detection, adherence and rehabilitation.
- Informal employment allows role recovery without formal IPS [1].

**Barriers**
- Few specialised teams; the cost of multidisciplinary staff; urban concentration.
- Poor continuity; out-of-pocket costs.
- Mental health literacy is low; stigma around marriage leads to hiding illness [1].

**Feasible Indian model**
- Integrate early detection into the **DMHP**, primary care (Ayushman Bharat health and wellness centres), schools and colleges.
- Task-sharing with trained community health workers (ASHAs).
- Collaborate with faith healers (the Dawa-Dua programme at Erwadi, Tamil Nadu).
- Use Tele-MANAS and telepsychiatry for follow-up.
- Family psychoeducation; affordable generic antipsychotics and depots.
- Examples: SCARF first-episode programmes, NIMHANS early psychosis services, and COPSI community care (Chatterjee 2014).

**Legal framework:** MHCA 2017 (rights, advance directives, supported admission); the National Mental Health Policy 2014; the National Suicide Prevention Strategy 2022.

### Recent advances
- **Established:** EIP as a standard of care in many high-income countries; NICE 2-week access standard.
- **Emerging:**
  - Extended EIP (5 years).
  - Transdiagnostic youth services (headspace model; Orygen).
  - Digital tools (smartphone relapse prediction, online peer support such as Horyzons).
  - Clozapine earlier in the pathway.
- **Investigational:** biomarker-guided staging; non-D2 drugs (xanomeline–trospium) for first episode; LMIC adaptations, for example in the NIHR-funded global early psychosis networks.
