## Exam answer

**Concept [1, 2].**
- Chronic illness **raises the risk of depressive disorders in almost every chronic condition studied**, and depression in turn raises the risk of medical illness (**bidirectional**) [2].
- Severity, chronicity or organ involvement **cannot predict** how a given person will respond. The response depends on the person's **subjective experience** of illness [1].

### Stresses of chronic illness [1]
**Three tasks** (Perry and Viederman):
1. Acknowledge being ill.
2. Accept **regressive dependency** on carers.
3. Resume normal function.

**Specific stresses:**
- **Narcissistic injury:** the illness shatters fantasies of invulnerability, and the person feels "defective" or "weak".
- **Chronicity:** chronic illness forces a **permanent change of self-concept and body image**.
- **Separation and abandonment fears.**
- **Loss of privacy, bodily exposure and invasive procedures.**
- **Pain and insomnia:** treating them can by itself relieve "depression".
- **Disability:** loss of work role and self-esteem.
- **Confronting mortality.**

### Framework [1]
- The model is **stressor → subjective appraisal → response** (Lazarus and Folkman). It is shaped by:
  - **attachment style:** secure, preoccupied, dismissing or fearful;
  - **personality type:** Kahana and Bibring's seven types;
  - **coping style;**
  - **defence mechanisms** (Vaillant's hierarchy);
  - **optimism.**
- **Insecure attachment:** lower trust in doctors.
  - Preoccupied: **exaggerated illness behaviour** and high service use.
  - Dismissing and fearful: **poorer diabetes self-care, adherence and glucose control** (Ciechanowski).

| Personality type | Meaning of illness | Management tip |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Dependent | Threat of abandonment | Reassure within limits; scheduled visits |
| Obsessional | Loss of control | Detailed information, choices; avoid a "battle of wills" |
| Histrionic | Loss of love or attractiveness | Warm but formal; clear boundaries |
| Masochistic | Ego-syntonic punishment | Share their pessimism; frame treatment as another burden to endure |
| Paranoid | Proof the world is against them | Acknowledge feelings without disputing; keep distance |
| Narcissistic | Threat to perfection | Humble stance; do not challenge entitlement |
| Schizoid | Fear of intrusion | Respect privacy; gentle interest |

### Psychological and emotional complications [1]
- **Denial** (Weisman and Hackett): rejecting part or all of the meaning of an event to reduce distress.
  - It can be **adaptive** (e.g., "major deniers" after MI did better) or **maladaptive** (delay, non-adherence).
  - Judge it by three questions: does it **block necessary treatment**? Is it denial of **facts** (the worst) or of implications or emotion? Is it **temporary "buying time"**?
  - **Avoid direct confrontation** (it is counterproductive). Address the fear driving it, with a gentle, empathic, non-judgemental stance.
- **Anger:** commonest with paranoid, narcissistic, borderline or antisocial traits. Use empathy plus limit-setting, and redirect the anger.
- **Anxiety and fear:** elicit the **specific fear** (pain, death, disfigurement, dependence). **Blanket reassurance backfires.**
- **Sadness and grief:** loss of function, role or body part, often an **adjustment disorder**. Mourning takes time, so avoid reflexive antidepressants.
- **Guilt and shame:** illness experienced as punishment, or linked to behaviour (smoking, HIV). Stay non-judgemental; scolding worsens non-adherence.

### Behavioural complications [1]
- **Non-adherence:**
  - up to **50%** don't take prescribed medication;
  - **causes:** humiliation, helplessness, anger, denial, mistrust, **comorbid depression**, cost, side effects, complexity, lack of information, mismatched health beliefs.
- **Signing out against medical advice:**
  - **2.7%** of admissions;
  - **causes:** anger, overwhelming fear, withdrawal or craving, delirium or dementia, psychosis, outside responsibilities;
  - usually a **communication, not a true wish to leave**.
- **Adaptive responses to encourage:** support seeking, altruism, a re-ordering of priorities (**post-traumatic growth**), becoming an expert on the illness.

### Depression: the principal psychiatric complication [2]
**Continuum** (Table 7–2):

| Normal sadness | Subthreshold depression | Major depressive disorder |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Intimacy kept; belief things will improve; can enjoy memories; self-worth fluctuates; can look forward; **will to live intact** | Low mood like MDD, but short of criteria in number or duration; includes PDD if over 2 years | Isolation; sense of permanence; excessive guilt; self-loathing; **pervasive, non-reactive sadness**; hopelessness; loss of interest; suicidality |

**Prevalence:**
- MDD in **2–4%** of the community, **5–10%** in primary care and **6–14%** in medical inpatients.
- PDD and subthreshold depression are the **commonest** forms, in up to **26%** of medical outpatients.
- **Adjustment disorder** is one of the commonest diagnoses in medical patients.
- At least **10–20%** of subthreshold depressions progress to MDD.

**Table 7–1 (MDD prevalence):**

| Illness | MDD (%) |
| --- | --- |
| Cancer | 8–24 |
| Diabetes | 9–26 |
| Heart disease | 17–27 |
| COPD/asthma | **20–50** |
| HIV/AIDS | **18–50** |
| Stroke | 15–31 |
| Epilepsy | 20–29 |
| Multiple sclerosis | 26–35 |
| Alzheimer's disease | 13–22 |
| Parkinson's disease | 18–27 |

**Aetiology:** a **final common pathway** (Figure 7–1).
- **Biological:**
  - disease severity, pain, treatment intensity, closeness to death;
  - genetic vulnerability;
  - **inflammation** ("sickness behaviour"; proposed **inflammatory cytokine-associated depression**, with more neurovegetative symptoms);
  - **drugs with valid evidence:** **corticosteroids, interferon-α, IL-2, GnRH agonists, mefloquine, progestin implants**.
- **Psychosocial:** stigma and the personal meaning of the illness; disability; maladaptive coping; low self-esteem; poor spiritual well-being; low support; poor communication with carers. **Attachment security protects.**
- **Demographics:** age is inversely related to severity. The **female excess is not consistent** in the medically ill.

**Diagnostic difficulties:**
- **Overlap of somatic symptoms** (fatigue, anorexia, insomnia, slowing).
- **Mimics:** post-stroke or MS emotionalism, apathy, hypoactive delirium, parkinsonian masked face.
- **Death wishes vs adaptive death acceptance.**
- **Masked presentations:** somatic amplification, refusal of treatment.
- **Overlap of categories:** MDD vs depression due to another medical condition vs substance-induced.
- **DSM-5 clues to a medical cause:** a temporal link with the illness, and atypical age of onset, course or family history.
- **Approach:** no diagnostic scheme is clearly superior (Koenig 1997). Use the qualitative features in Table 7–2 and, if still unclear, a **trial of treatment**.

**Consequences:**
- **3-fold non-adherence**; about **2-fold** health-care use and workplace disability; twice the ER visits; longer stays; **50% higher** medical costs.
- **Higher mortality** in cardiovascular disease, cancer, transplantation and diabetes; faster HIV progression.
- The WHO World Health Survey found that **depression plus a chronic disease harms health more than any combination of chronic diseases**.
- **Reverse risks** (MDD raising the risk of medical illness): CAD 1.5–2×, stroke 1.8×, cancer 1.9×, diabetes 1.4×, **epilepsy 4–6×**, Alzheimer's 2.1×.

**Screening:**
- Fewer than half of cases are detected by GPs. The commonest failure is **not screening at all**.
- **PHQ-9** (cut-off 5–10), **HADS** (no somatic items; 8–16), **CES-D** (4 of 20 items somatic), **BDI-II** (many somatic items), **PHQ-2** (sensitivity about 89%, specificity about 76%).
- Screening must lead to clinical assessment, not to automatic treatment.

**Treatment:**
- **Stepped care** (NICE 2015):
  1. Assessment, support, psychoeducation, monitoring.
  2. Low-intensity psychosocial help (group exercise, peer support, guided or computerised CBT) for subthreshold or mild depression. **Antidepressants are not recommended** here.
  3. Antidepressants and/or high-intensity therapy (CBT, behavioural couples therapy), in collaborative care.
  4. Inpatient care or brain stimulation for complex cases.
- **SSRIs are first-line.** Choose by profile:
  - **venlafaxine:** hot flushes;
  - **SNRIs or TCAs:** pain;
  - **mirtazapine:** nausea, insomnia, anorexia;
  - **bupropion:** fatigue;
  - **psychostimulants:** rapid effect in palliative care (mixed evidence).
- **ECT** helps in Parkinson's disease (it may improve motor symptoms too), post-stroke depression, MS, endocrine and renal disease, and dementia. **rTMS** has more than 80 RCTs; it works in post-stroke and Parkinson's depression.
- **Collaborative care** has strong evidence in cancer, CAD, diabetes, MS and hepatitis C. The Katon 2010 RCT improved **HbA1c, LDL, systolic BP and depression together**.
- **Medical outcomes:** SADHART, SADHART-CHF, MIND-IT, ENRICHD and M-HART **did not reduce cardiac events**. Subgroups that **remitted** or took SSRIs did better. Focus on **quality of life**.

**Conclusion.** The psychiatric complications of chronic illness range from **normal grief and denial**, through **adjustment disorder and non-adherence**, to **major depression**. Major depression affects up to half of patients with some illnesses, worsens adherence, cost and survival, and is **both under- and over-diagnosed**. [Certain, per the source] Good management rests on:
- understanding the **patient's subjective meaning, personality and coping**;
- avoiding confrontation and scolding;
- **routine screening linked to assessment**;
- **stepped, collaborative care**, with SSRIs and CBT as the mainstays.

## In depth

### Coping (Lazarus and Folkman) [1]
- **Coping:** thoughts and behaviours to manage the problem (**problem-focused**) or the emotion (**emotion-focused**).
- **Folkman's 8 types:** confrontive, distancing, self-controlling, seeking support, accepting responsibility, escape–avoidance, planful problem-solving, positive reappraisal.
- People use **several strategies** at once, consistent with their past style. Ask how they coped with previous crises.
- **Problem-focused coping** is used when the situation seems controllable. Education can shift appraisal (e.g., diabetes as treatable).
- **Lipowski's 8 illness concepts:** challenge, enemy, punishment, weakness, relief, strategy, irreparable loss, value. "**Challenge**" and "**value**" go with adaptive coping.

### Defence mechanisms (Vaillant; Table 3–2) [1]
- **Mature:** suppression, altruism, humour, sublimation, anticipation. **Praise them; do not interpret them.**
- **Neurotic:** repression, control, displacement, reaction formation, intellectualisation, rationalisation, isolation of affect, undoing.
- **Immature:** splitting, idealisation, devaluation, projection, projective identification, acting out, passive aggression. They "make the observer suffer". Denial sits at the intermediate level.
- **Psychotic:** psychotic denial, delusional projection, schizoid fantasy.

### Groves' four challenging patient types [1]
- The four types are **dependent clingers, entitled demanders, manipulative help-rejecters and self-destructive deniers**.
- The **countertransference** they evoke is diagnostically useful.

### Principles to improve adherence [1]
1. Ask non-judgementally ("many patients find it difficult…") and design a collaborative plan.
2. Inform fully.
3. Rule out cognitive deficits.
4. Uncover psychological motives.
5. Treat psychiatric comorbidity.
6. Minimise side effects, cost and complexity.
7. Address cultural factors.
8. **Never shame, scold or frighten.**
9. Use positive reinforcement.
10. Involve family who support the treatment plan.

### Managing the threat to sign out against medical advice [1]
- Treat the threat as a **communication**.
- **Apologise on behalf of the system** if the anger is justified.
- Avoid scare tactics.
- Tailor your approach to the personality type, and treat any comorbidity.
- Involve supports and inform the patient.
- **Assess capacity** if indicated.
- Invite the patient back.
