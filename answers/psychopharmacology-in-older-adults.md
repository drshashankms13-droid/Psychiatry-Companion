## Exam answer

**Introduction.** Older adults (60 and over in India; 65 and over elsewhere) are the **largest consumers of psychotropics** and the most vulnerable to their harms. They have:
- **multimorbidity and polypharmacy**;
- **altered pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics**;
- **cognitive impairment, frailty and falls**.

Maudsley: "**It is reasonable to assume that all drugs are more likely to cause adverse effects in older patients**" [1].

### Principles (Maudsley Box 6.1) [1]
1. **Use drugs only when absolutely necessary.** Non-drug treatments first where effective (CBT, problem-solving therapy, sleep hygiene, BPSD behavioural strategies).
2. **Avoid drugs that:**
   - block **α1 receptors** (falls);
   - are **anticholinergic** (delirium, constipation, retention, cognitive decline, **dementia risk**);
   - are **very sedative**;
   - have a **long half-life** (diazepam);
   - are **potent enzyme inhibitors** (fluoxetine, fluvoxamine, paroxetine).
3. **"Start low, go slow" but do not under-treat.** Some drugs need full adult doses.
4. **Don't treat one drug's side effect with another drug** (the prescribing cascade). Find a better-tolerated alternative.
5. **Keep regimens simple**: once-daily where possible; adherence aids; carers involved.

### Pharmacokinetic changes [1]
| Parameter | Change | Consequence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Absorption** | ↓ gut motility, ↓ gastric acid | Slower onset; similar extent |
| **Distribution** | **↑ body fat, ↓ body water, ↓ albumin** | ↑ Vd and longer action of lipophilic drugs (**diazepam**); ↑ free fraction of highly bound drugs (**warfarin, phenytoin, valproate**) |
| **Metabolism** | ↓ liver size and blood flow; capacity largely preserved without disease | Interactions not larger, but **their consequences are amplified** |
| **Excretion** | **Renal function falls about 35% by 65 and 50% by 80**; creatinine misleading (low muscle mass) | Use **eGFR**; "assume **at most two-thirds** of normal renal function". **Lithium, sulpiride, amisulpride, paliperidone, gabapentin, pregabalin** accumulate |

### Pharmacodynamic changes [1]
- **↑ sensitivity** to CNS drugs (benzodiazepines, opioids, anticholinergics), partly from increased BBB permeability.
- Impaired homeostasis (BP, temperature, gut motility), causing **orthostatic hypotension, falls, constipation, hypothermia**.
- **Delayed therapeutic response** (antidepressants may take longer).
- **Higher serious adverse-event risk:**
  - **agranulocytosis and neutropenia with clozapine**;
  - **stroke with antipsychotics**;
  - **bleeding with SSRIs**;
  - **hyponatraemia**;
  - **QTc**;
  - **delirium**.

### Drug-class guidance
**1. Antidepressants [1]:**
- **SSRIs first-line (sertraline commonly)**; escitalopram/citalopram (QTc: citalopram maximum 20 mg in over-65s).
- **Avoid paroxetine** (anticholinergic, interactions) and **TCAs** (last resort: cardiac, anticholinergic, falls, fractures, overdose).
- **Risks to monitor:**
  - **hyponatraemia** (check Na at baseline and 2–4 weeks; SIADH);
  - **GI bleeding** (with NSAIDs, steroids, warfarin; consider a **PPI**);
  - **falls and osteopenia**.
- **Alternatives:**
  - **mirtazapine** (sleep, appetite; sedation, falls);
  - **vortioxetine, duloxetine, agomelatine** (effective in older people; agomelatine needs LFT monitoring; vortioxetine and duloxetine are safest for interactions);
  - **trazodone** (sleep; postural hypotension);
  - **methylphenidate** (rapid onset, apathy).
- **NMA in older adults:** quetiapine, duloxetine, agomelatine, imipramine and vortioxetine have the highest efficacy [1].
- **Treatment-resistant late-life depression:** **augment with aripiprazole** (or bupropion) rather than switching (OPTIMUM) [1]. Lithium (lower levels, 0.4–0.6 mmol/L). **ECT** (highly effective).
- **Maintenance:** over 60% relapse within 2 years if stopped after 2 years of treatment. Continue effective treatment; **deprescribe ineffective** drugs [1].
- **Depression in dementia:** antidepressants are **of doubtful benefit**. Prefer psychological and physical therapies [1].

**2. Antipsychotics:**
- **Late-onset psychosis or schizophrenia:**
  - **low doses** (e.g., risperidone 0.5–2 mg, quetiapine 25–200 mg, olanzapine 2.5–10 mg, aripiprazole 2–15 mg, amisulpride 50–200 mg);
  - watch for **EPS and tardive dyskinesia** (much higher incidence in older adults), postural hypotension, sedation, anticholinergic effects, QTc, metabolic effects.
- **Dementia (BPSD):**
  - **antipsychotics increase mortality (about 1.6–1.7×) and stroke (about 3×)** [general knowledge];
  - **use only for severe, distressing or dangerous psychosis or agitation after non-drug measures**;
  - **risperidone** has the best evidence for short-term aggression;
  - review and **attempt withdrawal within 6–12 weeks** [1, general knowledge].
- **Lewy body or Parkinson's disease dementia:** **avoid typical antipsychotics** (severe sensitivity reactions). Use **low-dose quetiapine or clozapine**; **pimavanserin** (FDA) for Parkinson's disease psychosis; cholinesterase inhibitors [1].
- **Clozapine:** higher agranulocytosis risk; orthostasis; constipation (ileus) [1].

**3. Mood stabilisers:**
- **Lithium:**
  - **lower doses and target levels (0.4–0.8 mmol/L)**;
  - **renal decline**;
  - interactions with **ACE inhibitors, ARBs, thiazides, NSAIDs** cause toxicity;
  - neurotoxicity at "therapeutic" levels;
  - monitor eGFR, TSH and calcium closely.
- **Valproate:** ↑ free fraction (low albumin), so monitor free levels if possible; tremor, falls, hyperammonaemia, thrombocytopenia.
- **Lamotrigine:** well tolerated; slow titration.
- **Carbamazepine:** **hyponatraemia**, interactions (enzyme induction), ataxia. Avoid where possible.

**4. Anxiolytics and hypnotics:**
- **Avoid benzodiazepines and Z-drugs** where possible:
  - falls and **hip fractures**;
  - cognitive impairment, delirium;
  - respiratory depression;
  - dependence;
  - possible dementia association.
- If unavoidable: **short-acting (lorazepam, oxazepam), short course, lowest dose**.
- **Insomnia:**
  - **CBT-I first**;
  - **melatonin prolonged-release** (licensed in over-55s);
  - low-dose doxepin (3–6 mg);
  - **orexin antagonists** (suvorexant, lemborexant, daridorexant);
  - avoid sedating antihistamines (anticholinergic).

  See `management-of-insomnia-in-the-elderly`.
- **Anxiety:** SSRIs; **pregabalin** (renal dosing); buspirone.

**5. Cognitive enhancers [1]:**
| Drug | Mechanism | Start → target | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Donepezil | Selective, reversible AChE-I | 5 → 10 mg | Half-life about 70 hours; CYP3A4/2D6; bradycardia, syncope, GI effects, nightmares |
| Rivastigmine | AChE + BuChE inhibitor | 1.5 mg twice daily → 3–6 mg twice daily; **patch 4.6 → 9.5 (13.3) mg/24 h** | Few interactions; GI effects (less with patch); **PDD licence** |
| Galantamine | AChE-I + nicotinic modulation | 8 → 16–24 mg XL | CYP3A4/2D6 |
| Memantine | NMDA antagonist (+ 5-HT3 antagonist) | 5 → 20 mg (weekly steps) | Renal dosing; dizziness, constipation, hypertension |

- Check **pulse/ECG** (bradycardia, heart block) before cholinesterase inhibitors.
- **Anti-amyloid antibodies** (lecanemab, donanemab) are specialist, ARIA-monitored treatments. See `monoclonal-antibodies-in-neuropsychiatry`.

### Systematic safeguards
- **Medication review (structured):**
  - **STOPP/START** criteria;
  - **AGS Beers Criteria** (potentially inappropriate medications);
  - **anticholinergic burden scales** (ACB) [general knowledge].
- **Baseline and monitoring:**
  - **eGFR**, LFT, **Na**, ECG (QTc), weight, BP lying and standing;
  - **cognition** (MoCA), falls risk;
  - drug levels (lithium, valproate).
- **Interactions:** narrow therapeutic index drugs (**digoxin, warfarin, theophylline, phenytoin, lithium**). Enzyme inhibitors and inducers (SSRIs, erythromycin, carbamazepine) [1].
- **Adherence:** dosette boxes, blister packs, caregiver supervision, simplify regimens.
- **Capacity and consent** (MHCA 2017 Section 4). **Covert administration** only after **MDT and family discussion** with documentation and best-interest justification [1].
- **Deprescribing:**
  - review every drug's indication;
  - taper benzodiazepines, antipsychotics in BPSD, and ineffective antidepressants;
  - **avoid abrupt withdrawal** [1].

**Conclusion.** Geriatric psychopharmacology rests on:
- **knowing age-related pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic change** (reduced renal clearance, increased CNS sensitivity, frailty);
- **choosing drugs with low anticholinergic, α1, sedative and interaction liability** (sertraline, escitalopram, mirtazapine, vortioxetine; low-dose quetiapine or aripiprazole; lorazepam only short-term);
- **dosing low and slow but adequately**;
- **monitoring sodium, ECG, renal function, falls and cognition**;
- using **ECT** when indicated;
- **deprescribing regularly**.

## In depth

### Background
- **Polypharmacy** (5 or more drugs) affects a large proportion of older adults. **Adverse drug reactions** cause a substantial share of hospital admissions in older people [general knowledge].
- **Prescribing cascades** are common. Example: an antipsychotic causes EPS, then an anticholinergic is added, which causes confusion.

### Epidemiology
- **Dementia:** about 5% of people over 65 and 20% of those over 80 [1]. Alzheimer's disease is about 60% of cases [1].
- **India:** over 10% of the population is aged 60 or over [general knowledge].

### Classification: high-risk drug groups (Beers / STOPP)
- First-generation antihistamines; TCAs; paroxetine.
- Long-acting benzodiazepines.
- Antipsychotics in dementia (except for psychosis).
- Anticholinergic antiparkinsonian drugs (benzhexol, trihexyphenidyl).
- **Use of trihexyphenidyl for EPS prophylaxis in older adults** is a common Indian prescribing error [general knowledge].

### Mechanism
- **Cholinergic reserve** declines with age and dementia, so anticholinergic drugs cause delirium.
- **Dopaminergic decline** increases EPS sensitivity.
- **Baroreceptor blunting** increases orthostasis.

### Clinical features: adverse-effect presentations
- Delirium; falls and fractures; constipation and urinary retention.
- Hyponatraemia (confusion, lethargy).
- Parkinsonism mistaken for disease progression.
- Weight loss (cholinesterase inhibitors).

### Assessment checklist
- "Brown bag" medication review, including OTC drugs and Ayurvedic or herbal remedies (common in India).
- Renal function (eGFR); LFT; Na; ECG; BP lying and standing.
- Cognitive baseline; functional status (ADL, IADL).
- Caregiver availability.

### Differential: drug effect vs disease
| Symptom | Possible drug cause |
| --- | --- |
| Confusion | Anticholinergics, benzodiazepines, lithium toxicity, hyponatraemia (SSRIs), opioids |
| Falls | α1 blockers, sedatives, antihypertensives, hyponatraemia |
| Tremor | Lithium, valproate, SSRIs |
| Parkinsonism | Antipsychotics, metoclopramide, valproate |
| Bradycardia and syncope | Cholinesterase inhibitors, β-blockers |

| Drug | Starting dose | Usual range in older adults | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Sertraline | 25 mg | 50–100 mg | Hyponatraemia, bleeding |

### Critical appraisal
- RCTs **exclude** frail, multimorbid older people, so evidence is extrapolated.
- Observational harm data are confounded by indication [1].
- Deprescribing trials are few.

### Indian context
- **Gaps:**
  - high OTC and **benzodiazepine** use;
  - trihexyphenidyl co-prescription;
  - few geriatric psychiatrists;
  - **NPHCE** geriatric clinics.
- **Cost:** generics make SSRIs, mirtazapine and quetiapine affordable; rivastigmine patch and memantine are costlier.
- **Families as caregivers** are critical for supervision; consent and capacity under **MHCA 2017**.

### Recent advances
- **Orexin antagonists** for insomnia in older adults and dementia (suvorexant, lemborexant) [general knowledge].
- **Brexpiprazole** licensed for agitation in Alzheimer's dementia (FDA 2023) [general knowledge].
- **Anti-amyloid antibodies** [1].
- **Pharmacogenomic-guided prescribing** in older adults.
- **Deprescribing guidelines** (Maudsley Deprescribing Guidelines, 2024) [1].
