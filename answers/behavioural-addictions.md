## Exam answer

**Introduction.** Classical definitions tie addiction to **drug ingestion**. One example: "a state of periodic or chronic intoxication produced by repeated consumption of a drug" (WHO) [1]. The behavioural-addiction concept holds that **non-chemical behaviours** can show the same core features. Examples include:
- gambling;
- video gaming;
- internet and smartphone use;
- sex;
- exercise;
- shopping;
- overeating [1].

Marlatt's definition shows the bridge: "a repetitive habit pattern that increases the risk of disease and/or personal and social problems, often experienced as **loss of control**, with immediate gratification and delayed costs, and **high relapse rates** after attempts to change" [1].

### Core components (Brown 1993; Griffiths "components model") [1]
1. **Salience:** the activity dominates thinking (preoccupation), feelings (craving) and behaviour.
2. **Mood modification / euphoria:** a "buzz", "high" or escape.
3. **Tolerance:** increasing amounts needed (e.g., bigger bets).
4. **Withdrawal:** irritability, restlessness and low mood on stopping.
5. **Conflict:** interpersonal and intrapsychic.
6. **Relapse and reinstatement:** a rapid return to peak patterns after abstinence.

Carnes listed ten "signs of addiction", including:
- out-of-control behaviour;
- continuing despite consequences;
- using the behaviour to cope;
- giving up activities [1].

### Commonalities with substance addictions [1, 2]
- **Psychological:** the behaviour acts as a **reinforcer** (variable-ratio schedule in gambling); tolerance; affective contrast; cue conditioning; stress-induced use (Donegan 1983).
- **Sociological:** onset in youth (18–25); peer and parental influences; early onset predicts severity; links with crime; higher rates in single, divorced or unemployed people.
- **Cultural:** normative ambiguity; periods of prohibition; **12-step fellowships** (Gamblers Anonymous).
- **Neurobiological:**
  - mesolimbic dopamine and reward prediction;
  - **blunted ventral striatal and vmPFC response** to reward;
  - impaired inhibition and executive function;
  - serotonergic dysfunction (low CSF 5-HIAA);
  - opioid system involvement (response to naltrexone);
  - **dopamine agonist-induced gambling** in Parkinson's disease [2].
- **Genetic:** twin heritability of gambling disorder is about **50%**. **64% of gambling–alcohol co-occurrence** reflects shared genes [2].
- **Comorbidity** (NESARC) in pathological gamblers [2]:
  - 6× alcohol use disorder;
  - 4.4× other SUD;
  - 3× depression;
  - 8× mania;
  - 8× personality disorder.
- **Treatment response:** CBT, MI, and opioid antagonists work across both.

**ICD-11 gaming disorder** (WHO 2019):
- **impaired control** over gaming (onset, frequency, intensity, duration, termination, context);
- **increasing priority** given to gaming over other interests and daily activities;
- **continuation or escalation despite negative consequences**;
- **significant impairment**;
- usually present for **12 months** (shorter if severe).

### Behavioural addiction in children and adolescents
- **Types:**
  - **gaming** (most common clinically);
  - excessive smartphone and **social media** use;
  - online video streaming;
  - pornography (adolescents);
  - **gambling-like mechanics** (loot boxes, skins betting, fantasy sports, online rummy);
  - lottery and adolescent gambling. Griffiths: 65% of UK adolescents had played fruit machines and 0.5–6% were probable pathological gamblers [1].
- **Prevalence:** gaming disorder about **2–3%** of adolescents worldwide (meta-analysis; general knowledge). Indian school surveys report problematic internet use in about 10–20% [Guessing; studies vary widely].
- **Why children are vulnerable:**
  - **immature prefrontal control** with a hyper-responsive reward system (dual-systems model);
  - **design features** such as variable rewards, streaks, social comparison and infinite scroll;
  - easy access; parental modelling;
  - pandemic-era increases (online schooling).
- **Risk factors:**
  - male sex (gaming); female sex (social media);
  - **ADHD** (strongest association), **ASD**, depression, social anxiety;
  - bullying and loneliness;
  - family conflict; poor parental monitoring;
  - low self-esteem.
- **Consequences:**
  - school refusal and academic decline;
  - sleep loss and circadian disruption;
  - obesity; musculoskeletal and visual problems;
  - aggression when devices are removed;
  - family conflict;
  - depression and anxiety;
  - cyberbullying; exposure to sexual content; financial loss from in-app purchases.
- **Assessment:**
  - detailed **time-use history**; content; functional impairment;
  - parent and teacher reports; comorbidity screening (ADHD, depression, ASD);
  - **scales:** Internet Addiction Test (Young), **IGDS9-SF**, Game Addiction Scale for Adolescents, Smartphone Addiction Scale – Short Version.
- **Distinguish** high engagement (passionate, adaptive) from addiction (impairment).
- **Management:**
  1. **Psychoeducation** for parents and child.
  2. **Family-based interventions:**
     - parent management training;
     - consistent, negotiated **screen-time rules**;
     - device-free bedrooms and meals;
     - parental controls;
     - parents modelling healthy use.
  3. **CBT for internet and gaming addiction (CBT-IA):** self-monitoring, cognitive restructuring, alternative activities, relapse prevention. **MI** for an ambivalent adolescent.
  4. **Treat comorbidity:** ADHD (methylphenidate and bupropion reduce gaming in ADHD; Korean studies, general knowledge), depression, social anxiety.
  5. **School liaison:** gradual return; academic support.
  6. **Pharmacotherapy:** no approved drug; bupropion and naltrexone are experimental.
  7. **Prevention:**
     - WHO and IAP screen-time guidance (none below 2 years; at most 1 hour for 2–5-year-olds);
     - digital literacy;
     - regulation of loot boxes and gaming design.

### Critique of the concept
- **For:** shared phenomenology, neurobiology, genetics, comorbidity and treatment response. Gambling disorder is the clearest model [1, 2].
- **Against:**
  - **risk of pathologising everyday behaviour** ("everything is an addiction");
  - weak validity for many proposed addictions (work, dance, tanning);
  - tolerance and withdrawal are poorly defined for behaviours;
  - excessive use may be a **symptom** of depression, ADHD or anxiety (coping) rather than a primary disorder;
  - industry and moral-panic influences;
  - diagnostic thresholds can inflate prevalence (the DSM-5 threshold change worried some for this reason) [2].
- **Balanced view:** recognise disorders with strong evidence (gambling, gaming), keep others as **research categories**, and focus on **functional impairment** rather than time spent.

**Conclusion.** Addiction is better understood as a **brain–behaviour syndrome of impaired control over a rewarding activity** than as a property of a drug. Gambling disorder (DSM-5, ICD-11) and gaming disorder (ICD-11) are now formally recognised. In **children and adolescents**, gaming and smartphone and social media overuse are the main presentations. They are closely tied to ADHD, depression and family dynamics, and need **family-based, CBT-informed, comorbidity-focused** management.

## In depth

### Background
- **Miller (1980)** and **Orford (1985, *Excessive Appetites*)** broadened addiction beyond drugs. Orford's view: appetite plus restraint failure.
- **Peele's** experiential view of addiction (*Love and Addiction*, 1975).
- **Glasser's "positive addictions"** (jogging, meditation) may be "mixed blessing addictions" (Brown) [1].
- **Gambling** history:
  - DSM-III (1980): impulse disorder;
  - DSM-III-R and DSM-IV: criteria modelled on substance dependence;
  - DSM-5 (2013): reclassified as an addictive disorder [2].

### Epidemiology
- **Gambling disorder:** past-year problem gambling ranges from **0.2% (Norway) to 5.3% (Hong Kong)**. US pathological gambling 0.4–1.1%, with a further 1–2% problem gamblers [2]. **Fewer than 10% seek treatment.** About a third recover naturally [2].
- **Gaming disorder:** about 2–3% worldwide (Stevens 2021 meta-analysis; general knowledge); higher in Asian adolescents.
- **India:**
  - online rummy and fantasy sports are legal grey areas;
  - Tamil Nadu (2022) and Karnataka passed online gambling bans;
  - the **Promotion and Regulation of Online Gaming Act, 2025** bans real-money online games nationally [general knowledge; check].

### Classification
- **Brand's I-PACE model** (Interaction of Person–Affect–Cognition–Execution, 2016/2019):
  - predisposing person variables;
  - affective and cognitive responses (cue reactivity, craving, coping style);
  - executive function;
  - over time, habit formation, a reduced role for "liking" and an increased role for "wanting" [general knowledge].
- **Blaszczynski & Nower pathways model (gambling)** [2]:
  1. Behaviourally conditioned.
  2. Emotionally vulnerable (depression, anxiety, trauma).
  3. Antisocial impulsivist (impulsivity, ADHD, antisocial features).

### Mechanism
- **Reinforcement:** variable-ratio schedules (slots, loot boxes) are the most resistant to extinction. **Near misses** activate reward circuitry.
- **Cognitive distortions in gambling** (Toneatto) [2]:
  - illusion of control;
  - gambler's fallacy;
  - chasing;
  - superstition;
  - selective memory;
  - attributional bias.
- **Neurobiology** [2]:
  - blunted ventral striatum and vmPFC response to reward (a reward-deficiency hypothesis);
  - reduced vlPFC activation (inhibition);
  - D2/D3 antagonists paradoxically **increase** gambling motivation;
  - **dopamine agonists** (pramipexole, ropinirole) cause impulse-control disorders in Parkinson's disease;
  - serotonergic: low platelet MAO-B; low CSF 5-HIAA;
  - noradrenergic and opioid systems also involved.
- **Genetics** [2]:
  - twins: 49% heritability (female twins);
  - shared liability with alcohol use disorder;
  - DRD2 A1, DRD1 and DRD3 variants.
- **Environment:**
  - accessibility; game and gambling design;
  - childhood maltreatment;
  - parental gambling [2].

### Clinical features (gambling disorder; DSM-5 criteria)
1. Needs to gamble with increasing amounts (tolerance).
2. Restless or irritable when cutting down (withdrawal).
3. Repeated unsuccessful attempts to stop.
4. Preoccupation.
5. Gambles when distressed.
6. **Chasing losses.**
7. Lies to conceal gambling.
8. Has jeopardised or lost relationships, job or education.
9. Relies on others for money ("bailout").

Severity: mild 4–5, moderate 6–7, severe 8–9 criteria. Episodic or persistent course. Exclusion: manic episode [2].

### Assessment
- **Screening tools** [2]:
  - **SOGS** (South Oaks Gambling Screen; sensitivity 0.99, specificity 0.75);
  - **PGSI** (9 items; ≥8 = problem gambling);
  - **NODS-CLiP** (3 items: control, lying, preoccupation);
  - **BBGS** (3 items: withdrawal, lying, borrowing; sensitivity 0.96, specificity 0.99).
- **Severity and outcome measures:** PG-YBOCS; Gambling Symptom Assessment Scale; timeline follow-back; GAMTOMS.
- Also assess **suicide risk** (high in gambling disorder), debts, legal problems and family impact.

### Differential diagnosis
- Manic or hypomanic episode (gambling as part of mania).
- Dopamine agonist-induced impulse-control disorder.
- Antisocial personality disorder (gambling as one of many antisocial acts).
- Professional or social gambling.
- Frontotemporal dementia (disinhibition).
- For gaming: depression with withdrawal into gaming; ASD special interest; social anxiety avoidance.

### Management (gambling disorder) [2]
- **Brief interventions:** MI by telephone plus a mailed CBT workbook had good outcomes at 12–24 months. Even **5 minutes of advice** reduced gambling in people not seeking treatment.
- **CBT** (meta-analysis of 22 RCTs: effect size 2.01 after treatment and 1.59 at 17 months):
  - cognitive restructuring of distortions;
  - **exposure and response prevention / imaginal desensitisation** (aversion therapy less effective);
  - problem-solving; relapse prevention.
- **MI** alone or with CBT; group CBT.
- **Gamblers Anonymous.**
- **Self-exclusion programmes**; financial controls (third-party money management).
- **Pharmacotherapy:**
  - **opioid antagonists (naltrexone 50–150 mg, nalmefene)** have the best evidence, especially with a strong urge or family history of alcoholism;
  - **lithium** for bipolar-spectrum comorbidity;
  - SSRIs mixed;
  - N-acetylcysteine and topiramate under study [2, general knowledge].
- **Family therapy and support for relatives.**

### Critical appraisal
- **Measurement problems:** dozens of internet addiction scales with variable cut-offs, so prevalence estimates vary 10-fold.
- **Direction of causality:** depression may lead to gaming (coping) more than gaming causes depression. Longitudinal data are mixed.
- **WHO's gaming disorder decision (2019)** was criticised by some scholars (Aarseth 2017 open letter) as premature and stigmatising, and supported by clinicians who see severe cases [general knowledge].
- **Treatment evidence for gaming disorder** is thin: small trials, mostly from Asia.

### Indian context
- **NIMHANS SHUT Clinic** (Service for Healthy Use of Technology, started 2014): India's first specialised tech-addiction clinic, mainly for adolescents and young adults [Likely].
- **Online gaming regulation:**
  - IT Rules amendment 2023 (self-regulatory bodies for online games);
  - **28% GST on online gaming** (2023);
  - state gambling bans;
  - the 2025 Act banning real-money gaming [general knowledge; check].
- **Gambling law:** the Public Gambling Act 1867 is state-dependent; Goa, Sikkim and Daman allow casinos.
- **Cultural norms:** gambling at Diwali (card games); lotteries legal in about 13 states.

### Recent advances
- **ICD-11 implementation** (2022).
- **Digital interventions** (app-based CBT).
- **Neuromodulation** (rTMS to DLPFC) trials for gambling and gaming craving.
- **Loot-box regulation** (Belgium bans; the UK chose voluntary codes).
- **Policy:** debates over the "attention economy" and design-based duty of care (UK Online Safety Act 2023).
