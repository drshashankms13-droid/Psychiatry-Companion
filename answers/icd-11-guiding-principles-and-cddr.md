## Exam answer

**Definition.** The guiding principles of ICD-11 are the values WHO used to decide what to change in the classification of mental disorders: **clinical utility, global applicability and scientific validity**, pursued through a participative, multilingual, multidisciplinary process [1]. The **Clinical Descriptions and Diagnostic Requirements (CDDR)** are the clinical guidelines that express these principles for each disorder in Chapter 06, published by WHO in 2024 as the successor to the ICD-10 "blue book" (CDDG, 1992) [1, 5].

### The guiding principles

| Principle | Meaning | How it shaped ICD-11 [1, 2] |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Public health purpose** | Help member states reduce the burden of mental disorders | Focus on high-burden disorders; primary care version |
| **Clinical utility** (the core principle) | The classification must be easy to use, aid communication, guide treatment and predict course | Essential features instead of rigid criteria; uniform templates; fewer residual categories |
| **Global applicability** | Usable across cultures, languages and resource settings | Working groups from all WHO regions; culture-related guidance; translations |
| **Scientific validity** | Changes supported by evidence | Working groups reviewed literature; new categories only with evidence |
| **Participation** | Involve countries, clinicians, service users and families | Global Clinical Practice Network of about 15,000 clinicians in 155 countries |
| **Harmonisation with DSM-5** | Avoid arbitrary differences | Shared structure; differences only where justified |
| **Lifespan and developmental approach** | Disorders described from childhood to old age | Childhood chapter dissolved; developmental ordering |
| **Dimensional within categorical** | Capture severity and symptom profile | Personality severity and traits; psychosis symptom qualifiers |

WHO defined clinical utility as a classification that **communicates** usefully between users, has **implementation characteristics** that make it easy and accurate to use in practice, and helps to **select interventions** and manage patients [1, 2].

### Structure of a CDDR entry [1, 2]

Essential (required) features → additional features → **boundary with normality** → **boundary with other disorders** → coded qualifiers → course → developmental presentations → **culture-related features** → sex- and gender-related features.

### CDDR compared with ICD-10 CDDG and DSM-5

| Dimension | ICD-10 CDDG (1992) | DSM-5 / DSM-5-TR | ICD-11 CDDR |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Diagnostic style | Descriptions + guidelines with some counts | **Operational criteria**: symptom counts, durations, exclusions | **Essential features**, flexible; counts only when empirically supported (e.g. depressive episode) [1] |
| Philosophy | Clinical description | Reliability for clinicians and research | Clinical utility for global users, including non-specialists |
| Developer | WHO | American Psychiatric Association | WHO with global working groups |
| Cost and access | Free for statistics | Commercial | Free online |
| Use | Official statistics worldwide | Mainly US clinical and research use | Official statistics worldwide |
| Content consistency | Variable across groupings | Consistent | **Uniform template** for every disorder |
| Cultural guidance | Limited | Cultural formulation interview; cultural concepts of distress | Culture-related features **for every disorder** |
| Dimensional elements | Minimal | Severity specifiers; alternative personality model in Section III | Personality **severity + traits** in the main classification; psychosis qualifiers |
| Functional impairment | Not always required | Usually required | Generally required, with exceptions (e.g. schizophrenia) |
| Differences of note | – | 6-month schizophrenia duration; DMDD; no complex PTSD | 1-month schizophrenia; complex PTSD; ODD with chronic irritability instead of DMDD; gaming disorder [1] |

### Evaluation: strengths

- **Fits clinical reasoning:** clinicians compare patients with a prototype rather than counting items; ICD-11 adopts this approach [1].
- **Reliability preserved:** clinic-based field studies in 14 countries showed reliability at least as high as criteria-based systems [1].
- **Higher perceived utility:** clinicians across 13 countries rated the guidelines easy to use and good for communication [1].
- **Global relevance:** culture-related sections, LMIC participation, free availability and a primary care version.
- **Boundary with normality** sections reduce over-diagnosis (e.g. acute stress reaction is classed as normal) [1].
- **Behavioural indicator tables** for intellectual disability help where standardised tests are unavailable [1].

### Evaluation: limitations

- **Less precise for research:** without explicit thresholds, research groups may need their own operationalisation; DSM-5 remains dominant in research.
- **Risk of variability:** flexible guidance depends on training; untrained clinicians may apply it inconsistently.
- **Evidence for superiority is mainly WHO-led** and vignette-based; independent naturalistic data are limited.
- **Validity unchanged:** like DSM-5, the CDDR remain descriptive and syndrome-based, without biological markers.
- **Two parallel systems** with deliberate differences complicate communication, research synthesis and training.
- **Implementation costs:** coding software, legal documents and teaching need updating.

### Conclusion

The CDDR translate ICD-11's guiding principles into a clinically usable, globally applicable and freely available guide. Compared with ICD-10 they are more consistent, more evidence-based and better suited to culture and lifespan; compared with DSM-5 they trade criterion precision for clinical flexibility. They are best suited to clinical and public-health use worldwide, while DSM-5 criteria keep an edge for research operationalisation.

## In depth

### Background

- The ICD-10 mental disorders chapter existed in several versions: a statistical version with short glossary definitions, the CDDG for clinicians (the "blue book"), the DCR-10 for research and a primary care version [1].
- A WHO survey found that clinicians regularly read the CDDG when making an initial diagnosis, contradicting the belief that classifications are used only for billing codes [1].
- Early in the ICD-11 process WHO surveyed psychiatrists and psychologists worldwide; most preferred flexible guidelines to strict criteria and wanted fewer categories [1].
- The DSM-5 and ICD-11 working groups overlapped, and both organisations tried to harmonise the overall structure of the chapters [1].

### Field-study programme [1]

| Component | Design | Purpose |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Formative studies | Card-sorting by clinicians of how disorders relate | Inform the structure of groupings |
| GCPN internet studies | Standardised vignettes manipulated to test key differences; six languages | Accuracy and consistency versus ICD-10; scaling of qualifiers |
| Clinic-based studies | Real patients, 14 countries, local languages | Clinical utility, usability, reliability for high-burden disorders |

A key strength was timing: studies were done early enough for results to change the guidelines [1].

### Classification principles visible in the structure

- **Developmental order:** neurodevelopmental disorders first, neurocognitive disorders last [1].
- **Grouping by shared aetiology** (disorders specifically associated with stress) or **shared phenomenology and validators** (obsessive-compulsive and related disorders, dissociative disorders) [1].
- **Abolition of the organic/non-organic dichotomy:** new chapters for sleep–wake disorders and conditions related to sexual health; "non-organic" removed from elimination disorders [1].
- **Mental and non-mental cross-listing ("secondary parenting"):** Tourette syndrome, sleep–wake disorders and sexual dysfunctions are listed in their home chapters but cross-listed in Chapter 06 [1, 2].
- **Factors influencing health status:** personality difficulty, hazardous substance use, acute stress reaction, burn-out and malingering are placed outside mental disorders [1, 2].

### Comparison of key design choices with DSM-5

| Issue | DSM-5 choice | ICD-11 choice | Rationale for ICD-11 [1] |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Personality disorder | 10 categories (Section II); hybrid model in Section III | Severity + 5 trait domains + borderline pattern | Underdiagnosis and artificial comorbidity with categories |
| Persistent irritability in children | New disorder (DMDD) | Qualifier within ODD | Evidence that this is a form of ODD |
| Trauma | PTSD with 4 clusters | PTSD with 3 core features + complex PTSD | Lower ICD-10 threshold; complex PTSD has different prognosis and treatment |
| Grief | Persistent complex bereavement for further study (later PGD in DSM-5-TR, 12 months) | Prolonged grief disorder (6 months) | Distinct, debilitating condition |
| Gender | Gender dysphoria in mental disorders | Gender incongruence in sexual health chapter | Destigmatisation while preserving access to care |
| Gaming | Internet gaming disorder for further study | Gaming disorder as a diagnosis | Public health concern, treatment seeking |
| Acute psychosis | Brief psychotic disorder | ATPD restricted to polymorphic form | Polymorphic form common and not predictive of schizophrenia |

### Critical appraisal

- **Utility versus validity tension:** a highly usable category may still be invalid; ICD-11 explicitly placed utility first, which some scientists criticise as pragmatic rather than scientific.
- **Who decides utility?** Field studies measured clinicians' opinions; service-user perspectives were included in consultations but less in formal testing.
- **Cultural guidance quality** varies by disorder and relies on a literature dominated by high-income countries.
- **Primary care simplification** may increase recognition but risks over-diagnosis of common mental disorders.
- **Comparability:** meta-analyses mixing ICD-11 and DSM-5 diagnoses must account for different thresholds (e.g. schizophrenia duration, PTSD threshold).

### Indian context

- Indian clinicians participated in GCPN studies and working groups; Indian authors (e.g. Sharan, Ray) are co-authors of the main ICD-11 papers [1].
- The CDDR's flexibility and culture-related features fit Indian presentations: somatic idioms of distress, possession and trance states, dhat-like concerns, and family-based help seeking.
- Free availability matters in public-sector training; the primary care version supports the District Mental Health Programme and task-sharing.
- Barriers: official records, insurance, courts and disability certification still use ICD-10 codes; teaching curricula need revision.

### Recent advances

- WHO published the CDDR in 2024; training materials and coding tools are being rolled out.
- Continuing debate on integrating dimensional frameworks (HiTOP) and neuroscience-based approaches (RDoC) into future classifications.
