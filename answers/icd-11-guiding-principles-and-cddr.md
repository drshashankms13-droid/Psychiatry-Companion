## Exam answer

**Introduction.** The WHO Department of Mental Health and Substance Abuse developed the ICD-11 chapter on mental, behavioural and neurodevelopmental disorders over more than a decade, in what its authors describe as the most global, multilingual, multidisciplinary and participative revision ever undertaken for a psychiatric classification [1]. The clinician's version — first called the **Clinical Descriptions and Diagnostic Guidelines (CDDG)**, published as the **Clinical Descriptions and Diagnostic Requirements (CDDR)** — is the ICD-11 equivalent of the ICD-10 "blue book" [1].

### Guiding principles

1. **Clinical utility — the core principle.** A classification that is not useful at the point of care will not be used faithfully, and then health statistics built on it are invalid [1]. Utility means it should communicate well, help choose treatment, predict course, be easy and quick to use, and fit how clinicians think [1, 2].
2. **Global applicability.** Usable in all WHO regions, in many languages, and by non-specialists in low- and middle-income countries, where over **80%** of the world's population lives [1].
3. **Scientific validity.** Working groups reviewed the evidence for each grouping [1].
4. **Public health aim.** Help member states reduce the burden of mental disorders by improving identification and treatment, including by frontline health workers [2].
5. **Harmonisation with DSM-5** — avoid arbitrary differences; allow justified conceptual ones [1].
6. **Participation** of countries, professionals, service users and families [2].

### Innovations in the CDDR

- **Standard template ("content form") for every disorder** [1, 2]:
  - brief definition
  - **essential (required) features**
  - **boundary with normality** (threshold)
  - **boundary with other disorders** (differential diagnosis)
  - coded qualifiers and subtypes
  - course features
  - associated clinical presentations
  - **culture-related, developmental and gender-related features**
- **Essential features instead of rigid criteria:** arbitrary symptom counts and durations are avoided unless supported across countries and cultures (depressive episode is an exception) — matching the way clinicians actually diagnose [1].
- **Lifespan approach:** childhood-onset grouping removed; developmental presentations described for each disorder [1].
- **Culture-related guidance** for each grouping (e.g. *khyâl cap*, *ataque de nervios* for panic) [1].
- **Dimensional elements** within a categorical structure — personality disorder severity and traits, psychosis symptom qualifiers [1].
- **Field-tested:** internet-based Global Clinical Practice Network (about **15,000 clinicians, 155 countries**) and clinic-based studies in **13–14 countries** [1].

### Critical evaluation

| Strengths | Limitations |
| --- | --- |
| High clinical utility in field studies across 13 countries [1] | Utility ratings came from clinicians already engaged in the process |
| Reliability at least as high as strict criteria-based approaches [1] | Reliability data mainly from developmental studies; less real-world evidence |
| Flexible, prototype-like guidance fits clinical judgement [1] | Flexibility may reduce precision and comparability for research |
| Systematic boundary-with-normality sections reduce over-diagnosis | Thresholds still a matter of judgement |
| Culture- and development-specific guidance [1] | Most cultural material derived from Western literature |
| Removes artificial comorbidity and residual "unspecified" categories [1] | New disorders (gaming, compulsive sexual behaviour) contested |
| Dimensional qualifiers support recovery-oriented care [1] | Still descriptive — no aetiological validity |
| Free, digital, multilingual | Training and implementation burden in low-resource systems |

**Conclusion.** ICD-11's guiding principles — clinical utility, global applicability and scientific validity — produced a CDDR that is more consistent, culturally informed and usable than the ICD-10 blue book, with comparable reliability. Its limits are those of all descriptive classifications: flexible guidance trades research precision for clinical usefulness, and validity remains unproven. It should be judged as a practical clinical tool, which is what it was designed to be.

## In depth

### Background

- ICD-10 had separate versions for statistics (short glossary), clinicians (CDDG, 1992) and research (Diagnostic Criteria for Research, 1993) [1].
- A shortcoming of the ICD-10 CDDG was inconsistent information across disorder groupings; ICD-11 working groups therefore submitted **content forms** with uniform headings [1].
- A WPA–WHO global survey of psychiatrists and a parallel survey of psychologists shaped the desired characteristics: fewer categories, flexible guidelines, and clinical usefulness [1].
- The term **"diagnostic requirements"** replaced "diagnostic guidelines" in the final 2024 publication, to signal that essential features must be present (author's knowledge).

### What "clinical utility" means

Clinical utility is defined in ICD-11 work as the extent to which a classification [1, 2]:
- helps clinicians communicate with each other, patients, families and administrators;
- is easy to use and fits clinicians' conceptual models ("goodness of fit");
- helps select interventions and predict outcome;
- defines groups that are homogeneous enough for treatment and research.

Formative field studies asked clinicians to sort disorders; their "natural taxonomies" informed the chapter structure [1].

### Field studies

| Type | Method | Findings |
| --- | --- | --- |
| GCPN internet studies (about a dozen) | Standardised vignettes manipulated to test ICD-11 vs ICD-10 differences, in English, Chinese, French, Japanese, Russian and Spanish | Identified confusing guidance, which was then revised before finalisation [1] |
| Clinic-based studies | 13–14 countries, real patients, local languages | High clinical utility; reliability for high-burden disorders at least as high as criteria-based systems [1] |
| Qualifier-scaling studies | Psychotic symptom ratings | Informed dimensional rating format [1] |

### Examples of the template in action

- **Panic disorder — culture section** lists *khyâl* attacks (Cambodian), *ataque de nervios* (Latin American), *trúng gió* (Vietnamese) as cultural concepts of distress, and explains when culturally expected attacks should not count toward panic disorder [1].
- **Depressive episode — boundary with normality** guides differentiation from culturally normative grief [1].
- **OCRD — poor to absent insight qualifier** prevents mislabelling as delusional disorder [1].

### Critical appraisal in detail

- **Flexibility vs operationalisation:** DSM-5 and ICD-10 DCR use explicit criteria; ICD-11 deliberately avoids them. Field studies suggest reliability does not suffer, but research users may still need structured instruments.
- **Who rated utility?** GCPN participants volunteered; they may be more favourable than average clinicians.
- **Global applicability:** representation from low- and middle-income countries was substantial in working groups, but the published evidence base remains largely from high-income countries.
- **Harmonisation with DSM-5** was partial — differences remain (schizophrenia duration, complex PTSD, prolonged grief thresholds, ODD with chronic irritability vs DMDD) [1].
- **Dimensional elements** are modest compared with research frameworks such as HiTOP; ICD-11 remains a categorical system with taxonomic constraints [1].

### Indian context

- Indian contributors from AIIMS New Delhi and other centres participated in working groups [1].
- Culture-related guidance (possession trance, dhat-like presentations, somatic idioms of distress) is useful in Indian practice.
- Adoption in India requires changes in hospital information systems, insurance coding, medical education and disability certification, which still refer to ICD-10.
