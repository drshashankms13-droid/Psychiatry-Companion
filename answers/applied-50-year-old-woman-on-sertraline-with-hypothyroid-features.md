## Exam answer

**Summary.**
- A **50-year-old menopausal widow** has had **major depressive disorder treated with sertraline 100 mg/day for over 2 years**.
- Over **6 months** she has developed **weakness, cold intolerance, constipation and weight gain**.
- Examination: **dry, coarse skin, bradycardia, hypothermia and swelling of the hands and feet**.
- Background: **husband died 2 years ago**. He had **alcohol dependence** and **suspected her for many years** (jealousy). She has **no children**.

### Most likely diagnosis
**Overt hypothyroidism (myxoedema)** in a woman with depression, possibly **contributing to persistent or treatment-resistant depression**.

**Supporting features:**
- **Classic physical features** [1, 2]:
  - **cold intolerance** (often a prominent early complaint);
  - **constipation**, **weight gain**, **weakness**;
  - **dry, rough skin**;
  - **non-pitting oedematous** appearance of the face and limbs (here, swollen hands and feet);
  - **slow pulse**.
- **Hypothermia with bradycardia:** a warning sign. Hypothyroid **coma** "should be suspected in any patient with severe impairment of consciousness and hypothermia" and carries high mortality [1].
- **Demography:** hypothyroidism is about **8 times commoner in women** and rises with age from middle age [1]. Subclinical hypothyroidism is especially common in **postmenopausal women** (about 20%) [2].

### Why it matters psychiatrically
- Hypothyroidism is "**notorious for leading to mistakes in diagnosis**". It develops insidiously, with diffuse complaints [1].
- Slowing, poor concentration, fatigue and reduced responsiveness "**may be confused with depression**". Depression is also commoner in hypothyroidism [1].
- **Both overt and subclinical hypothyroidism predict poorer antidepressant response** in major depression [2].
- **K&S illustrative case:** a 54-year-old woman's depression, lethargy and weight gain were blamed on the **menopause**. After 6 months of HRT failed, TSH was **14 mIU/L**. Levothyroxine improved energy and interest within 8 weeks [2]. This mirrors the present case.

### Differential diagnosis
| Possibility | Points for / against |
|---|---|
| **Hypothyroidism with secondary or worsened depression** | Physical signs; the most likely |
| **Residual or relapsed MDD on sertraline** | Possible, but does not explain the physical signs |
| **Menopause-related symptoms** | Fatigue, weight gain and low mood can occur. Perimenopause carries a **2–5-fold** risk of depression [3]. Menopause does not explain hypothermia or bradycardia |
| **Prolonged grief disorder** | Loss 2 years ago. Look for **yearning or preoccupation**, emotional pain, identity disturbance, loss of meaning, impairment beyond cultural norms [4] |
| **Hypothyroid organic psychosis or cognitive impairment** | Ask about paranoid ideas, hallucinations, memory loss ("myxoedematous madness") [1] |
| **SSRI-related hyponatraemia** | Fatigue and weakness. Risk factors include **older age, female sex and hypothyroidism** [3]. Check sodium |

### Evaluation
1. **History:**
   - course of depression and response to sertraline; adherence;
   - **current mood, suicidal ideation, hopelessness**;
   - **grief symptoms** [4];
   - memory and concentration;
   - menopausal symptoms;
   - past thyroid surgery or thyroid medication. These "may warrant investigation" in psychiatric patients [1];
   - lithium exposure, a common cause [2];
   - alcohol use; social support (widowed, no children, a marriage marked by alcohol and suspicion).
2. **Mental state:** psychomotor retardation, bradyphrenia, perseveration, **paranoid colouring**, perceptual disturbance, cognitive testing [1].
3. **Examination:**
   - **temperature with a low-reading thermometer**, pulse, BP, consciousness;
   - **slow-relaxing ankle reflexes**;
   - goitre, hair, voice, puffy face;
   - cardiac failure or effusion [1].
4. **Investigations:**
   - **TSH and free T4.** Raised TSH means primary failure; normal TSH with low T4 suggests a pituitary cause [1].
   - **Thyroid antibodies** (thyroglobulin, thyroid peroxidase) for Hashimoto's thyroiditis [1]. Autoimmunity is the commonest cause where salt is iodised [2].
   - **Cholesterol** (raised), **anaemia** [1, 2].
   - **ECG:** low voltage, flat or inverted T waves, and bradycardia [1].
   - **Sodium** (SSRI) [3].
   - **EEG** if confused: low voltage and slowing [1].

### Management
**1. Medical (urgent):**
- **Hypothermia with bradycardia** raises concern for evolving **hypothyroid coma**. Arrange urgent medical assessment and admission if consciousness is impaired [1].
- **Confirm the diagnosis by laboratory tests before starting treatment** [1].
- **Thyroxine replacement** with the physician. Treatment "is usually highly rewarding, with behavioural disturbances responding well to adequate thyroxine therapy" [1].
- Monitor TSH (in the K&S case, TSH normalised at 8 weeks) [2].

**2. Psychiatric:**
- **Continue the antidepressant** for now. "Supplementation with an antidepressant or neuroleptic is often initially helpful" alongside thyroxine [1].
- **Reassess depression after the patient is euthyroid.** Poor response may have reflected hypothyroidism [2].
- **If depression persists despite euthyroidism:** consider augmentation, for example **tri-iodothyronine 20–50 mcg/day**.
  - It is usually well tolerated and has good literature support, but there are **some negative studies** and **no advantage over an antidepressant alone in non-refractory illness**.
  - It needs **clinical and biochemical TFT monitoring** and specialist referral [3].
  - Thyroid hormone can help depressed patients even without hypothyroidism (Bauer 1998) [1].
- **Risk assessment** for suicide.

**3. Grief and psychosocial:**
- **Screen for prolonged grief** [4]. Explore the ambivalent relationship: a husband who drank and was suspicious of her.
- **Address loneliness and isolation**: widowhood and no children. Build social support.

**4. Menopause:**
- Assess menopausal symptoms. **HRT is first-line** for menopausal insomnia, anxiety and depression [3]. Discuss with a gynaecologist, weighing VTE risk [3].

**5. Follow-up:**
- **Cognitive recovery** may lag. Deficits may remain if hypothyroidism went **undiagnosed for long** (Jellinek) [1].
- Repeat TFTs; monitor mood.

**Conclusion.** New cold intolerance, constipation, weight gain, coarse dry skin, bradycardia, hypothermia and swelling point to **overt hypothyroidism** in a middle-aged menopausal woman with depression. It can mimic and worsen depression and blunt the response to sertraline. Steps:
1. Confirm with TFTs, and treat urgently if hypothermia suggests impending coma.
2. Replace thyroxine with the physician.
3. Continue the antidepressant, then reassess once euthyroid.
4. Address grief, isolation and menopausal symptoms.

## In depth

### Neurocognitive deficits in hypothyroid adults and response to treatment (Table 10.3) [1]
| Domain | After treatment |
|---|---|
| General intelligence | Not generally affected |
| Attention and concentration | Improves after 5–7 months |
| Memory | Improves, or treatment prevents further decline |
| Visuospatial | Variable |
| Expressive language | No improvement |

### Thyroid and depression links [1, 2]
- In depression, the commonest TFT change is **mild elevation of T4**, which falls with recovery. **Blunted TSH response to TRH** occurs in about 25% [1].
- **T3** levels predict relapse in unipolar depression. Exogenous T3 appears to **potentiate or accelerate** antidepressant response [2].
- Hypothyroidism **reduces brain 5-HT synthesis** and blood flow in the amygdala, hippocampus and anterior cingulate. These normalise with replacement [2].
