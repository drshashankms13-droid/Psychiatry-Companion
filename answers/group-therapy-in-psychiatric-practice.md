## Exam answer

**Definition.** Group psychotherapy is a treatment in which a **professionally trained leader selects, composes, organises and leads** a group of members who work together towards the goals of each individual and of the group itself. Properties unique to groups — mutual support, feedback from peers, shared information and reduced isolation — are harnessed to relieve psychological suffering [1].

### History in brief [1]

| Year / person | Contribution |
| --- | --- |
| **Joseph Pratt, 1905** | "Classes" for tuberculosis patients in Boston — first recognisable group intervention; improved adherence and mood |
| Lazell, 1921; Marsh | Groups for schizophrenia and manic-depressive patients; Marsh: "*By the crowd they have been broken, by the crowd they shall be healed*" |
| **Jacob Moreno** | Founded psychodrama; first used the term "**group psychotherapy**" |
| Trigant Burrow, 1925 | Coined "group analysis" |
| **Samuel Slavson** | Founder of modern group therapy; activity groups for children; first president of the American Group Psychotherapy Association |
| **Wilfred Bion** | "Group as a whole"; **basic assumption groups** |
| **S.H. Foulkes** | "**Group matrix**"; Group Analytic Society |
| Maxwell Jones, 1950s | **Therapeutic community** |
| Kurt Lewin | Field theory; coined "**group dynamics**" |
| **Irvin Yalom** | Interpersonal, "here-and-now" group therapy; therapeutic factors |

### Types of group [1]

| Feature | Supportive | Psychodynamic | Self-help | Cognitive-behavioural |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Frequency | 1–5 per week | 1–2 per week | Daily (7 per week) | 1–3 per week |
| Size | 8–15 | **5–9** | No limit | 5–10 |
| Goal | Better adaptation to daily living | Reconstruction of personality dynamics | Social support | Relief of specific symptoms |
| Indications | Crises, severe illness | Neuroses, mild personality disorders | Shared life experience | Phobias, anxiety |
| Composition | Homogeneous for level of illness | Balance of similarities and differences | Homogeneous | Homogeneous for symptoms |
| Transference | Not analysed | Used | Not analysed | Not relevant |
| Extra-group contact | Encouraged | Discouraged | Encouraged (sponsors) | Discouraged |

Other orientations: interpersonal (Yalom), **psychodrama**, systems, Gestalt, transactional analysis (Berne's Parent–Adult–Child ego states), redecision therapy; self-help groups (AA, Al-Anon, bipolar and cancer support groups) [1].

### Therapeutic factors

**Yalom's 13 factors** [1]: **universality**, **altruism**, **instillation of hope**, **imparting information**, corrective recapitulation of the primary family group, development of socialising techniques, **imitative behaviour**, **cohesiveness**, existential factors, **catharsis**, **interpersonal learning** (input and output), self-understanding.

- **Cohesion** ("we-ness") is believed to be the most important factor related to positive outcome [1].
- MacKenzie's four groups: supportive, learning, self-revelation and psychological work factors [1].

### Selection and preparation [1]

- **Suitable:** an interpersonal problem, motivation, capacity for empathy, enough ego strength not to be the sole focus, some psychological-mindedness, willingness to self-disclose.
- **Exclude or defer:** acutely psychotic inpatients not stabilised on medication, acutely suicidal patients, those who do not speak the group's language; antisocial personality disorder is often excluded except in homogeneous settings such as prison or substance groups.
- **Pre-group preparation** reduces drop-out and anxiety and builds the alliance — a 14-item orientation checklist covers goals, composition, the leader's role, recording, setting, time frame, member changes, attendance, fees, coordination with other therapies, **extra-group socialisation**, contract changes, **confidentiality** and questions.

### Stages of group development (AGPA, 5 stages) [1]

**Forming/pre-affiliation** (inclusion, search for similarities) → **storming/power and control** (testing the leader, conflict) → **norming/intimacy** (cohesion, deeper disclosure) → **performing/differentiation** (transferences, members share responsibility) → **adjourning/separation** (denial, regression, recapitulation; review and closure).

### Leadership

- Self-awareness, empathy, belief in groups, interpersonal skill; set and hold the frame; contain intense affect; manage countertransference; use interpretation in time; cautious self-disclosure [1].
- **Co-therapy** shares the load (e.g. addiction groups); a **recovered peer** with at least 2 years' sobriety can be part of the team [1].

### Clinical applications [1]

| Population | Group approach |
| --- | --- |
| Mood disorders | Psychoeducation, problem-solving, stigma reduction (**Life Goals** programme for bipolar disorder) |
| Personality disorders | **Interpersonal group psychotherapy** for BPD; **DBT skills groups** |
| Anxiety disorders | **CBT groups** — the fastest-growing form; manualised, time-limited |
| Schizophrenia | Inpatient activity groups → transitional → aftercare; social skills, cognitive and psychoeducation groups |
| Substance use | 12-step, harm-reduction groups, relapse prevention, sobriety networks |
| Trauma, medical illness, bereavement, eating disorders, elderly, adolescents | Homogeneous groups |

### Advantages and limitations

- **Advantages:** cost-effective (more patients per therapist), peer support, normalisation, opportunities to practise social behaviour, multiple feedback, role-modelling.
- **Limitations:** less individual attention, confidentiality harder to guarantee, risk of scapegoating or harmful confrontation, drop-out, need for trained leaders.

**Conclusion.** Group therapy is a versatile, evidence-informed and economical modality that harnesses therapeutic factors unique to groups — especially cohesion, universality and interpersonal learning. Careful selection, pre-group preparation and stage-appropriate leadership are what make it effective across mood, anxiety, personality, psychotic and substance use disorders.

## In depth

### Background — phases of modern development [1]

1. **World War II:** large numbers of psychiatric casualties → groups to treat more people in less time (mostly psychodynamic).
2. **Community mental health movement (1960s–70s):** deinstitutionalisation → homogeneous groups for people sharing a condition.
3. **Managed care (1990s):** brief, symptom-focused, time-limited groups.
4. **2000s:** terrorism, disasters and trauma groups; groups addressing internet addiction, gender and sexuality, and new family forms.

Freud's *Group Psychology and the Analysis of the Ego* addressed large-group phenomena; Wolf adapted psychoanalysis to groups (the "go-around"); Ezriel focused on here-and-now member-to-member transferences; Ackerman worked at the group–family interface; T-groups and encounter groups (National Training Laboratories) showed the risks of passive or charismatic leadership [1].

### Therapeutic factors in more detail (Sadock's list) [1]

| Factor | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Abreaction | Reliving repressed painful material with emotion, leading to insight |
| Acceptance | Feeling accepted; differences tolerated |
| Altruism | Helping another member; term from Auguste Comte |
| Catharsis | Expression of suppressed material with emotional relief |
| Cohesion | Working together towards a common goal; most important factor |
| Consensual validation | Correcting distortions by comparison with others (H.S. Sullivan) |
| Contagion | One member's emotion stimulates the same in another |
| Corrective familial experience | Reworking family-of-origin conflicts |
| Empathy, identification, imitation | Understanding and modelling others ("spectator therapy") |
| Insight | Intellectual vs emotional insight |
| Inspiration | Instillation of hope |
| Interpretation | Leader formulates meaning of resistances and defences |
| Reality testing | Accurate perception of self and others |
| Transference | Including **multiple transferences** among members |
| Universalisation | "I am not alone" |
| Ventilation | Self-disclosure relieving guilt |

### Selection tools and session format [1]

- **Group Therapy Questionnaire** and **Group Selection Questionnaire** (CORE-R battery) help decide suitability.
- The usual session lasts **90 minutes**; brief CBT groups may be 1 hour for 12 weeks; inpatient groups 30–45 minutes.
- Closed (short-term) groups do not replace drop-outs; open (long-term) groups add members after working through the loss.
- Breach of confidentiality is grounds for dismissal; members must commit to it before joining.

### Combined therapy [1]

- Individual and group treatment together, by the same or different therapists.
- Risk of **splitting** in borderline or narcissistic patients; therapists must communicate regularly.
- Patients use individual sessions for intrapsychic work and the group to practise new behaviour.

### Groups for psychosis [1]

- Older psychoanalytic techniques were unsuitable for psychotic patients; modern groups are structured.
- **Acute** (inpatient activity, art, occupational, community meetings) → **transitional** → **maintenance/aftercare**; goals must be realistic.
- Attendance rules can be relaxed; attachment to the group or programme over time matters more than session attendance.

### Evidence

- Meta-analyses generally show group therapy to be about as effective as individual therapy for depression, anxiety, social anxiety, substance use and eating disorders, at lower cost (author's knowledge; e.g. Burlingame et al.).
- CBT groups are the most researched because they are manualised and standardised [1].
- DBT skills groups, interpersonal group therapy for BPD and life goals programmes for bipolar disorder have supporting trials [1].

### Critical appraisal

- Outcome research favours structured groups because they are easier to measure; psychodynamic groups are under-researched rather than proven ineffective [1].
- Poorly led groups can harm vulnerable members (premature confrontation, scapegoating) — leadership training matters [1].
- Cultural factors (shame, gender norms, hierarchy) affect self-disclosure and need adaptation.

### Indian context

- Family involvement is often preferred over stranger groups; **multi-family psychoeducation groups** are well accepted in Indian services.
- Group formats are common in **de-addiction centres** (including AA and NA meetings), rehabilitation day-care programmes and inpatient wards (community meetings, occupational therapy).
- Language diversity and stigma limit outpatient groups; homogeneous groups (same language, same condition) work better.
- Groups are a cost-effective way to address the shortage of trained therapists.

### Recent advances

- **Online and video-based groups** expanded during COVID-19; evidence suggests comparable outcomes for structured groups (author's knowledge).
- Transdiagnostic CBT groups (e.g. Unified Protocol) treat mixed anxiety and depression together.
- Peer-led and peer-co-facilitated groups as part of recovery-oriented services.
