> **Source discipline:** this answer uses only *Kaplan & Sadock's Comprehensive Textbook of Psychiatry*, 10th ed., §27.8 "Psycho-oncology" [1]. The question gives no sub-questions, so the answer follows the source's sequence: assessment, diagnosis, suicide risk, then management.

## Exam answer

**Case summary.**
- **Patient:** Mrs X, 68 years old, treated for **breast cancer 5 years ago**, now newly diagnosed with **recurrence**.
- **Presentation:** she broke down, crying, and said she **does not want to live any more**.
- **Past history:** a brief **depression after her third child's birth**.
- **Family history:** a **strong history of depression and suicide**.
- **Supports:** her 70-year-old husband is very supportive; her daughter's family lives next door and she visits them daily.

### 1. Understanding the reaction: normal response or disorder? [1]
- **Recurrence or treatment failure** is typically met with disbelief and denial, then emotional upheaval with mixed depression and anxiety, poor appetite, insomnia and poor concentration. This usually **resolves over 7–10 days**.
- The normal response to a cancer diagnosis also has three phases: shock (under 1 week), turmoil (1–2 weeks), then adaptation.
- **The clinical question** is when normal sadness has become a **depressive disorder**. Mrs X needs a careful evaluation rather than reassurance alone, because she carries several risk factors (below).

### 2. Assessment [1]
**Standard psychiatric history**, plus:
- **Her understanding of the recurrence and prognosis:** what she and the family have been told, and what they have absorbed. Discrepancies between patient, family and staff cause distress.
- **The meaning of the illness** at this life stage.
- **Medical factors affecting mental state:**
  - **CNS metastasis**;
  - electrolyte disturbance and **hypercalcaemia**;
  - B12 or folate deficiency; **thyroid** abnormalities;
  - **pain**;
  - drugs: **tamoxifen**, corticosteroids, opioids, chemotherapy agents.
- **Environment:** family functioning and its interface with the oncology team.

**Depression in a medically ill patient:**
- Rely on **dysphoria, anhedonia, hopelessness, worthlessness, excessive guilt and suicidal ideation**, because appetite, sleep, fatigue and concentration overlap with cancer and its treatment.
- In elderly patients, poor memory and concentration may be the presenting complaint.
- If psychotic features are present, **exclude delirium first**: it is commonly misdiagnosed as depression, especially the hypoactive type.
- Use the **inclusive approach** clinically (count all symptoms), because it is the most sensitive.
- PHQ-9 or BDI can screen, but do not separate MDD from depression due to a medical condition.

**Her risk factors for depression [1]:**
- **breast cancer** is a site with a higher prevalence of depression;
- **previous depression** (postpartum);
- **family history of depression**;
- **recurrence**, meaning more advanced disease;
- older age;
- possible uncontrolled pain or drug effects (tamoxifen).

### 3. Suicide risk assessment [1]
- Suicide risk in cancer patients exceeds that of the general population, and is highest **early after diagnosis**. A small long-term excess persists even 25 years or more after a breast cancer diagnosis.
- **Risk factors present**:
  - possible depression;
  - **positive family history of suicide**;
  - **previous history of depression**;
  - **breast cancer**;
  - **older age**.
- **Risk factors to explore:**
  - **hopelessness**;
  - uncontrolled pain;
  - extreme fatigue, anxiety, delirium;
  - substance use;
  - feeling alone;
  - **intolerance of loss of control, dependence and loss of dignity**;
  - financial problems;
  - active statements and plans.
- **Protective factors:** strong objective **social support** (husband, daughter next door, daily contact with grandchildren).
- **Principles:**
  - **suicidal thoughts in a patient with advanced disease should not be viewed as rational**; they may reflect a **treatable major depressive episode**;
  - assess **hopelessness and depression** in anyone with a persistent desire for death;
  - explore the reasons for the thoughts and the seriousness of the risk;
  - **listen empathically and without judgement**. Discussing suicidal thoughts often **decreases** risk;
  - acknowledging her need to retain a **sense of control** often leads patients to reject suicide.

### 4. Provisional diagnosis
- **Depressive disorder in a patient with recurrent breast cancer**, to be differentiated from:
  - an **adjustment disorder with depressed mood**, the commonest psychiatric diagnosis in cancer;
  - a normal reaction to recurrence;
  - **depressive disorder due to another medical condition** (for example, CNS metastasis, hypercalcaemia) or **medication-induced** depression (for example, tamoxifen);
  - **delirium**.
- The distinction rests on severity, persistence beyond 7–10 days, and core cognitive symptoms (hopelessness, worthlessness, guilt, suicidality).

### 5. Management [1]
**Immediate safety:**
- If risk is high, **acute psychiatric hospitalisation** may be needed.
- Early psychiatric involvement with high-risk patients can often avert suicide.
- **Initial steps:** maintain a supportive relationship, **control symptoms** (pain, nausea, depression), and **involve family**.

**Therapeutic alliance and support:**
- An alliance with the psychiatrist, and recruitment of family support.
- The **relationship with the oncologist** is a key source of support.
- **Ongoing contact** ensures continued evaluation and care.

**Treat reversible causes** (thyroid, drugs, metabolic), but **start antidepressants concurrently** to relieve suffering quickly.

**Pharmacotherapy:**
- **SSRIs first-line:**
  - sertraline, citalopram and escitalopram have fewer P450 interactions;
  - **citalopram maximum 20 mg/day over age 60** (QT).
- **If she is on tamoxifen:** avoid strong **CYP2D6 inhibitors** (fluoxetine, paroxetine). Prefer **venlafaxine or citalopram**.
- **Venlafaxine** also reduces **hot flushes** after breast cancer treatment.
- **Mirtazapine** for insomnia, weight loss or nausea.
- **Psychostimulants** (methylphenidate) for **rapid** relief of fatigue and psychomotor slowing if prognosis is short.
- **Start low and go slow** in a frail elderly patient.
- **ECT** if refractory, with severe weight loss, psychosis or **high suicide risk**.

**Psychotherapy:**
- **supportive psychotherapy** and **CBT**;
- **meaning-centred psychotherapy** (Frankl-based) for existential distress if the disease is advanced;
- **group therapy** (supportive-expressive, cognitive-existential) to reduce isolation;
- crisis intervention first, then other modes. Returning episodically at relapse is not resistance.

**Family:**
- Her family is a strength. Engage the husband and daughter, and screen **caregiver distress**, which can equal or exceed the patient's.
- Watch for a "**conspiracy of silence**".
- Educate them that attitude does not determine survival, to avoid **blaming the victim**.

**Spiritual:** ask about faith and its meaning; refer to a chaplain if she wishes.

**Follow-up:**
- Re-evaluate mood and suicidality through treatment of the recurrence.
- Respect her autonomy: a **competent patient may refuse treatment**.

### Epidemiology to quote [1]
- Psychiatric disorder affects about 50% of cancer patients (adjustment disorder more than two-thirds of these; major depression 10–15%).
- Depressive or adjustment disorder: 25% (meta-analysis).
- In early breast cancer, depression is twice the rate in women generally, especially in year 1. One large study estimated about 53%.
