## Exam answer

**Introduction.**
- **What it is:** JME is an **idiopathic (genetic) generalised epilepsy** of adolescence, defined by **myoclonic jerks on awakening**, usually with generalised tonic–clonic seizures [1].
- **Frequency:** about **10% of all epilepsy**.
- **Why recognise it:**
  - a **specific drug response** (valproate excellent; **carbamazepine and phenytoin worsen it**);
  - a **need for lifelong treatment**.
- **Spectrum:** the idiopathic generalised epilepsies form a **continuum**: childhood absence → juvenile absence → JME → GTCS on awakening.

### Epidemiology and genetics [1]
- **Family history** of epilepsy in up to 50%; variable inheritance; **genetic heterogeneity**.
- Familial JME is listed among the ligand-gated channelopathies (Table 6.6).
- **Sex:** M = F.
- **Onset:** **80% between 12 and 18 years**.

### Clinical features [1]

| Seizure type | Frequency | Features |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Myoclonic jerks** | **Defining and necessary** (alone in under 5%) | Jerks **soon after awakening**. Patients describe "shakiness", "clumsiness", "twitches", "nervousness". **Often not volunteered**: they precede the first GTCS by months to years. A cluster may warn of an impending GTCS |
| **Generalised tonic–clonic seizures** | **95%** | Typically **on awakening**; usually the presenting event |
| **Absences** | About 40% | May **precede myoclonus by years**, so patients are first labelled "absence epilepsy" |

- **Precipitants:**
  - **sleep deprivation**;
  - **alcohol the night before**;
  - **photosensitivity**: "up to 30%" in the JME section, but "40–90%" in the photosensitivity section. ⚠️ Lishman is internally inconsistent here.
- **Neurological examination and intelligence:** normal.

### Diagnosis [1]
- **History:** **ask specifically** about morning jerks, clumsiness and "nervousness". Family members may have noticed them.
- **EEG:**
  - interictal **4–6 Hz generalised polyspike-and-slow-wave** discharges lasting up to 20 seconds;
  - **sleep deprivation**, hyperventilation and **photic stimulation** increase yield (photoparoxysmal response);
  - **over 50% have focal EEG features** (asymmetry, focal slow waves or spikes), which can **mislead towards focal epilepsy**.
- **MRI:** **not required** for definite idiopathic generalised epilepsy (ILAE imaging recommendation).
- **Differential diagnosis:**
  - **epilepsy with GTCS on awakening** (no myoclonus, or myoclonus appearing later; considerable overlap);
  - juvenile absence epilepsy;
  - focal epilepsy (because of the focal EEG features);
  - non-epileptic jerks or "clumsiness".

### Treatment [1, 2]
- **Valproate:** **over 80% become seizure-free on monotherapy**. Valproate remains the **drug of choice for generalised epilepsy**.
- **Second line:** **lamotrigine, topiramate, levetiracetam**.
- **Avoid carbamazepine and phenytoin:** they typically **increase seizure frequency**.
- **Duration:** **lifelong treatment** is widely held to be necessary because of a **very high relapse rate on withdrawal**.
- **Lifestyle:** regular sleep; moderate alcohol; avoid photic triggers (cover one eye, tinted glasses, high-refresh screens).
- **Women of child-bearing potential:**
  - valproate is a **major teratogen** with neurodevelopmental effects (Maudsley: not to be used in women of child-bearing potential) [2];
  - Lishman calls young women with generalised epilepsy a "particularly difficult treatment dilemma" [1].

### Psychiatric aspects [1]
- **Personality (Janz):**
  - **irresponsibility, impulsivity, self-centredness, emotional instability**;
  - not systematically studied (may reflect adolescence).
  - **Borderline personality disorder in 11/170** consecutive JME patients (Gelisse 2001), unlike TLE series.
- **Depression:**
  - patients with generalised epilepsy are also at risk;
  - in one comparison, depression occurred in 3/18 with JME vs 11/20 with TLE (confounded by seizure frequency).
- **Adherence and lifestyle** (sleep deprivation, alcohol) are central in adolescents; impulsivity can undermine control.

**Conclusion.** JME:
- is a common (about 10%) adolescent-onset genetic generalised epilepsy;
- presents with **morning myoclonic jerks** (often unreported), **GTCS on awakening** (95%) and absences (40%);
- is triggered by **sleep deprivation, alcohol and flicker**;
- has **4–6 Hz polyspike-wave** EEG discharges.

It responds excellently to **valproate** (but consider teratogenicity in women; lamotrigine, levetiracetam or topiramate as alternatives), is **aggravated by carbamazepine and phenytoin**, and needs **lifelong** treatment. Impulsive or borderline traits may complicate adherence.

## In depth

### Why JME is often missed [1]
- Myoclonic jerks are dismissed as morning clumsiness.
- Patients present only after a GTCS.
- Focal EEG features suggest focal epilepsy, so carbamazepine is chosen and **worsens** seizures.
