## Exam answer

**Introduction.**
- Endocrine disorders and mental illness have an intimate, **bidirectional** relationship [1].
- **History** [1, 2]:
  - Kraepelin hypothesised that dementia praecox was an endocrine disorder, caused by lack of thyroxine early in development.
  - In 1888 the Committee of the Clinical Society of London first confirmed a link between thyroid disorder and psychiatric morbidity.
  - Asher described psychotic symptoms in 14 hypothyroid patients and named this **"myxoedema madness"** (1949).
- **Common features:** depression, anxiety and cognitive difficulties are the commonest neuropsychiatric symptoms (NPS) of thyroid disease [1].
- **Overlap with primary disorders:** hypothyroid symptoms can be mistaken for depression. Hyperthyroidism presents with anxiety (up to 80%), emotional lability, dysphoria, depression (up to 70%) and, rarely, mania [1].
- **Screening:** both hypo- and hyperthyroidism cause cognitive impairment, so thyroid screening is recommended in cognitive disorders. There is no consensus on population screening, but the Garber CPG endorses **case-finding in psychiatric disorders** [1].

**Neurobiology** [2]:
- Thyroid hormone receptors are present in the CNS, mainly in limbic structures. They interact with noradrenaline and serotonin pathways.
- T3 enhances GABA release and calcium influx, and facilitates neurotransmitter release.
- Dopamine and glucocorticoids decrease TSH secretion.
- Oestrogen increases TBG and free T4; androgens have the opposite effect.
- Hypothyroidism is linked to:
  - decreased β-receptor function;
  - reduced perfusion of the amygdala, hippocampus and anterior cingulate;
  - lower central serotonin activity (serotonin correlates with T3).

### 1. Hypothyroidism

**Definition and biochemistry.** Overt hypothyroidism (OH) means raised TSH with low T4 and/or T3, plus clinical evidence of deficient thyroid hormone action [1].

**Causes** [2]: iodine deficiency, Hashimoto's thyroiditis, lithium.

**Epidemiology** [1, 2]:
- 2% of women and 0.1% of men.
- Most patients with affective disorders are euthyroid, but 1–4% have OH and **4–40% have subclinical hypothyroidism (SCH)**.
- Up to **52% of refractory depression** shows evidence of SCH.
- Up to **25% of female rapid cyclers** are hypothyroid.
- Meta-analysis odds in hypothyroidism: **depression 3.56, anxiety 2.32**.

**Neuropsychiatric features** [1, 2]:
- **Course:** ill-defined, non-specific, insidious onset. Can occur in some euthyroid, antibody-positive patients [2].
- **Early cognitive deficits:** attention, concentration, memory (mainly verbal memory) and processing speed.
- **With chronicity:** inability to manage daily chores and slowed processing.
- **Striking features:** psychomotor retardation and increased fatiguability. Difficulty with sustained mental effort, understanding complex questions and learning new tasks. Reduced concern for others and reduced responsiveness.
- **Mood:** depressed mood, apathy, daytime somnolence. Melancholic depression in severe cases [2].
- **Severe cases:** illusions and **visual hallucinations leading to paranoia**. The psychosis is an admixture of affective and schizophrenia-like features (myxoedema madness).

**Differential diagnosis** [1]:
- **Resemblance to depression:** reduced responsiveness, slowed thought and action, and attention problems mimic depression.
- **Depression in OH:** coloured by **irritability** and may run a chronic course.
- **Psychosis in OH:** laden with **confusion, persistent cognitive disturbance and visual hallucinations**.

**Treatment** [1, 2]:
- **Levothyroxine (LT4)** improves NPS. Bringing TSH into the physiological range generally suffices, but complete resolution is not consistent.
- **Residual NPS** can persist despite adequate LT4 (up to 1.6 µg/kg/day), for example in long-standing dementia. They have been attributed to a disease-labelling effect or ascertainment bias [1]. Other reasons: a co-existing psychiatric disorder, poor adherence, irreversibility in severe cases [2].
- **Work-up of residual NPS (McDermott; Table 1)** [1]:
  - physical examination;
  - obstructive sleep apnoea screening;
  - metabolic panel;
  - vitamin D;
  - thyroid antibodies;
  - exercise, diet and sleep hygiene;
  - change of levothyroxine brand.
- **T3 augmentation** of LT4 (T3 not available in India) helped in early reports, but controlled studies are inconsistent [1]. T3 addition may help with a D2 deiodinase gene polymorphism [2].

### 2. Subclinical hypothyroidism [1, 2]
- **Definition:** raised TSH with normal free T4 and T3.
- **Prevalence:** 7.5% of women, 3% of men, 3–5% of pregnancies [2].
- **NPS:** depression, anxiety and cognitive problems are reported in the young, but findings are inconsistent. Reasons: different TSH upper limits and the physiological rise with age. Mild depressive symptoms and cognitive deficits occur in the elderly [2].
- **Risk factors for progression to OH:** **female sex, TSH >10 mIU/L, thyroid antibodies**. Their presence is a reason to start LT4 to prevent progression.
- **Treatment outcome:** NPS response to LT4 is modest and inconsistent.

### 3. Hyperthyroidism (thyrotoxicosis)

**Definition and causes** [1, 2]:
- **Biochemistry:** TSH <0.1 mIU/L with raised thyroid hormone.
- **Commonest cause:** **Graves' disease** (80% of cases; 4× more common in women; onset in the 30s–40s) [2].
- **Other causes** [2]: toxic nodular goitre, solitary adenoma, thyroiditis, exogenous thyroid hormone, TSH-secreting adenoma, carcinoma.
- **Prevalence:** 0.5%. Postpartum thyroiditis 5–9% [2].

**Neuropsychiatric features** [1, 2]:
- **Mechanism:** symptoms resemble primary psychiatric disorders because of **secondary hyperadrenergic activity**.
- **Onset:** usually **abrupt**.
- **Symptoms:** nervousness, fatigue, restlessness, irritability, overactivity, emotional lability, poor frustration tolerance. Also distractibility and an exaggerated startle response.
- **Anxiety and mania:** excess thyroid hormone may precipitate anxiety or mania. **T4 correlates with anxiety, not depression.** Anxiety disorders occur in about 60% [2].
- **Course of mood:** an **inflated sense of well-being** early (mania-like). **Depression** appears in prolonged disease, through noradrenergic exhaustion.
- **Apathetic hyperthyroidism** (elderly): depression, lethargy, apathy, mental slowing or pseudodementia, without eye signs or adrenergic features.
- **Delirium:** in severe disease, but rare. **Thyroid storm** is life-threatening: tachycardia, weakness, fever, altered mental status [2].
- **Psychosis:** uncommon; an admixture of affective and schizophrenia-like features.
- **Suicide:** rates are increased in Graves' disease, especially with orbitopathy [2].

**Differential diagnosis: anxiety states** [1]:

| Pointer | Hyperthyroidism | Anxiety state |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Temperature | Heat intolerance, preference for cold | — |
| Eye signs | Classical signs, e.g., lid lag | Absent |
| Appetite and weight | **Increased appetite despite weight loss** | Appetite reduced |
| Stress precipitation | Present | More common and more exclusive |

Other differentials [2]: panic disorder, GAD, phobias, hypoglycaemia, dehydration, phaeochromocytoma, COPD.

**Investigations** [2]: blunted TSH response to TRH. Radioactive iodine uptake is raised, except in thyroiditis and exogenous hormone use.

**Treatment** [1, 2]:
- **Response to euthyroidism:** most patients improve once euthyroid (**50% completely, 35% partially; 15% no recovery**). Residual symptoms occasionally need psychotropics.
- **Beta-blockers** give prompt relief even before euthyroidism. **Propranolol 60–80 mg** is preferred because it also reduces peripheral T4→T3 conversion [1].
- **Antithyroid drugs** [2]: propylthiouracil 75–100 mg TDS or methimazole 10–40 mg OD. Radioactive iodine and subtotal thyroidectomy are other options. Lugol's iodine before surgery prevents thyroid storm.
- **Psychotropic cautions:** avoid antipsychotics that cause tachycardia or QT prolongation; use TCAs with caution [2].

### 4. Other thyroid-related syndromes
- **Autoimmune (Hashimoto's) thyroiditis** [2]:
  - can present as bipolar disorder, acute psychosis, depression, dementia or acute encephalopathy;
  - antibodies may impair myelin integrity;
  - MRI shows non-specific white-matter hyperintensities and frontal hypoperfusion.
- **Thyroid cancer** [2]: anxiety and depression are common.
- **Non-thyroidal illness** [1]:
  - systemic illness lowers T3 and T4, and suppresses TSH in severe forms;
  - acute psychosis or mood disorder can cause **hyperthyroxinaemia**;
  - substance use disorders can raise TSH.

### 5. Thyroid and refractory depression [1]
- **Thyroid abnormalities in depression:** depression is linked not to overt thyroid disease but to subtle irregularities. These are raised TSH, altered thyroid-hormone circadian rhythm and a blunted TSH response to TRH.
- **Upper-quartile TSH** (within the normal range) is associated with recurrent, severe, poorly responding depression.
- **TSH and brain thyroid status:** TSH may not reflect brain thyroid status. In the D2 deiodinase **Thr92Ala** polymorphism, impaired T4→T3 conversion causes "brain hypothyroidism".
- **Thyroid hormone augmentation:** T3 (better studied) or T4 has been added to augment or accelerate antidepressant response even without biochemical thyroid dysfunction. With **SCH or OH**, T4/T3 supplementation is warranted in refractory depression or rapid cycling.

### 6. Psychotropics and the thyroid [1, 2]

| Drug | Effect | Action |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Lithium** | Impairs hormone secretion and iodine trapping; inhibits synthesis. Goitre 2.5–50% (non-tender; may appear within weeks). **OH 8–19%, SCH up to 23%.** Mean TSH difference about 4.0 mIU/L over about 70 months | **TSH at baseline, then at least 6-monthly.** Hypothyroidism **never justifies stopping lithium.** Supplement in OH, and in SCH if TSH >10 mIU/L |
| Lithium-induced risk factors | Antithyroid antibodies, female sex, older age, family history of thyroid disease | — |
| Lithium-induced **hyperthyroidism** | Can occur years later; more common in women; short-lived painless thyroiditis; many later become hypothyroid | Treat as hyperthyroidism (e.g., carbimazole) |
| Lithium and calcium | Raises calcium (by 10%) and PTH | Check calcium with thyroid function tests |
| **Carbamazepine** | Lowers free T4 within 2 months without clinical hypothyroidism; reversible | Monitoring not routinely needed; **monitor with the valproate + CBZ combination** (free T4 falls, TSH rises) |
| **TCAs, phenothiazines** | Drug–iodide complexes can cause clinical hypothyroidism | Regular thyroid monitoring |
| Prolactin-raising antipsychotics | Derange thyroid function tests more | — |
| Methadone, stimulants | Euthyroid hyperthyroxinaemia | — |
| Benzodiazepines | No effect on thyroid function | — |

### 7. General principle [1, 2]
- **Endocrine treatment first:** organic psychiatric syndromes from thyroid disorders mostly need endocrine treatment alone, **unless NPS persist after 4 weeks of adequate treatment or are severe**.
- **Screen for endocrinopathy:** serum electrolytes, fasting blood sugar, **TSH** and calcium should be measured to rule out endocrine disease. Endocrinopathy should be considered in **treatment-resistant psychiatric disorders** [2].

**Conclusion.** Thyroid dysfunction mimics primary psychiatric disorders:
- **hypothyroidism:** depression, slowing and cognitive decline, with myxoedema madness in severe cases;
- **hyperthyroidism:** anxiety, lability, early mania-like states, and apathetic depression in the elderly.

Correcting thyroid status is the core treatment, with propranolol for adrenergic symptoms and psychotropics for persistent or severe NPS. Thyroid monitoring is essential with lithium and some other psychotropics.

### Lithium-induced thyroid dysfunction: algorithm logic (IPS CPG Figure 1) [1]
1. Check TSH before lithium and then at least every 6 months.
2. If hypothyroid (overt), or subclinical with TSH >10: add levothyroxine and continue lithium.
3. If hyperthyroid: antithyroid treatment (e.g., carbimazole). Watch for later hypothyroidism.
4. Check calcium as well (lithium-induced hyperparathyroidism).
5. If lithium is stopped and thyroxine withdrawn, hypothyroidism can re-emerge. Lithium may accelerate underlying thyroiditis or SCH.

### Clinical pearls from the sources
- **Inverse use:** lithium is used as a short-term add-on to radioiodine in treating hyperthyroidism [1].
- **Assume an endocrine cause first:** psychiatric symptoms may appear early and physical symptoms later. Depression severity may correlate with the severity of the hormonal disorder [2].
- **Antibody-positive euthyroid patients** may still show neuropsychiatric symptoms [2].
- **Pregnancy:** anti-TPO and anti-TG antibodies predispose to postpartum thyroiditis and later hypothyroidism [2].

### Background
- **Clinical Society of London (1888):** some mental disturbance, from irritability and agoraphobia to **dementia and melancholia**, in almost all untreated myxoedema patients. Psychosis in about **15%** [3].
- **Asher (1949), "Myxoedematous madness":** 14 patients [3]:
  - 5 with an organic reaction (hallucinations, persecutory ideas);
  - 5 schizophrenia-like with paranoid colouring;
  - 2 advanced dementia;
  - 2 depressive.
  - **The hypothyroidism had been missed by the referring doctor in every case.**
- **Whybrow (1969)** described the psychological profiles of hypo- and hyperthyroidism. **Prange and colleagues (1969)** introduced **T3 acceleration** of imipramine [3; general knowledge for Prange].
- **Thyroid–depression links:**
  - **TRH test blunting** in about 25% of depressed patients;
  - loss of the nocturnal TSH surge;
  - **mild hyperthyroxinaemia** in depression, which falls with recovery [3, 4].

### Epidemiology
**Hypothyroidism [3, 4]:**
- female:male about **8:1** [3];
- **Whickham survey:** raised TSH in **7.5% of women and 2.8% of men** [3];
- **up to 21% of women over 74** [3];
- mild:overt ratio from 5:1 to 15:1 [3];
- overt hypothyroidism: 2% of women, under 0.1% of men [4];
- **SCH progresses to overt disease at 5–8% a year**. Risk is higher with high TSH, female sex, age, antibodies and family autoimmunity [4].
- **Pregnancy:** overt 0.3–0.5%; SCH 3–5% [4].
- **Postpartum thyroiditis about 7.5%** (5–9%). **20–40% develop permanent hypothyroidism** [4].

**Psychiatric populations [3, 4]:**
- **bipolar disorder not on lithium**: hypothyroidism in 25% of women and 12% of men [4];
- **rapid cycling**: up to 25% [3];
- **lithium**:
  - about **20% overt** hypothyroidism and 20% subclinical;
  - **goitre up to 50%**;
  - **women 5 times more often**;
  - risk higher with age and baseline antibodies [4];
- in a survey of 1,200 psychiatric inpatients, **8 had thyrotoxicosis**, **6 unsuspected** [3].

**Hyperthyroidism [3, 4]:**
- about **0.5%**, with subclinical disease in a further 0.8% [3];
- female:male about **6:1** [3];
- **Graves' disease about 80%**, with onset in the 3rd–4th decades [4];
- **toxic nodular goitre** in the elderly [3, 4].
- **About 1% of thyrotoxic patients are first diagnosed with a major psychiatric illness** (Brownlie 2000) [3].

**India [general knowledge]:**
- **hypothyroidism about 11%** in an adult urban multicentre study (Unnikrishnan 2013);
- SCH about 8%;
- anti-TPO positivity about 21%;
- historical **iodine-deficiency** belts (sub-Himalayan) despite salt iodisation.

### Classification
| Category | Biochemistry | Neuropsychiatric relevance |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Overt primary hypothyroidism** | ↑TSH, ↓fT4 [3, 4] | Cognitive slowing, depression, psychosis ("myxoedema madness"), dementia, coma |
| **Subclinical hypothyroidism** | ↑TSH, normal fT4 [3, 4] | Mild depression, working-memory and executive deficits (inconsistent); **poorer antidepressant response** [4] |
| **Central (secondary/tertiary)** | Low or normal TSH, ↓fT4; absent or delayed TRH response [3] | Pituitary tumour; postpartum haemorrhage (Sheehan); consider other axes |
| **Euthyroid sick syndrome** | ↓T3, normal TSH (↓T4 when severe) [4] | In medical and psychiatric illness; do not treat |
| **"Acute stress hyperthyroxinaemia"** | Transient ↑T4 on psychiatric admission in about **9%** of 480 admissions (Cohen and Swigar); normalises in weeks [3] | Avoid misdiagnosis; repeat after weeks |
| **Overt hyperthyroidism** | ↓TSH, ↑fT4 and/or fT3 [3, 4] | Anxiety, irritability, agitated depression, mania, psychosis, delirium (storm) |
| **T3 toxicosis** | Normal fT4, ↑fT3 (about 1%) [3] | Measure fT3 when suspicion is high |
| **Subclinical hyperthyroidism** | ↓TSH, normal hormones [4] | Nervousness, irritability, AF risk |
| **Inappropriate TSH** | ↑T4 with non-suppressed TSH: TSH-oma or thyroid-hormone resistance [3] | Endocrine referral |
| **Apathetic hyperthyroidism** | Biochemical thyrotoxicosis | **Elderly; anergia, depression, weight loss; no eye signs** [3] |
| **Autoimmune thyroiditis (antibody-positive euthyroid)** | Anti-TPO/anti-Tg positive | Higher depressive symptoms in some studies [4] |
| **Hashimoto encephalopathy (SREAT)** | Very high anti-TPO; any thyroid status | Encephalopathy, seizures, myoclonus, psychosis; steroid-responsive [general knowledge] |

### Mechanism in detail
**Thyroid hormone physiology [4]:**
- T4 is converted to **T3 (the active form)** peripherally and, in the brain, by **type 2 deiodinase in astrocytes** [4; general knowledge for D2].
- Only about 0.3% of T3 and 0.02% of T4 are free.
- **T4 is the main determinant of thyroid hormone available to the brain**; T3 is the main determinant for peripheral organs [4].
- **T3 is a neuromodulator**: it enhances GABA release and calcium influx [4].
- **Nuclear T3 receptors** are dense in the **amygdala and hippocampus** (mood regions) and sparse in the brainstem and cerebellum [3].

**Hypothyroidism [3, 4]:**
- **Cerebral hypometabolism:**
  - **cerebral blood flow ↓38%**;
  - O2 and glucose use ↓27%;
  - cerebrovascular resistance about doubled (Scheinberg 1950);
  - anaemia worsens it;
  - PET shows **global hypometabolism** beyond what vascular resistance explains [3].
- **Monoamines:**
  - ↓ postsynaptic **β-receptor** number and function;
  - **↓ brain 5-HT synthesis** (blunted prolactin and cortisol response to fenfluramine);
  - serum 5-HT correlates with T3 [4].
- **Regional CBF:** reduced in the **amygdala, hippocampus and anterior cingulate**, restored by treatment (ventral ACC) [4].
- **Autoimmunity** itself may cause fatigue and low motivation, even when euthyroid [4].
- EEG slowing, which improves with replacement [3].

**Hyperthyroidism [3]:**
- the direct effect of raised thyroxine on CNS metabolism;
- **increased β-adrenergic sensitivity**. **β-blockers improve psychiatric symptoms** without changing hormone levels (Trzepacz 1988);
- **GAD correlates with the free T4 index; depression does not** (Kathol 1986);
- chorea and athetosis through enhanced **striatal dopaminergic sensitivity**.

**Thyroid and mood disorders [3, 4]:**
- Depression shows **activation of the HPT axis**: ↑CSF TRH, blunted TSH response, ↑fT4 and reverse T3, falling with recovery. T4 falls are greater in responders [4].
- **T3 augmentation** may act by correcting brain hypothyroidism, through 5-HT1A autoreceptor desensitisation, or through 5-HT2 sensitisation [4].
- **Rapid cycling** is associated with low thyroid function. **Supraphysiological T4** reduced episodes (Bauer) [3].

### Clinical features in detail
**Hypothyroidism: neuropsychiatric progression [3]:**
1. Non-specific: poor concentration, **bradyphrenia**, calculation difficulty, poor grasp of complex questions, early registration memory deficit.
2. Psychomotor retardation; **fatiguability**; slowness in routine tasks; reduced responsiveness to others; perseveration.
3. **Perceptual disturbance**: illusions, then visual (or other) hallucinations, then **paranoid ideas**.
4. Drowsiness, lethargy, difficulty rousing, leading to **myxoedema coma**.
- Subjective awareness early, which may be mistaken for depression. **Irritability** and sometimes agitation or aggression [3].
- **The unifying feature of thyroid psychoses is paranoid colouring** (Asher) [3].
- **Distinguishing from schizophrenia:** visual hallucinations with **persistent cognitive disturbance** are rare in schizophrenia [3].

**Neurological accompaniments of hypothyroidism [3]:**
- **slow-relaxing ankle reflexes** (not specific);
- myopathy (weakness may persist after therapy);
- sensory axonal neuropathy; carpal tunnel syndrome;
- **cerebellar ataxia** (reversible);
- seizures, syncope, confusional episodes;
- stroke and TIA (coagulopathy, vascular risk);
- **sleep apnoea**.

**Myxoedema coma [3]:**
- precipitated by **infection, surgery, trauma** and **sedatives**;
- **hypothermia** (use a low-reading thermometer), slow respiration, cardiac failure, arrhythmia;
- high mortality.

**Neuropsychology (Table 10.3, Dugbartey 1998) [3]:**
- general intelligence is not affected;
- **attention** improves at 5–7 months;
- **memory** improves, or treatment prevents further decline;
- visuospatial function partly improves;
- **expressive language (fluency) does not improve**;
- executive function shows no change.

**Hyperthyroidism [3, 4]:**
- **GAD in 80%**; panic; "organic anxiety syndrome";
- **agitated depression** (about one-third met major-depression criteria);
- emotional lability; hyperacuity of perception; over-reaction to noise;
- cognitive impairment (arithmetic, recent memory) often unrecognised by the patient.
- **Psychosis [3]:**
  - Brownlie 2000 (n = 18): **manic 7, depressive 7**, schizophreniform 2, paranoid 1;
  - **paranoid features are common**; a manic tint to schizophrenic pictures; agitation with depression.
- **Thyroid storm:** delirium with fever, now rare [3]. Precipitated by illness, injury, stopping antithyroid drugs, or radioiodine [4].
- **Neurological [3]:**
  - fine **8–10 Hz** tremor (peripheral β-receptors);
  - proximal myopathy;
  - brisk reflexes;
  - **myasthenia gravis association**;
  - **thyrotoxic periodic paralysis** (hypokalaemic; commoner in Asian men [general knowledge]);
  - seizures;
  - EEG changes in 50%.

**Treatment-related syndromes [3]:**
- **Thyroxine-induced psychosis:** usually **mania**, within **4–7 days** of starting. It resolves in 1–2 weeks and recovery is complete. It is more likely with a personal or family psychiatric history (Josephson and Mackenzie: 18 cases, 12 manic).
- **Psychosis during antithyroid treatment** as levels fall: with transient hypothyroidism or even when euthyroid [3].

**Hashimoto encephalopathy (SREAT)** [general knowledge]:
- subacute **relapsing–remitting encephalopathy**, stroke-like episodes, **seizures, myoclonus, tremor**, psychosis, depression, cognitive decline;
- **very high anti-TPO**; raised CSF protein; EEG slowing; MRI often normal;
- **dramatic response to corticosteroids** (IV methylprednisolone, then oral taper);
- an **autoimmune encephalitis** differential: anti-TPO is a marker, not the cause;
- Lishman lists it among causes of rapidly progressive dementia [3].

### Assessment
**When to test thyroid function in psychiatry [1, 3, 4]:**
- **all new presentations** of depression, anxiety, mania, psychosis and cognitive impairment (case-finding);
- **refractory depression**; **rapid cycling**;
- **before and during lithium**: baseline TSH (with antibodies if possible), then 6-monthly [1];
- **postpartum depression**: TSH, fT4 and anti-TPO (American Thyroid Association) [4];
- **past thyroidectomy, radioiodine or thyroid medication** (prompted investigation in 5 of 18 patients in Tonks 1964) [3];
- **elderly with apathy, weight loss or atrial fibrillation** (apathetic hyperthyroidism) [3];
- **neck surgery** history: also check **calcium** (hypoparathyroidism) [3].

**Interpretation pitfalls [3, 4]:**
- **acute psychiatric admission** can transiently raise T4 [3];
- **non-thyroidal illness** [1, 4];
- **oestrogen** raises TBG and total T4, so use **free** hormones [4];
- **dopamine agonists and glucocorticoids** lower TSH [4];
- **biotin** interferes with immunoassays [general knowledge];
- **lithium** causes goitre and hypothyroidism, and rarely thyroiditis or hyperthyroidism [1, 4];
- **carbamazepine** lowers T4 without raising TSH; **quetiapine** lowers fT4 [general knowledge].

**Wayne index: clinical discriminators of hyperthyroidism vs anxiety [3]:**
- **symptoms**, in order: heat sensitivity and preference for cold; increased appetite; weight loss; sweating; palpitations; tiredness; nervousness; dyspnoea;
- **signs**, in order: **AF**; hyperkinetic movements; pulse >90; palpable goitre; thyroid bruit; exophthalmos; lid retraction; hot hands; lid lag; fine tremor.
- **The most decisive single pointer is increased appetite despite weight loss** [3].
- Anxiety favours: younger onset, more psychological precipitants, panic, neurotic personality (Gurney 1967) [3].

### Differential diagnosis
- **Hypothyroidism vs depression:** retardation, poor concentration and reduced responsiveness overlap. **Physical signs** (puffy face, cold intolerance, slow reflexes, hoarse voice, constipation) and **cognitive deficits** favour hypothyroidism [3].
- **Hypothyroid psychosis vs schizophrenia:** visual hallucinations, cognitive impairment, clouding and physical stigmata [3].
- **Hypothyroid dementia** vs Alzheimer's and other dementias: reversible if treated early [3].
- **Hyperthyroidism vs:**
  - **anxiety disorders** [3, 4];
  - **mania**: Lishman's case had recurrent "hypomania" with overlooked tachycardia [3];
  - **alcohol withdrawal** (tremulousness and lability wrongly blamed on alcohol) [3];
  - phaeochromocytoma, hypoglycaemia, stimulants, cocaine [4].
- **Apathetic hyperthyroidism vs depression or occult malignancy** in the elderly [3].
- **Hypo- or hyperparathyroidism** after thyroid surgery: tetany, anxiety, depression; hypercalcaemic depression and confusion [3].

### Management details
**Hypothyroidism [3]:**
- **Levothyroxine:**
  - start **≤50 µg/day**, lower in the elderly or cardiac patients (25 µg) [3; general knowledge for 25 µg];
  - increase by **25–50 µg every 2–3 weeks** to **100–150 µg/day** [3];
  - target TSH in the normal range.
- **Psychotropic support** initially:
  - antidepressants or antipsychotics as needed;
  - **ECT** in severe psychotic depression without organic features [3];
  - **phenothiazines carry a small risk of precipitating hypothermic coma** [3].
- **Watch for thyroxine-induced mania** in the first week [3].
- **Response predictors [3]:**
  - organic psychoses (disorientation, confusion) respond better than "functional" psychoses;
  - **duration over 2 years predicted poor response to thyroid alone** (Tonks: 18 patients);
  - long untreated dementia may be irreversible (Jellinek).
- **Residual symptoms despite normal TSH:**
  - 26% excess GHQ caseness (Saravanan 2002);
  - **T3/T4 combination** trials are inconsistent, after initial positive results (Bunevicius 1999) [3];
  - possibly helpful in the absence of thyroid tissue or with **DIO2 polymorphism** [1; general knowledge].
- **Subclinical hypothyroidism (Surks 2004 consensus) [3]:**
  - **TSH >10: treat**;
  - **TSH <10: treat only if symptomatic** (a several-month trial; continue only with clear benefit);
  - monitor TSH every 6–12 months;
  - **pregnancy: expert advice** (treat).

**Hyperthyroidism [3, 4]:**
- **β-blockers** for rapid symptomatic relief (propranolol, atenolol, nadolol) [4];
- **antithyroid drugs**:
  - methimazole 10–40 mg/day then 5–10 mg maintenance; PTU 75–100 mg three times daily [4];
  - **agranulocytosis** risk;
  - remission about 30% after 6 months of treatment vs about 80% after 2 years [4];
- **radioiodine** (hypothyroidism common afterwards) [4];
- **surgery** (pre-treat to prevent storm; hypoparathyroidism risk) [4];
- **psychiatric symptoms usually resolve with euthyroidism** (Kathol) [3];
- **antipsychotics:** avoid **low-potency** agents (tachycardia) and **QT-prolonging** drugs. Use **TCAs cautiously, if at all** [4];
- **lithium** is used as a radioiodine adjunct [1].

**Lithium-related thyroid disease [1, 4]:**
- **continue lithium and add levothyroxine** for overt hypothyroidism or SCH with TSH >10 [1];
- check **calcium** (hyperparathyroidism) [1];
- lithium thyrotoxicosis (painless thyroiditis) is rare.

**Thyroid augmentation in depression and bipolar disorder [3, 4; general knowledge]:**
- **T3 25–50 µg** augmentation of antidepressants;
  - TCA-era meta-analysis (Aronson 1996) positive;
  - **STAR*D level 3: T3 ≈ lithium** (remission about 25% vs 16%; T3 better tolerated);
- **high-dose T4 (250–500 µg)** in **rapid-cycling** and refractory bipolar depression (Bauer) [3];
- monitor bone density and cardiac rhythm with supraphysiological doses.

### Critical appraisal
- Classic descriptions come from **pre-laboratory, severe, untreated** cases. Modern presentations are milder [3].
- **SCH literature is inconsistent**: differing TSH cut-offs, age effects and ascertainment bias [1, 3].
- **Causality vs coincidence** in thyroid psychoses [3].
- The **antibody-positive euthyroid** depression link is inconsistent [4].
- **SREAT** is controversial: anti-TPO is common in the general population, so it may be over-diagnosed [general knowledge].
- K&S quotes "normal TSH 0.3–4.7 mIU/L" (garbled in the extract). Use local laboratory ranges [4].

### Indian context [general knowledge]
- **High prevalence of hypothyroidism and SCH**, especially in women and pregnancy; universal salt iodisation since 1992.
- **T3 (liothyronine) availability is limited in India** [1].
- **Lithium monitoring:** TSH every 6 months, often missed in community practice [1].
- **Postpartum thyroiditis screening** in postpartum depression is rarely done. Integrating it into perinatal psychiatry is recommended [4].
- The **IPS CPG (2022)** endorses case-finding in psychiatric disorders [1].

### Recent advances [general knowledge]
- **ATA/ETA guidance (2021)**: limited role for T3/T4 combination in persistent symptoms.
- **TRUST trial (2017)**: levothyroxine for SCH in older adults gave **no benefit** in symptoms or fatigue.
- **DIO2 Thr92Ala polymorphism** as a possible predictor of combination-therapy benefit.
- **Thyroid eye disease:** teprotumumab; psychological morbidity of orbitopathy.
- **Autoimmune encephalitis panels** now distinguish SREAT from antibody-defined encephalitides.
