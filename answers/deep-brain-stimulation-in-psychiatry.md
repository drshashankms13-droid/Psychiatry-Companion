## Exam answer

**Definition.** **Deep brain stimulation (DBS)** is an **invasive, reversible, adjustable** neuromodulation technique. Electrodes are implanted stereotactically into specific deep brain targets and connected to a subcutaneous **implantable pulse generator (IPG)**, which delivers continuous high-frequency electrical stimulation.

- It was developed for movement disorders (Parkinson's disease, essential tremor, dystonia).
- It is now used in carefully selected patients with **severe, chronic, treatment-refractory OCD, depression and Tourette syndrome**.
- It is the reversible successor to ablative psychosurgery [1].

### Mechanism
- High-frequency stimulation (about 100–185 Hz) of a network hub has several effects:
  - **functional inhibition or "jamming"** of local abnormal activity (depolarisation block, synaptic depression);
  - activation of passing white-matter fibres, which changes activity across the whole circuit;
  - downstream neurochemical and plastic changes [1].
- **Network model:** psychiatric disorders are treated as dysfunctional circuits [1]:
  - **OCD:** hyperactive cortico-striato-thalamo-cortical (CSTC) loops, i.e. orbitofrontal cortex/ACC → ventral striatum → thalamus.
  - **Depression:** fronto-limbic hyperactivity, including **subcallosal cingulate (Brodmann area 25)**, which is hyperactive in sadness and normalises with antidepressants, CBT and rTMS; reward-circuit hypofunction (nucleus accumbens, medial forebrain bundle).
  - DBS is thought to **inhibit or override fronto-limbic hyperactivity** [1].

### Procedure (three stages) [1]
1. **Image-guided stereotactic insertion** of electrodes (about 1 mm; usually 4 contacts per side), often under local anaesthesia, with intraoperative macrostimulation to watch for adverse effects.
2. **Subdermal implantation of the IPG** in the chest, with a replaceable or rechargeable battery.
3. **Programming:** polarity, amplitude, frequency and pulse width are adjusted over months.

### Targets and evidence

| Disorder | Targets | Key evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **OCD** | **Ventral capsule/ventral striatum (VC/VS)**, anterior limb of internal capsule (ALIC), nucleus accumbens, bed nucleus of stria terminalis, **anteromedial subthalamic nucleus (STN)**; also inferior thalamic peduncle, medial forebrain bundle (MFB) | Alonso 2015 meta-analysis (17 studies in the pooled analysis, n=116): **~60% responders** (≥35% Y-BOCS fall), mean Y-BOCS reduction ~45%. Class I evidence for STN (Mallet 2008); class II for NAcc [1] |
| **Treatment-resistant depression** | **Subcallosal cingulate (SCC/Cg25)**, VC/VS, **medial forebrain bundle**, NAcc | Kisely 2018 meta-analysis (9 sham-controlled studies, n=190): overall OR ~5.5 favouring active; MFB strongest. **BROADEN (SCC, n=90)** and **RECLAIM (VC/VS, n=30)** industry trials stopped for futility [1] |
| **Tourette syndrome** | **Centromedian-parafascicular (CM-Pf) thalamus**, globus pallidus interna, ALIC/NAcc | Meta-analysis of 57 studies (n=153): ~53% reduction in YGTSS; ON vs OFF SMD ~0.96 [1] |
| Others (investigational) | Anorexia nervosa (SCC, NAcc), addiction (NAcc), schizophrenia, Alzheimer's (fornix) | Case series / pilot RCTs |

### Patient selection and assessment
**Indian guidelines** (Doshi et al. 2019) [1]:
- **Who:** adults with **chronic, severe, treatment-refractory** OCD, depression or Tourette syndrome, after failure of adequate trials of medication, evidence-based psychotherapy (ERP/CBT) and, for depression, ECT.
- **Team:** a multidisciplinary team with functional neurosurgery expertise and an independent oversight committee.
- **Follow-up:** at least 1 year, with standard treatments continued.
- **Consent:** capacity-based consent, including discussion of uncertainty.
- **Pre-operative work-up:** standardised severity scales (Y-BOCS, MADRS/HAM-D, YGTSS), MRI, neuropsychology, medical fitness, and exclusion of psychosis, active substance misuse and severe personality disorder.

### Management and safety
- **Surgical risks:** intracranial haemorrhage (about 1–2%), infection (about 3–5%), seizures, lead migration or fracture, battery depletion.
- **Stimulation-related effects** (usually reversible by reprogramming): hypomania, anxiety, mood swings, impulsivity, sleep change, paraesthesia, dysarthria.
- **Battery-depletion emergency:** abrupt worsening of OCD or depression, with suicide risk.
- **Combined care:** DBS is **not stand-alone**. ERP, medication and psychosocial care continue [1].
- **Law:** in India, psychosurgery needs **informed consent plus Mental Health Review Board approval** (MHCA 2017, Section 96) [1].

### Advantages and disadvantages [1]

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
| --- | --- |
| Reversible; adjustable; sham stimulation possible (enables RCTs); flexible targets; side effects often reversible | High cost; battery replacement; burr holes (open procedure); needs a skilled interdisciplinary team and intensive follow-up; not superior in efficacy to ablation |

### Key evidence
- **OCD:**
  - Greenberg 2010 (VC/VS, n=26): about 60% response.
  - Mallet 2008 (STN, NEJM, n=16): significant Y-BOCS reduction vs sham.
  - FDA Humanitarian Device Exemption for ALIC DBS in OCD in 2009; CE mark in Europe in 2009 [1].
- **Depression:**
  - Mayberg 2005 (SCC, n=6): proof of concept.
  - BROADEN 2017 and RECLAIM 2015: negative.
  - **Bergfeld 2016** (VC/VS, with long open optimisation followed by randomised crossover): active stimulation significantly better than sham [1].

**Conclusion.** DBS offers reversible, adjustable circuit-level treatment. It is a reasonable option for a small group of patients with **severe, refractory OCD** (about 60% respond) and Tourette syndrome. For depression it remains **investigational**, because large RCTs have been inconsistent. It should be offered only in specialist centres with multidisciplinary oversight, as part of comprehensive ongoing care, and in India only with MHRB approval.

## In depth

### Background
- **Ablative psychosurgery:** Moniz's prefrontal leucotomy (1935; Nobel Prize 1949), Freeman's transorbital lobotomy and its abuse, then the 1960s shift to precise **stereotactic lesions** (cingulotomy, capsulotomy, subcaudate tractotomy, limbic leucotomy) [1].
- **DBS in movement disorders:** Benabid (Grenoble, 1987) for tremor; later Parkinson's disease (STN, GPi).
- **First psychiatric DBS:** Nuttin et al. 1999 (ALIC, OCD), modelled on capsulotomy. Mallet noticed OCD improvement in Parkinson's patients with STN DBS.
- **Depression:** Mayberg et al. 2005 (SCC/Cg25), followed by VC/VS, NAcc (Schlaepfer 2008) and MFB (Schlaepfer 2013) [1].
- **Late 2010s:** circuitry-based (tractography/connectomic) targeting and **closed-loop (adaptive) DBS** [1].

### Epidemiology of use
- More than 200,000 people worldwide have DBS, nearly all for movement disorders. Psychiatric DBS involves a few hundred to low thousands of patients.
- **Refractory OCD:** about 10% of OCD patients remain severely ill after multiple treatments, so only a fraction are candidates.
- **India:** a few centres (for example, Mumbai and NIMHANS collaborations) have performed DBS or capsulotomy for OCD. Numbers are small [1].

### Classification and criteria
**Refractoriness definitions:**
- **OCD:**
  - Y-BOCS ≥28 (or ≥26 with severe impairment) for at least 5 years.
  - Failure of at least 2–3 SRIs at maximum doses (including clomipramine).
  - Failure of antipsychotic augmentation.
  - At least 20 sessions of ERP.
- **Depression:** failure of at least 4 adequate treatments, including ECT, and illness duration of at least 2 years.

**Guidelines:**
- **WSSFN/ASSFN consensus (Nuttin 2014):** ablative procedures have only level II evidence. Neurosurgery is one part of a comprehensive programme and is symptomatic, not curative [1].
- **ASSFN/CNS guideline (Hamani 2014):** level I evidence for bilateral STN DBS in OCD; level II for bilateral NAcc DBS [1].

### Mechanism in detail
| Hypothesis | Explanation |
| --- | --- |
| Depolarisation block / "functional lesion" | HFS silences local neurons, mimicking ablation (explains similarity to capsulotomy) |
| Synaptic depression | Neurotransmitter depletion at stimulated synapses |
| Axonal activation | Orthodromic and antidromic activation of fibres (e.g., hyperdirect pathway from OFC to STN) |
| Network "jamming" | Replaces pathological oscillations (e.g., beta/theta) with a regular pattern |
| Long-term plasticity | Delayed effects over months: neurogenesis, BDNF, network reorganisation |

**Connectomic insight:** response in OCD correlates with stimulation of a specific fibre bundle from ventral prefrontal cortex to STN, regardless of the nominal target (Li et al. 2020). This supports **circuit-based** rather than nucleus-based targeting [1].

### Clinical features in detail: course after DBS
- **OCD:** mood and anxiety may lift within days ("acute mood effect"). Obsessions and compulsions improve gradually over 3–12 months. ERP is easier to engage in after DBS.
- **Depression:** delayed and variable response; longer optimisation periods improve outcome (Bergfeld 2016) [1].
- **Stimulation-induced hypomania:** commoner with VC/VS and STN; settles with parameter change.
- **Tourette syndrome:** tics fall by about half on average; ages 18 and above preferred because tics often remit naturally in adolescence.

### Assessment in detail
| Domain | Tool |
| --- | --- |
| OCD severity | Y-BOCS (response ≥35% reduction) |
| Depression | MADRS/HAM-D (response ≥50%) |
| Tourette | YGTSS |
| Function / QoL | GAF, SF-36 |
| Neuropsychology | Baseline and follow-up (executive, memory) |
| Imaging | MRI with tractography for targeting |
| Capacity and expectations | Structured consent; independent ethics review |

### Differential: DBS vs alternatives for refractory illness
| Option | Invasiveness | Reversibility | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Augmentation strategies / intensive ERP | None | — | First step |
| rTMS / deep TMS (OCD: dTMS FDA-cleared 2018) | Non-invasive | Yes | Moderate [1] |
| ECT (depression) | Non-invasive, seizurogenic | — | Strong |
| VNS (depression) | Invasive (neck) | Yes | Long-term open data [1] |
| Ablation (capsulotomy, cingulotomy; gamma knife, MRgFUS) | Lesional | No | Level II; similar efficacy to DBS, lower cost [1] |
| **DBS** | Invasive (cranial) | **Yes** | OCD ~60% response; depression mixed |

### Management details
- **Programming:** start with low amplitude (e.g., 2–4 V, 90–130 Hz, 90–210 μs). Titrate over weeks. Choose contacts based on acute effects and side effects. **Constant-current vs constant-voltage** devices affect dosing.
- **Batteries:** high-amplitude psychiatric settings drain batteries quickly (every 1–2 years). Rechargeable IPGs reduce surgery but need adherence to recharging. Battery failure can cause severe rebound.
- **Continuing care:** keep medication and ERP or psychotherapy going; arrange suicide-risk monitoring; manage expectations.
- **Contraindications:** psychosis, current substance dependence, severe personality disorder, inability to consent or follow up, and medical contraindications to surgery. Caution with MRI after implantation (MR-conditional devices only).

### Critical appraisal
- **Small, highly selected samples;** most data are open-label case series. Publication bias is likely.
- **The depression RCT paradox:** large industry trials failed while open studies were positive. Possible reasons [1]:
  - The fixed-parameter design did not allow flexible titration.
  - The optimisation phase was too short.
  - Effects are delayed.
  - Targeting was imprecise without individual connectomics.
  - Patients were heterogeneous.
- **Blinding:** stimulation side effects (hypomania, paraesthesia) can unblind patients. Placebo response to surgery is high.
- **Ethics:** history of psychosurgery abuse; personality or identity change concerns; consent in desperate, chronically ill patients; commercial influence; access inequity because of cost (several lakh rupees, about US$30,000–50,000).
- **Efficacy vs ablation:** DBS is probably not superior to capsulotomy for OCD. Ablation is cheaper but irreversible [1].

### Indian context
- **MHCA 2017, Section 96:** psychosurgery (including DBS when used as treatment for mental illness) requires **informed consent and MHRB approval**. Treatment of persons who cannot consent is effectively barred [1].
- **Indian guidelines on neurosurgical interventions** (Doshi, Arumugham, Reddy et al., *Indian J Psychiatry* 2019): indications, targets, team, follow-up and consent [1].
- **Cost and access:** devices cost several lakh rupees, with limited insurance coverage. Few centres combine functional neurosurgery with psychiatric expertise.
- **Indian experience:** case series of capsulotomy and DBS for OCD at specialist centres, with outcomes broadly similar to international data.

### Recent advances
- **Established:** connectomic and tractography-guided targeting; FDA HDE for OCD [1].
- **Emerging:**
  - **Closed-loop (adaptive) DBS:** sensing biomarkers and stimulating on demand. Scangos 2021 reported a single depression case.
  - Directional leads.
  - Rechargeable IPGs.
  - Individual network targeting (the "OCD tract").
- **Investigational:** DBS for anorexia nervosa, addiction, PTSD, and Alzheimer's disease (fornix, ADvance trials); **MRgFUS** ablation as an incisionless alternative [1].
