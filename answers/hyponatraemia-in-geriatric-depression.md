## Exam answer

### Introduction [1, 2]
- **Definition:** hyponatraemia is serum sodium **below 135 mmol/L** [1]. The normal range is 136–145 mmol/L [2].
- **Severity:** mild 130–134; **moderate 125–129**; **severe <125 mmol/L**. Clinically relevant hyponatraemia is mainly moderate or severe, though mild cases can be symptomatic [1].
- **Burden:**
  - hospitalisation with hyponatraemia rises from **1 in 1,600** in the general population to **1 in 300** on any antidepressant [2];
  - hyponatraemia of **all severities** is associated with increased mortality [2];
  - in hospital it brings longer stays and referral to long-term care [1].
- **Older patients:** particularly prone to hyponatraemia when starting SSRIs and most other antidepressants, alongside postural hypotension and falls [2, p688].

### Mechanism [1, 2]
- **SIADH** (syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion) is the most likely mechanism [2].
- **SERT affinity:** the fall in sodium correlates with **serotonin-transporter (SERT) binding affinity**. SSRIs and SNRIs lowered sodium by about 1 mmol/L on average. Clinical hyponatraemia may be the **extreme of a normal distribution** of effect rather than an idiosyncratic reaction [1, 2].
- **Mirtazapine** has the lowest SERT affinity [1].

### Risk by antidepressant [1, 2]

**Meta-analysis (39 studies, n = 8.2 million)** [1]:
- **Overall risk:** antidepressant exposure roughly **triples the odds** of hyponatraemia (OR 3.16).
- **Event rates:**
  - **SNRIs 7.44%**;
  - **SSRIs 5.59%**;
  - TCAs 2.66%;
  - **mirtazapine 1.02%**;
  - **trazodone 0.89%**.
- **Head-to-head vs SSRIs:**
  - **SNRIs higher** (OR 1.29);
  - **mirtazapine lower** (OR 0.61; for clinically relevant hyponatraemia OR 0.51);
  - TCAs and trazodone not significantly different.
- **Geriatric subgroup:**
  - mirtazapine remained significantly safer (OR 0.57; clinically relevant 0.47);
  - SNRIs showed a non-significant trend to higher risk (OR 1.31).
- **Bupropion:** only one study, with no excess risk. It may be an option for older adults.
- **Onset:** venlafaxine may cause hyponatraemia sooner (mean 4 days) than SSRIs (9–24 days).

**Maudsley Table 3.14** [2]:

| Drug | Risk | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| SNRIs | High | Strong |
| SSRIs | High | Strong |
| Tricyclics | Moderate | Strong |
| MAOIs | Low | Weak |
| Mirtazapine, mianserin | Low | Strong |
| Trazodone | Low | Strong |
| Bupropion | Low | Moderate |
| Agomelatine | Low | Moderate |

- **No antidepressant is risk-free**, including vortioxetine, desvenlafaxine and vilazodone [1, 2].
- **Older adults:**
  - agomelatine has not been linked to hyponatraemia in older patients (although one database study found an association);
  - lofepramine is less likely to cause it than other TCAs and SSRIs [2, pp416, 688–689].

### Risk factors (Maudsley Box 3.4) [2]
- **Older age:** perhaps the most important. **Monitoring is essential in older people, especially women.**
- **Female sex.**
- Low body weight; **frailty**.
- History of hyponatraemia or low baseline sodium.
- **Co-prescribed sodium-lowering drugs:**
  - **diuretics** (especially thiazides);
  - NSAIDs;
  - antipsychotics;
  - **carbamazepine**;
  - chemotherapy;
  - calcium antagonists;
  - ACE inhibitors;
  - laxatives;
  - opiates, tramadol, omeprazole, trimethoprim.
- **Reduced renal function** (GFR below 50 mL/min).
- **Medical comorbidity:** hypothyroidism, diabetes, COPD, hypertension, head injury, heart failure, stroke, cancers.
- Major surgery.
- Possibly CYP2D6 poor metabolisers.

### Clinical features [1, 2]
- **Mild to moderate:**
  - nausea, vomiting, headache;
  - **dizziness, lethargy, fatigue**;
  - **confusion, disorientation**, irritability, restlessness;
  - muscle weakness, cramps or spasms.
- **Severe:** **seizures, coma, respiratory arrest**.

### Evaluation [1, 2]
1. **Identify high-risk patients before starting.** Use the risk factors above: older age, female sex, diuretics, low sodium, renal impairment.
2. **Baseline serum sodium** in high-risk patients.
3. **Timing:**
   - risk is greatest in the **first 4 weeks** (onset usually within 30 days);
   - it declines to background by **3–6 months**;
   - the effect is not clearly dose-related [2].
4. **Monitoring schedule for high-risk patients** [2]:
   - sodium at **baseline, 2 weeks and 4 weeks, then every 3 months**;
   - sodium may fall precipitously between tests.
   - The meta-analysis advises monitoring for **2–4 weeks after starting** any antidepressant in hyponatraemia-prone patients, **especially SSRIs and SNRIs** [1].
5. **Educate** patients and carers about symptoms [2].
6. **New hyponatraemia after months or years of treatment:** assume **other causes** [2].
7. **Review all co-prescribed drugs** that lower sodium [2].

### Management (Maudsley) [2]

| Serum sodium | Action |
| --- | --- |
| **>130 mmol/L (mild)** | Fluid restriction may suffice (increasing salt intake is often impractical). **Stop the antidepressant if symptoms persist** |
| **>125 mmol/L** | **Monitor sodium daily until normal**; withdraw the antidepressant as soon as possible |
| **<125 mmol/L** | **Refer urgently to specialist medical care**; **stop the antidepressant immediately**. Risk of seizures, coma and respiratory arrest. **Over-rapid correction may be harmful** |

**Restarting antidepressant treatment** [2]:
- **Recurrence:** hyponatraemia may recur with the same or another SSRI; it is less likely with another class.
- **Other drugs:** withdraw other sodium-lowering drugs, because risk rises **exponentially** with diuretics and similar combinations.
- **Switch class:** choose **mirtazapine**, a noradrenergic TCA (**nortriptyline, lofepramine**), **moclobemide**, **agomelatine** or **bupropion**. Start low, go slow, and monitor closely.
- **If it recurs and an antidepressant is essential:** water restriction and/or careful **demeclocycline**.
- **If no standard antidepressant can be given:** consider **ECT or (es)ketamine**.

### Choosing an antidepressant in an older patient at risk [1, 2]
- **Mirtazapine** is the treatment of choice when hyponatraemia risk dominates [1].
- **SNRIs** should be prescribed more cautiously than SSRIs and TCAs, especially in the elderly [1].
- **Balance other harms in older people** [2]:
  - SSRIs carry the highest risk of falls and hyponatraemia;
  - "other antidepressants" (venlafaxine, mirtazapine) carry higher risks of stroke, fracture, seizures and mortality in one observational study;
  - TCAs cause anticholinergic effects, postural hypotension and overdose toxicity.

**Conclusion.** Antidepressant-induced hyponatraemia (mainly SIADH) is common and dangerous in older people, especially frail women on diuretics, and it typically appears within the first month. SNRIs and SSRIs carry the highest risk, and mirtazapine, trazodone, bupropion and agomelatine the lowest. Manage it in four steps:
1. identify risk and check sodium at baseline, 2 and 4 weeks;
2. stop the drug and grade the response by sodium level, referring urgently if below 125 mmol/L and avoiding over-rapid correction;
3. restart with a different class (mirtazapine first);
4. consider ECT or esketamine if antidepressants cannot be used.

## In depth

### Critical appraisal (from the sources)
- **Heterogeneity** [1]: high, with different sodium cut-offs, settings and designs.
- **Generalisability** [1]: most participants were older or hospital-based, which limits application to younger, healthier patients.
- **Combination therapy** [1]: unclear whether patients took one or several antidepressants. This may inflate rates for add-on drugs (mirtazapine, mianserin, bupropion, trazodone).
- **MAOI data** [1]: mostly moclobemide; not representative of irreversible MAOIs.
- **Database pitfalls** [2]: reporting bias, confounding by indication (low-risk drugs given to high-risk patients) and co-prescription. An FDA database study found mirtazapine most associated, and a French one duloxetine, in contrast to the meta-analysis.
