## Exam answer

**Introduction.**
- Dissociative (psychogenic non-epileptic) seizures and epilepsy **commonly coexist**:
  - **about 1 in 5** people with functional seizures also have epilepsy [2];
  - 7–32% [3];
  - prior epilepsy in fewer than 20% [1].
- **Neurological disease producing similar symptoms is a risk factor** for FND [2].
- **Lishman:** "the most difficult group to treat is undoubtedly those patients with coexistent epilepsy" [1].

### Clinical spectrum [1, 2, 3]
1. **Epilepsy first, dissociative seizures added later** (fewer than 20% of dissociative-seizure patients overall [1]). **Modelling** on one's own or a relative's seizures is a predisposing factor; the patient's experience of epilepsy shapes the attacks.
2. **Dissociative seizures first, epilepsy later** (or epilepsy recognised later).
3. **Epileptic seizure triggering a dissociative "elaboration"**: a partial epileptic seizure followed by a non-epileptic continuation (Kapur 1995) [1].
4. **Learning disability:** higher rates of both, and interictal EEG abnormalities that confuse the picture [1].
5. **Post-surgical:** comorbid dissociative seizures **almost contraindicate epilepsy surgery**; they persist "with disastrous consequences" [1].

**Consequences of missing the dual diagnosis:**
- **if dissociative attacks are missed:**
  - escalating ASMs (toxicity, polypharmacy-related psychiatric effects);
  - emergency treatment for "status" (up to 10%);
  - failed surgery;
- **if epilepsy is missed** (everything labelled "functional"):
  - untreated seizures, injury, **SUDEP** risk [1].

### Evaluation [1, 2]
1. **Establish every attack type separately.** Ask how many types there are, and the sequence, duration and consistency of each. Informants and **home video** are essential.
2. **Video-EEG telemetry:**
   - **document each attack type** before concluding the patient does or does not have epilepsy;
   - **verify that captured attacks are typical** (with informants);
   - repeat telemetry when semiology changes or new attack types appear;
   - interictal EEG abnormalities do **not** prove that a given attack is epileptic;
   - non-specific abnormalities are over-represented in dissociative-seizure patients.
3. **Use semiology** (see the companion answer) for each attack type. **No single feature** is diagnostic.
4. **Prolactin** after an apparent GTCS: a normal result is useful; a raised result is not conclusive.
5. **Provocation by suggestion** (disclosed) can capture dissociative attacks. **An informant must confirm** they resemble the habitual ones.
6. **ASM withdrawal during telemetry** may be used to capture or unmask epileptic seizures.
7. **Psychiatric assessment:**
   - predisposing, precipitating and maintaining factors;
   - comorbid depression and anxiety;
   - trauma;
   - **"seizure phobia"** and **benzodiazepine misuse** (rescue medication taken for "prodromes" or anxiety);
   - **suicide risk** (raised in both epilepsy and FND).
8. **Review ASMs** for psychiatric adverse effects (levetiracetam, topiramate) and polypharmacy.

### Management [1, 2]
1. **Explain both diagnoses clearly**, together (neurologist and psychiatrist).
   - **Show telemetry videos** of each attack type to patient and carers ("invaluable").
   - Acknowledge that both are real; the patient is not feigning.
2. **Optimise epilepsy treatment** for the epileptic seizures only.
   - **Rationalise ASMs**; avoid escalation for non-epileptic attacks.
   - Watch for misuse of rescue benzodiazepines, taken for supposed "prodromes" or as self-medication for anxiety [1].
3. **Psychological therapy** for the dissociative attacks:
   - CBT (modelled on panic or PTSD) or psychodynamic approaches;
   - CBT for **seizure phobia** (avoidance, safety behaviours) [1].
4. **Treat comorbid depression or anxiety.** SSRIs are safe in epilepsy and ECT is not contraindicated [1].
5. **Carer education:** recognising each attack type and responding appropriately.
6. **Surgery:** exclude comorbid dissociative seizures before temporal lobectomy [1].
7. **Long-term pragmatic follow-up:**
   - many continue to have attacks despite intensive treatment;
   - offer **ongoing support** to patient and family;
   - **limit unnecessary investigations and interventions** [1].
8. **Monitor** for change in semiology (new attack types), and re-investigate if needed.

**Prognosis:**
- In dissociative seizures overall, two-thirds persist beyond 3 years. **Short duration before diagnosis** is the main favourable factor [1].
- Comorbid physical disease and maladaptive personality traits are **negative prognostic factors** [2].

**Conclusion.** Comorbid epilepsy and dissociative seizures are common (about 1 in 5 of those with functional seizures). They pose the hardest diagnostic and therapeutic problem in this field. Management requires:
- **separate characterisation of each attack type** (informant video, telemetry of every type, checking typicality);
- clear joint communication;
- **ASMs only for epileptic attacks**;
- psychological therapy for dissociative attacks and seizure phobia;
- treatment of comorbidity;
- careful surgical selection;
- pragmatic long-term follow-up to avoid iatrogenic harm.
