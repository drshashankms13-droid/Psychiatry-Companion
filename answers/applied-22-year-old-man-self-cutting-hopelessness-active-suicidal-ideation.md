## Exam answer

**Summary.**
- A **22-year-old unmarried male college student** has had **low mood**, inability to maintain friendships and **repeated self-cutting with a blade for 2 years**.
- **Frequent anger outbursts** and conflict with parents.
- **Irregular** with treatment and psychotherapy.
- Says "**everything will end soon**".
- **Appears euthymic** but expresses **hopelessness, worthlessness** and **active suicidal ideation**.

### Formulation and differential diagnosis
- **Most likely:** a **borderline (emotionally unstable) pattern of personality**:
  - chronic self-harm;
  - unstable relationships;
  - anger dyscontrol;
  - chronic low mood and hopelessness;
  - onset in late adolescence.
- BPD is common in psychiatric settings and has high comorbidity [1].
- **Look for comorbid conditions**, which must be treated "irrespective of any coexisting BPD diagnosis" [1]:
  - **major depressive disorder** (hopelessness and worthlessness despite a euthymic appearance);
  - **bipolar disorder**;
  - **substance use**;
  - anxiety disorders, PTSD, eating disorders.

### Risk assessment: the priority
**Why risk is HIGH [1, 2]:**
- **Active suicidal ideation** with **hopelessness**. Hopelessness is a key risk factor and is central to the three-step and cognitive models.
- An **ominous statement**: "everything will end soon".
- **Two years of self-harm.** More than **75% of people with BPD attempt suicide** and **2–5% die** [1].
- **Young adult male:** greater impulsivity and **more lethal methods** [2].
- **Poor treatment engagement**, **anger and impulsivity**, and **family conflict** [2].
- **Euthymic appearance does not reassure.** The clinician must rely on **clinical inquiry**, because standardised risk scales "are not predictive of risk" [2].

**Ask in detail [2]:**
- When the thoughts began, what led to them, how often they come;
- **a specific plan**, preparations, access to means;
- whether he has told anyone;
- past attempts: what happened, what he expected, whether he sought help, how he felt afterwards;
- the **difference between self-cutting and an attempt to die**. Both can occur in the same person [3].

**Also assess:** substance use; financial, academic and legal stress; coping skills; **protective factors** (connectedness, access to care, reasons for living) [2, 4].

**Scales as adjuncts:** C-SSRS, Beck Hopelessness Scale, Suicide Intent Scale, Reasons for Living [2].

### Immediate management
1. **Safety decision:** **admission vs outpatient care** depends on his state, the **quality of family support** and access to qualified outpatient care [2]. With active ideation, hopelessness and a possible plan, **inpatient care or very close supervision** is indicated (application).
2. **Means restriction:** remove blades and other lethal means. Restricted access to means is protective [4].
3. **Acute crisis treatment:** reduce anxiety, insomnia and depression; take anti-suicidal measures; **collaborate with family**, balancing confidentiality [2].
4. **Short-term sedation only if needed, no longer than 1 week** [1]:
   - **avoid benzodiazepines** (disinhibition in BPD) and **TCAs** (toxic in overdose);
   - **promethazine 25–50 mg** is a well-tolerated short-term option.
5. **MHCA 2017:** if he makes an attempt, he is presumed to have **severe stress**; s115 means no prosecution, and the state must provide care.

### Longer-term management
- **Psychological therapy is the core.** DBT and other psychological treatments have **much better evidence** than drugs [1].
- **Treat comorbid depression** (or other disorders) per usual guidance [1].
- **No routine medication for BPD itself** (NICE). Medicines alone are unsupported, but combined with psychotherapy they can improve mood and behaviour [1].
- **If drugs are used** [1]:
  - choose those **safe in overdose**;
  - **anger:** aripiprazole, valproate (male patient), topiramate;
  - **aggression:** quetiapine;
  - **omega-3 fatty acids** as an add-on for impulsivity and affective dysregulation;
  - avoid polypharmacy;
  - **clozapine** if he becomes severely treatment-refractory with high suicide risk.
- **Engagement:** address irregular attendance with a consistent therapist, crisis plan and family work [2] (application).
- **Family:** reduce conflict and involve parents in crisis planning [2].
- **Follow-up:** frequent review; re-assess risk at every contact.

**Conclusion.** This young man probably has borderline personality disorder, possibly with comorbid depression, and is at **high immediate suicide risk** despite looking euthymic. Steps:
1. Detailed clinical risk inquiry.
2. A safe setting (likely admission or close supervision) with means restriction.
3. Brief, safe crisis medication only if needed.
4. Long-term **psychological therapy (e.g. DBT)**, treatment of comorbidity and family involvement.

## In depth

### Theories that fit his presentation (seminar) [2]
| Model | Relevance |
|---|---|
| **Three-step theory** (Klonsky) | Pain plus hopelessness gives ideation; pain exceeding connectedness (lost friendships, family conflict) strengthens it; capability (practised self-cutting) enables an attempt |
| **Interpersonal theory** (Joiner) | Thwarted belongingness plus **acquired capability** from repeated self-harm |
| **Two-stage aggression model** (Apter, Plutchik) | Impulsive aggression directed at self (self-harm) or others (anger outbursts) |
| **Beck cognitive model** | Hopelessness; inability to recall reasons for living |
