## Exam answer

### Introduction [1]
- Migraine affects about **12% a year** (lifetime about 16%; F:M 3:1).
- **Psychological factors** are widely held to influence attacks and treatment.
- Psychiatric comorbidity worsens **quality of life** and treatment response, and raises the risk of **chronic ("transformed") migraine**.

### Mood and anxiety disorders [1]
**Prevalence (Table 8.1, population surveys):** migraineurs have **2–3 times** the risk of depression and anxiety.

| Study | Major depression (migraine vs control) | Anxiety disorder |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Breslau 1991 (Detroit) | **26.5% vs 9%** (OR about 3) | **53% vs 27%** (OR 2.6) |
| Merikangas 1990 (Zurich) | 14.7% vs 7.3% (OR 2.2) | 31.2% vs 14.1% (OR 2.7) |
| Lipton 2000 (USA/UK) | 47% vs 17% (OR 4.4) | — |
| Breslau 2003 | 40.7% vs 16% (OR 3.5) | — |

- **Anxiety risk exceeds depression risk.** Mixed anxiety–depression is common.
- **Specific associations:** **panic disorder** (OR 3.4), phobias, **GAD, social phobia**, **bipolar spectrum** (cyclothymia, hypomania).
- **Suicide attempts are increased**, not wholly explained by depression (Breslau 1992).
- **Bidirectional:** about **half of patients with major depression have migraine**.
  - Depression at baseline **tripled** the risk of new-onset migraine (OR 3; Breslau 2003).
  - Swartz found **phobia** (OR 1.7), not depression, predicted incident migraine.
- **Typical sequence** (Merikangas): **childhood anxiety → migraine → adult depression**.
- **Specificity:**
  - risks are similar in **severe non-migraine headache** (depression 35.8%) and comparable to arthritis or back pain, suggesting that **pain** drives part of the excess;
  - a **dose–response** with headache frequency;
  - **migraine with aura** tends to carry slightly more risk (major depression 49% vs 37% without aura).

### Personality [1]
- **Wolff's "migraine personality"** (ambitious, perfectionistic, obsessional, inflexible, resentful): based on **biased clinic samples**. Selby & Lance found most migraineurs "normal".
- **Neuroticism, harm avoidance, psychasthenia, social introversion** are raised, especially in **clinic attenders** (who differ from non-attenders: an ascertainment effect) and in chronic migraine. These traits are **not specific** (also seen in tension-type headache).
- **Constitutional emotional/autonomic reactivity** may predispose to attacks and to treatment resistance.
- Sumatriptan non-responders have more anxiety and neuroticism.

### Stress as a precipitant [1]
- **Sustained, bottled-up emotional tension** more than acute upset; **"let-down" attacks** (weekends, first day of holidays).
- Henryk-Gutt & Rees: **about 60%** cite work or relationship problems; **over half of diary-recorded attacks** followed stressful events; about a third had no precipitant.
- About half of migraineurs show a stress–headache relationship (same day, or 1–3 days later).

### Psychiatric phenomena during attacks [1]
- **Prodrome/postdrome:** irritability, anxiety, **elation or energy rebound**, lassitude.
- **During attacks** (Klee, hospitalised cases):
  - memory impairment **10%**;
  - **clouding or delirium 8%**;
  - anxiety 8%;
  - **complex hallucinations 6%** (e.g., Lilliputian figures);
  - **body-image change 6%** ("**Alice in Wonderland**" macro- and microsomatognosia, reduplication, **physical duality**);
  - severe depression 4%;
  - in total **22%** had severe mental symptoms.
- **Other phenomena:**
  - altered consciousness and **migrainous syncope** (basilar);
  - **dreamy states**, déjà vu, depersonalisation;
  - **transient global amnesia**;
  - acute organic reactions with paranoid features;
  - **"mental migraine equivalent"**: a psychotic episode without headache. **Psychosis is very rare** and usually an elaboration of an acute organic reaction;
  - **Capgras** during headache (Bhatia);
  - **confusional migraine in children** (6–24 hours).
- **Cognition:** subtle interictal slowing (especially with aura); no accelerated decline or AD risk; rare progressive impairment with recurrent infarcts.

### Differential diagnosis (psychiatric mimics) [1]
- Confusional episodes vs **toxic or metabolic delirium**.
- Prolonged organic reactions vs **transient paranoid reactions**.
- Mood swings with rebound elation vs **bipolar disorder**.
- Migrainous syncope vs **hysterical dissociation**.
- The truth usually emerges with **later headache** or a **history of typical attacks**.

### Chronic (transformed) migraine [1]
- Headache on **15 or more days a month**; malaise, insomnia, weight loss.
- **Depression is usually integral**; **over-medication** contributes.
- Migraine and affective disorder **reinforce each other** (a vicious circle).

### Management [1, 2]
1. **Screen** every frequent or disabling migraineur for **depression, anxiety (panic, phobia, GAD)** and suicidality. Ask about over-medication.
2. **Treat comorbid depression and anxiety:** "it is important to detect and treat depressive or anxiety states that are aggravating the situation".
   - **amitriptyline** is also an evidence-based migraine **prophylactic** (dual benefit);
   - **buspirone** may reduce headache in anxious migraineurs;
   - **interaction:** an SSRI (e.g., fluoxetine) with a **triptan** risks **serotonin syndrome**; monitor [2].
3. **Optimise migraine treatment:** triptans for attacks; **propranolol** (first choice), timolol, **valproate** (also a mood stabiliser), amitriptyline for prophylaxis when attacks occur twice or more a month.
4. **Psychological approaches:**
   - understand the personality and life situation, and the **"function" of attacks** (respite, outlet for repressed anger, self-punishment in chronic depression: Sacks);
   - address **work or interpersonal conflict**;
   - **relaxation training** (enduring improvement), **behavioural techniques** (Mitchell), **hypnosis**;
   - lifestyle: **regular sleep and meals**, judicious stress avoidance.
5. **Chronic migraine:** treat depression; withdraw **over-used medication**; occipital nerve stimulation in selected cases.
6. **Acute psychiatric presentations** (delirium, psychosis during attacks): treat the attack, review medication (attack medications may contribute), and use short-term symptomatic management. Episodes are usually self-limiting.

**Conclusion.** Migraine is strongly and bidirectionally associated with **anxiety** (OR about 2.5–3, panic and phobias especially), **depression** (OR 2–4; half of depressed patients have migraine), bipolar spectrum disorders and suicide attempts. These associations reflect shared vulnerability, the effects of chronic pain, and neuroticism rather than a specific "migraine personality". Attacks themselves can produce delirium, hallucinations, body-image distortion, amnesia and rarely psychosis. Management is to screen for and treat comorbid disorders (choosing agents such as amitriptyline that also prevent migraine, and watching SSRI–triptan interactions), optimise migraine prophylaxis, address stress and conflict, use relaxation and behavioural therapy, and break the depression–over-medication cycle of chronic migraine.
