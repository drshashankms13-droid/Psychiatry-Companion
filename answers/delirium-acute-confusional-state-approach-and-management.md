## Exam answer

### Definition and concept [1]
- **Definition:** a **complex neuropsychiatric syndrome** caused by one or more structural and/or physiological abnormalities that directly or indirectly affect the brain: **"brain failure"**.
- **Course:** potentially reversible, with **acute onset and a fluctuating course**.
- **Continuum:** wakefulness → delirium → coma.
- **Synonyms:** acute confusional state, acute brain failure, acute organic syndrome, toxic psychosis, encephalopathy, postoperative or ICU psychosis, cerebral insufficiency, terminal agitation.
- **Clinical importance:** probably the **commonest psychiatric morbidity in acute medical illness**, yet often under-recognised and under-treated.

### Epidemiology [1]

| Setting | Adults | Elderly |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Community | 0.4% | 1.1% |
| General hospital | 11–42% | 10–42% |
| Medical inpatients | 10–24% | 10–31% |
| Surgical / post-operative | 37–46% / 20–40% | — |
| ICU | 15–52% | 60–80% |
| Psychiatry referrals (Indian data) | 30.8–39% | 48.7% |
| Cancer | 10–35% | 28–85% |

**Why it matters [1]:**
- Mortality is about **twice that of matched controls**.
- Longer hospital stay and higher costs.
- Institutionalisation and functional decline.
- An important **risk marker for dementia**.
- A distressing experience for patients and caregivers.

### Pathogenesis (Maldonado) [1]
- **Hypotheses:**
  - neuro-inflammatory;
  - neuronal ageing;
  - oxidative stress;
  - neurotransmitter deficiency;
  - neuroendocrine;
  - diurnal dysregulation;
  - **network disconnectivity**.
- **Final common pathway:**
  - **reduced cholinergic** and **increased dopaminergic** transmission;
  - increased noradrenergic and altered serotonergic and GABAergic transmission;
  - raised cortisol (stress);
  - increased cytokines (neuroinflammation);
  - **melatonin deficiency**;
  - reduced CSF β-endorphins and somatostatin.
- **Beyond ACh and dopamine:** these interact with glutamate, GABA and opioid systems.

### Clinical features [1]

**Symptoms:**
- **Consciousness:** a range from hyperalert through alert and lethargic to comatose. Caregivers give conflicting reports.
- **Inattention:** difficulty focusing, sustaining and shifting attention; distractibility; repeated questions. Inattention may account for all the other cognitive deficits.
- **Memory and orientation:**
  - poor registration and recent memory; confabulation;
  - disorientation to time, place and person; misidentification;
  - talking as if at home or about dead relatives.
- **Visuospatial:** constructional apraxia, topographical disorientation.
- **Sleep–wake:** nocturnal insomnia, fragmentation, **reversal of the cycle**, vivid dreams confused with reality.
- **Emotion:** fear, anxiety, depression, euphoria, fluctuating within an interview.
- **Fluctuation:** unpredictable, **worse at night**, with lucid intervals.
- **Psychomotor:** agitation (pulling at tubes, getting out of bed) or retardation (apathy, minimal speech).
- **Thought:** disorganised and illogical; **persecutory delusions are common**; poor judgement.
- **Language:** slurred or pressured speech; word-finding and writing difficulty; incoherence; poor comprehension.
- **Perception:** visual (most common), tactile (insects crawling) and auditory hallucinations. Illusions (bed-sheet folds seen as animate; machine alarms heard as sirens). Macro- or micropsia, derealisation.

**Frequency of features (Gupta et al. 2008):**

| Core symptoms | % | Non-core symptoms | % |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Attention deficits | 97–100 | Perceptual disturbances | 50–63 |
| Sleep–wake disturbance | 92–97 | Delusions | 21–31 |
| Memory deficits | 88–96 | Affective changes | 43–86 |
| Disorientation | 76–96 | | |
| Thought process abnormality | 54–79 | | |
| Language disturbance | 57–67 | | |
| Motor alterations | 24–94 | | |

**Motor subtypes (Meagher):**
- **hyperactive:** agitated, restless, pulling at tubes;
- **hypoactive:** withdrawn, drowsy, minimal speech;
- **mixed:** both within 24 hours;
- **no motor subtype.**

### Risk factors and causes [1]
- **Predisposing factors ("SUSPECT CAM"):**
  - sensory (visual) impairment;
  - over-the-counter drugs;
  - comorbidity;
  - illness severity;
  - poor function;
  - alcohol;
  - **old age**;
  - male sex;
  - **cognitive decline or dementia**;
  - prescribed drugs.
- **Precipitating factors ("3MMICR"):**
  - more than 3 medications;
  - malnutrition;
  - iatrogenic events;
  - bladder catheter;
  - physical restraints.
- **Aetiology ("I WATCH DEATH"):**
  - infection, withdrawal, acute metabolic, trauma, CNS pathology, hypoxia;
  - deficiencies, endocrinopathies, acute vascular, toxins/drugs, heavy metals.
- **Mnemonics also listed:** "DELIRIUM", "DEMENTIA" and **"PINCH ME"** (pain, infection, nutrition, constipation, hydration, medication, environment).
- **Life-threatening causes:** Wernicke's encephalopathy, withdrawal, hypertensive crisis, hypoperfusion/hypoxia, hypoglycaemia, hyper/hypothermia, intracranial process or infection, metabolic disturbance or meningitis, poisoning, status epilepticus.
- **High-risk drugs:**
  - benzodiazepines, barbiturates, opioids;
  - anticholinergics (oxybutynin, TCAs, benztropine);
  - antihistamines, H2 blockers;
  - anticonvulsants;
  - antiparkinsonian drugs (levodopa, dopamine agonists);
  - antipsychotics, corticosteroids;
  - cardiac drugs, NSAIDs, theophylline, muscle relaxants.
- **Cumulative burden:** more than 3 drugs. The **Anticholinergic Burden (ACB) scale** scores drugs 1–3. Each 1-point rise in total ACB is linked to a 0.33-point MMSE decline over 2 years and a 26% higher risk of death.

### Approach to the patient with an acute confusional state [1]

**Step 1: the referral** ("uncooperative, pulling tubes, talking irrelevantly, not sleeping, hallucinating"):
- confirm a sudden change in mental state, fluctuation, cognitive and motor disturbance;
- review the history for physical illness, precipitants, and drugs or substances.

**Step 2: history** (CCSMH guideline):
- onset and course;
- predisposing and precipitating factors;
- medical and surgical history;
- **premorbid cognition**;
- psychiatric history;
- all medications (prescribed and over the counter);
- substance use (type, last intake).

**Step 3: examination:**
- **General:** hydration, nutrition, sepsis.
- **Focused examination:**
  - head trauma;
  - pupils: fixed and dilated (intracranial cause), miosis (opioids), mydriasis (anticholinergics);
  - papilloedema;
  - **ophthalmoplegia or nystagmus (Wernicke's)**;
  - thyromegaly, neck rigidity;
  - focal deficits;
  - skin: fever, injection marks, patches, liver stigmata;
  - chest, heart and abdomen;
  - **urinary retention, faecal impaction**.

**Step 4: rating** (arousal, screening, diagnosis, severity, cognition, subtype, aetiology, distress):

| Purpose | Instruments |
| --- | --- |
| Arousal | RASS |
| Premorbid cognition | IQCODE |
| Screening | Nu-DESC, DOSS, ICDSC |
| Diagnosis | **CAM, CAM-ICU**, DRS-R-98, MDAS |
| Severity | **DRS-R-98**, MDAS, Delirium Index |
| Cognition | MMSE, Cognitive Test for Delirium, clock drawing, digit span, vigilance "A" test |
| Motor subtype | Delirium Motor Subtype Scale |
| Aetiology | Delirium Etiology Checklist |
| Experience | Delirium Experience Questionnaire |
| Physical severity | APACHE-II, GCS, SOFA, Charlson |

**CAM (Inouye 1990)** is a bedside tool usable by non-psychiatrists. It is positive with:
1. acute onset/fluctuating course; **and**
2. inattention; **and either**
3. disorganised thinking; **or**
4. altered level of consciousness.

**CAM-ICU:**
- **First step:** check RASS. If RASS is −4 or −5, stop and recheck later.
- **Feature 2 (inattention):** "SAVEAHAART" letter test (>2 errors) or picture recognition.
- **Feature 4 (disorganised thinking):** 4 yes/no questions plus a command (>1 error).
- **Positive** = features 1 + 2 + (3 or 4).

**Step 5: diagnostic criteria:**
- **DSM-5:**
  - A. disturbance in attention and awareness;
  - B. develops over hours to days, is a change from baseline and fluctuates;
  - C. an additional cognitive disturbance;
  - D. not better explained by a neurocognitive disorder, and not in the context of coma;
  - E. evidence of a medical condition, substance, toxin or multiple aetiologies.
- **ICD-10:** impaired consciousness and attention; global cognitive disturbance; psychomotor disturbance; sleep–wake disturbance; emotional disturbance.

**Step 6: differential diagnosis (Mattoo 2010):**

| Feature | Delirium | Dementia | Depression | Schizophrenia |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Onset | Acute | Insidious | Variable | Variable |
| Consciousness | Impaired, fluctuating | Clear until late | Clear | Clear |
| Attention | Impaired | Late impairment | Poor | Poor |
| Orientation | Disoriented | Late disorientation | Usually oriented | Usually oriented |
| Memory | Poor short-term | Poor short- and long-term | Intact | Intact |
| Diurnal variation | Common | May occur | Morning worsening | Not seen |
| Psychotic symptoms | Fleeting, simple | Less common | Less common | Persistent, complex |
| Hallucinations | Visual > auditory; tactile | Usually absent | Auditory if present | Mostly auditory |
| Mood | Fluctuating | Flat, apathetic | Sad | Flat |

**Step 7: investigations:**
- **Must do:** **serum electrolytes, RFT, LFT, haemogram, ABG**.
- **As indicated:** further tests, including neuroimaging and EEG.
- Monitor abnormalities closely.

### Management [1]

**A. Treat the cause (IPS CPG):**
- stop offending drugs; minimise anticholinergic load (ACB scale); remove unnecessary medications;
- antibiotics for infection; antidotes for poisoning;
- **alcohol withdrawal delirium: benzodiazepines plus thiamine**.

**B. Non-pharmacological care (IPS CPG):**
- **Safety and monitoring:**
  - close observation of vitals, oxygenation, hydration, electrolytes, glucose, nutrition and input/output;
  - prevent pressure sores;
  - mobilise; remove the catheter early;
  - maintain bowel and bladder function;
  - review drug interactions.
- **Unambiguous environment:**
  - remove clutter; single room if possible; avoid bed moves;
  - avoid jargon (it may fuel paranoia);
  - eye contact, touch, clear instructions; involve family.
- **Support:**
  - familiar objects;
  - daytime light and dim night light (40–60 W);
  - noise below 45 dB by day and 20 dB at night; room at 21–23.8 °C.
- **Orientation:** repeated reminders of day, time, place and people; visible clock and calendar.
- **Competence:**
  - **glasses, hearing aids and dentures**; interpreter if needed;
  - encourage self-care and participation.
- **Restraints:** avoid if possible.
- **Sleep:** schedule care and IV fluids to allow uninterrupted sleep (a sleep protocol).
- **ICU:** the **ABCDEF bundle**:
  - awakening and breathing trials;
  - choice of sedation and analgesia;
  - delirium monitoring;
  - early mobility and exercise;
  - family involvement.

**C. Pharmacological treatment** (IPS CPG; Alici & Breitbart):
- **Options:**
  - antipsychotics;
  - benzodiazepines (**only for alcohol withdrawal**);
  - cholinesterase inhibitors (rivastigmine);
  - α2-agonist (dexmedetomidine);
  - melatonin;
  - combinations.
- **Antipsychotics are the mainstay.**
  - **Haloperidol** is the most commonly recommended drug and the "gold standard".
  - Olanzapine, risperidone, quetiapine, lurasidone and ziprasidone are **equally efficacious**.
- **Rules for antipsychotic use:**
  - weigh risks and benefits; start in liaison with family and the treating team;
  - **baseline ECG for QTc**;
  - document the indication;
  - use the **lowest effective dose; start low, titrate**;
  - monitor symptoms, over-sedation and EPS.
- **Hypoactive delirium:** antipsychotics are **generally not recommended**.

| Drug | Dose | Routes | Comments |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Haloperidol** | 0.5–2 mg every 2–12 h | PO, IV, IM, SC | Gold standard. EPS more likely above 4.5 mg/day. Monitor QT |
| Chlorpromazine | 12.5–50 mg every 4–6 h | PO, IV, IM, SC, PR | For agitation (sedative). Hypotension, anticholinergic effects |
| Olanzapine | 2.5–5 mg every 12–24 h | PO, IM | Older age, dementia and hypoactive delirium predict poor response |
| Quetiapine | 12.5–100 mg every 12–24 h | PO | Sedating; useful with insomnia |
| Risperidone | 0.25–1 mg every 12–24 h | PO | EPS above 6 mg/day; orthostatic hypotension |
| Aripiprazole | 5–30 mg every 24 h | PO, IM | May suit hypoactive delirium; evidence limited to case reports |
| Ziprasidone | 10–40 mg every 12–24 h | PO, IM | Monitor QT; case reports only |

- **QTc:**
  - **420–460 ms or more** is generally considered prolonged, so baseline monitoring is recommended;
  - haloperidol carries dose-related QT/TdP risk above 20 mg/day;
  - **risk factors:** age, female sex, bradycardia, LV failure, congenital long QT, hypokalaemia, hypomagnesaemia, hypocalcaemia, hepatic dysfunction.
- **Black box warning:** increased mortality in elderly patients with dementia-related psychosis.
- **Dose targeting:** aim for a **Riker SAS score of 3–4** (calm, easily rousable).
- **Cholinesterase inhibitors:**
  - donepezil may be useful;
  - physostigmine is no better than placebo;
  - rivastigmine may reduce frequency and duration. Added to antipsychotics, it led to recovery from chronic delirium in 71.4%.
- **Melatonin:**
  - delirium is associated with low melatonin;
  - **prevention:** a meta-analysis of 4 RCTs (n = 669) showed a trend towards lower incidence (RR 0.41, 95% CI 0.15–1.13, p = 0.08); there is no consensus;
  - **treatment:** data are limited to case reports and series.
- **Counterpoint (Maudsley, in the context of HIV):** antipsychotics are **probably not effective** in treating delirium. Use them only as a last resort in severe cases where non-drug measures fail, with haloperidol at the lowest dose [2].

**D. Communication [1]:**
- explain the diagnosis to colleagues and the treating team;
- discuss reversible causes and implicated drugs;
- **inform and involve the family**.

### Delirium in the emergency room (applying the approach) [1]
1. **Recognise:** acute change in mental state with inattention. Use CAM or **4AT**, and listen to family who say the patient is confused.
2. **Exclude life-threatening causes first:**
   - hypoglycaemia, hypoxia or hypoperfusion;
   - Wernicke's (ophthalmoplegia, nystagmus);
   - withdrawal;
   - status epilepticus, intracranial lesions or infection;
   - poisoning;
   - hyper- or hypothermia;
   - hypertensive crisis.
3. **Examine** pupils, fundi, neck, focal signs, skin, bladder and rectum. Send electrolytes, RFT, LFT, haemogram and ABG; neuroimaging or EEG as indicated.
4. **Specific treatment:**
   - **alcohol withdrawal:** benzodiazepines plus thiamine;
   - poisoning: antidotes;
   - infection: antibiotics;
   - stop culprit drugs.
5. **Safety:** observation, a calm environment, family presence, glasses and hearing aids. Avoid restraints where possible.
6. **Severe agitation or distress:**
   - low-dose **haloperidol** (0.5–2 mg) after ECG, or another antipsychotic, titrated to SAS 3–4;
   - **avoid benzodiazepines except in alcohol withdrawal**.

### Prevention (IPS CPG) [1]
- **Baseline cognition and risk factors;** serial MMSE/MoCA/HMSE. Screen for delirium if the score falls by 2 points.
- **Drugs:**
  - stop unnecessary drugs; minimise anticholinergics, opioids and sedatives;
  - avoid high doses with polypharmacy.
- **Senses and sleep:** restore glasses and hearing aids; ensure sleep and a normal sleep–wake cycle; adequate lighting.
- **Environment:** minimal room changes; reorientation and cognitive stimulation; quiet surroundings.
- **Body:** good pain control; mobilisation; no restraints; minimal catheters.

**Conclusion.** Delirium is acute brain failure, marked by inattention, fluctuating awareness and global cognitive disturbance. It is common in medically ill and elderly patients and carries high mortality and a risk of dementia. Management has five steps:
1. systematic detection (CAM, CAM-ICU, DRS-R-98);
2. a search for, and treatment of, the cause;
3. a multicomponent non-pharmacological approach;
4. cautious, short-term, low-dose antipsychotics for distressing agitation (benzodiazepines only in alcohol withdrawal);
5. prevention.

## In depth

### Course and outcome [1]
- **Course:** an acute insult may lead to recovery, persistent delirium, or a step-down in cognitive function.
- **Long-term:** delirium is a risk marker for later dementia, and it causes institutionalisation and functional decline.

### Anticholinergic medicines by category [1]
- **Antiarrhythmics:** disopyramide, procainamide, quinidine.
- **Antipsychotics:** chlorpromazine, clozapine, olanzapine, quetiapine, thioridazine.
- **Antiemetics:** prochlorperazine, meclizine, dimenhydrinate.
- **Antispasmodics:** atropine, dicyclomine, oxybutynin, tolterodine, scopolamine.
- **Antihistamines:** chlorpheniramine, diphenhydramine, promethazine.
- **Muscle relaxants:** cyclobenzaprine, orphenadrine.
- **Antiparkinsonian drugs:** benztropine, trihexyphenidyl, procyclidine.
- **TCAs:** amitriptyline, imipramine, nortriptyline, doxepin.

ACB score 3 examples include amitriptyline, clozapine, olanzapine, quetiapine, paroxetine and trihexyphenidyl.

### QT-prolonging psychotropics (higher risk) [1]
- **Typical antipsychotics:** thioridazine, haloperidol, chlorpromazine, pimozide.
- **Atypical antipsychotics:** ziprasidone, iloperidone, quetiapine.
- **SSRIs:** citalopram, escitalopram.
- **TCAs/TeCAs:** amitriptyline, imipramine, maprotiline, nortriptyline, desipramine, clomipramine.
- **Other antidepressants:** venlafaxine, mirtazapine.

### Critical appraisal (from the sources)
- **Antipsychotic evidence:** the efficacy of antipsychotics in ICU delirium has not been tested in large placebo-controlled RCTs [1, slide 111]. Maudsley regards them as probably ineffective [2]. Yet the IPS CPG calls them the mainstay [1]. The sources conflict, so use them for distress and safety, not as a cure.
- **Melatonin:** evidence for prevention is inconclusive; for treatment it rests on case reports [1].
