## Exam answer

**Definition.** Acute and transient psychotic disorder (ICD-11 **6A23**; ICD-10 F23) is a psychosis with:
- **acute onset** (from non-psychotic to clearly psychotic within 2 weeks);
- **polymorphic, rapidly changing** positive symptoms (delusions, hallucinations, perceptual disturbance, disorganisation) with emotional turmoil and perplexity;
- **full remission within 3 months**, usually within days to 1 month.

It is not explained by mood disorder, substances or an organic cause [1,2]. ICD-11 dropped ICD-10's four subtypes and kept only single vs recurrent episode [1]. In DSM-5-TR, the equivalent is **brief psychotic disorder** (remits within 1 month) or **schizophreniform disorder** (1–6 months) [1].

### Mechanism
Stress–vulnerability model: lower genetic loading + acute psychosocial stress (marriage, migration, job loss) or physiological stress (fever, childbirth) → transient hyperarousal (P300 latency shorter than controls even in remission; frontotemporal **hypermetabolism** on FDG-PET, the opposite of the hypofrontality of schizophrenia) and a pro-inflammatory state (raised IL-6, TNF-α, TGF-β) → brief polymorphic psychosis → rapid recovery [1,2]. Patients with a family history of ATPD need less stress to become ill, and vice versa [1,2].

### Course and outcome — key data

| Dimension | Findings |
| --- | --- |
| Recovery from episode | Most remit within 4 weeks of treatment; minimal residual or negative symptoms [1] |
| Chandigarh 5-year follow-up | 75% complete recovery with no residual symptoms [2] |
| WHO CAP cohort, 20-year follow-up (Chandigarh) | Up to 82% excellent outcome, no relapse, no residual symptoms [2] |
| Recurrence | 22% at 5 years (DOSMeD); 35% at 5 years (Rozario); 46.6% at 8 years (Malhotra); 30–38% in 2 years, up to 54% later (meta-analysis) [1,2] |
| Diagnostic stability | Retained in 30–73% across studies; pooled ≈0.56 over 4.5 years; 87% at 8 years in Jørgensen [1,2] |
| Change to schizophrenia | 15% at 1 year in one series; 1 in 17 at 12 years in the Chandigarh DOSMeD cohort [2] |
| Change to affective disorder | ~28% at 1 year in one series [2] |
| Medication-free remission | Up to ~60% stay well without medication [1] |
| Mortality and suicide | SMR similar to schizophrenia and bipolar disorder; suicidal behaviour 36–55% in the acute polymorphic phase [1] |
| Compared with first-episode schizophrenia | Better long-term prognosis (meta-analysis of 82 studies) [1] |

### Predictors

| Good outcome | Poor outcome / conversion to schizophrenia |
| --- | --- |
| Female sex [2] | Male sex [1] |
| Acute stress at onset [2] | Younger age at onset [1] |
| Polymorphic picture **without** schizophrenic symptoms [1,2] | First-rank symptoms, thought disorder [1] |
| Good premorbid adjustment [1] | Family history of schizophrenia [1] |
| Developing-country setting, rural, low SES [2] | Predominantly delusional or schizophrenia-like subtype [1] |
| Postpartum or febrile precipitant [2] | Longer episode duration |

### Assessment
Rule out organic causes in every case: fever, delirium, NMDA-receptor and other **autoimmune encephalitis** (acute onset, catatonia, autonomic instability, seizures; check anti-neuronal antibodies), substance use, postpartum medical causes, epilepsy. Assess stressors, premorbid function, family history and suicide risk. Use longitudinal follow-up at 3 months to confirm the diagnosis [1].

### Management
- Brief hospitalisation if risk is high.
- Low-dose antipsychotic: haloperidol, risperidone or olanzapine give dramatic improvement within about 4 weeks [1].
- Benzodiazepines for agitation.
- Address the stressor; psychoeducation for the family.
- Continue medication for about 3 months after remission, then taper with monitoring. Long-term maintenance is not routinely needed, except for recurrent cases or emerging schizophrenia.
- Follow up for recurrence and change of diagnosis.

### Key evidence
- **WHO IPSS, DOSMeD and CAP** studies established non-affective acute remitting psychosis (NARP), which is about 10 times more common in developing countries and more common in women [2].
- **Chandigarh studies (Malhotra):** 75–82% excellent outcome; stress, female sex and absence of schizophrenic symptoms predict good outcome [2].
- **Fusar-Poli 2016 meta-analysis:** brief psychotic episodes have better prognosis than remitted first-episode schizophrenia [1].

**Conclusion.** ATPD is a common, mostly **benign** psychosis in India and other low- and middle-income countries: most patients recover fully, often stay well without medication, and keep the diagnosis. A substantial minority, however, recur (about 1 in 3) or convert to schizophrenia or bipolar disorder (up to about 1 in 3 in some series), and acute-phase suicide risk is high. Treat briefly, rule out organic and autoimmune causes, and follow up long enough to catch the minority with a poor course.

## In depth

### Background
Acute remitting "third psychoses" were described in many countries before ICD-10 [2]:
- France: *bouffée délirante* (Magnan).
- Germany: cycloid psychosis (Kleist, Leonhard); motility psychosis.
- Scandinavia: psychogenic/reactive psychosis; Langfeldt's schizophreniform psychosis.
- USA: good-prognosis schizophrenia; hysterical psychosis.
- Japan: atypical psychosis (Mitsuda).
- Africa and the West Indies: acute psychotic reactions.
- India: "acute psychosis of uncertain origin", hysterical psychosis.

Common features were sudden onset, fluid polymorphic symptoms, prominent affect and fear, a precipitant, good premorbid adjustment and complete recovery [2].

WHO studies (IPSS 1968–70, DOSMeD 1978–80, CAP 1980–82) confirmed this group, which led to F23 in ICD-10 (1992). ICD-10 itself acknowledged that "the nomenclature of these acute disorders is as uncertain as their nosological status" [1,2]. Savita Malhotra's DLN Murthy Rao Oration (2007) summarised the extensive Indian validation work [2].

### Epidemiology
- Annual incidence 3.9–9.6 per 100,000 in UK and Danish data; 5.8–20% of clinic and hospital psychosis samples [1].
- In the CAP study (n=1,004), 41.7% had stress close to onset and two-thirds were well with no relapse at 1 year [2].
- ICMR multicentre study (Bikaner, Goa, Patiala, Vellore; n=323): psychological stress in 50% and physiological stress (fever, childbirth) in another 20% [2].
- Female preponderance (in most but not all studies), low SES, rural background, migrants, a summer peak in onset (Chandigarh), childbirth within 3 months before onset, and short non-specific fever beforehand [1,2].

### Classification and criteria

| System | Category | Duration rule |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ICD-10 F23 | Acute polymorphic without (F23.0) / with (F23.1) schizophrenic symptoms; acute schizophrenia-like (F23.2); predominantly delusional (F23.3); 5th digit for associated acute stress | Remission within 3 months; schizophrenic symptoms ≤1 month (else F20) [2] |
| ICD-11 6A23 | Single episode / multiple episodes; polymorphic picture required | Remission within 3 months; typically days to 1 month [1] |
| DSM-5-TR | Brief psychotic disorder (with/without marked stressor; with postpartum onset) | 1 day to <1 month |
| DSM-5-TR | Schizophreniform disorder | 1–6 months |

Mismatch example: a 6-week psychosis with schizophrenic symptoms is schizophrenia in ICD-11 but schizophreniform disorder in DSM-5. This lack of harmony has limited research [1].

### Mechanism in detail
- **Genetics:** family history of ATPD is 3 times higher, and of schizophrenia 4 times lower, in relatives of ATPD probands than schizophrenia probands. Schizophrenia in relatives is seen mainly when the proband has schizophrenic symptoms. Affective illness rates are similar. A Danish register found an RR of 1.93 with any family psychiatric history. A Japanese GWAS of "atypical psychosis" found partial overlap with schizophrenia but not bipolar disorder [1,2].
- **Neurophysiology:** in remission, ATPD showed higher P300 amplitude and shorter latency than schizophrenia, and shorter latency even than healthy controls, which suggests hyperarousal (Indian study, 25 per group) [1].
- **Imaging:** FDG-PET within 2 weeks of onset showed global (frontal and temporal) hypermetabolism in ATPD vs frontal hypometabolism in schizophrenia (PGIMER; n=17 vs 12) [1].
- **Immunology:** raised IL-6, TNF-α, TGF-β; TNF-α still higher at follow-up (n=41) [1].
- **Biochemistry:** altered amino-acid pathways, raised bilirubin; in cycloid psychosis, raised glycine and low tryptophan [1].
- **Autoimmune overlap:** some "ATPD" cases may be NMDAR or VGKC antibody encephalitis. Screen first-episode acute psychosis with atypical features [1].
- **Neurocognition:** impaired acutely but processing speed recovers within 6 months; brief psychotic disorder is the least impaired first-episode subgroup (Ayesa-Arriola) [1].

### Clinical features in detail
- **Polymorphic:** delusions and hallucinations that change from day to day, emotional turmoil (ecstasy, terror), perplexity, confusion without clouding, and fluctuating anxiety [1,2].
- **Schizophrenia-like:** first-rank symptoms with a stable picture over <1 month.
- **Predominantly delusional.**
- **With acute stress:** onset within 2 weeks of a stressor.
- **Postpartum:** onset within weeks after delivery.

Negative symptoms are minimal or absent [1].

### Assessment in detail

| Area | Purpose |
| --- | --- |
| Temperature, vitals, sensorium | Exclude delirium and infection |
| CBC, electrolytes, glucose, LFT/RFT, thyroid | Organic screen |
| Urine drug screen | Substances |
| HIV, VDRL | Infectious causes |
| EEG | Seizures, encephalopathy |
| MRI brain, CSF, anti-NMDAR/VGKC antibodies | If catatonia, dyskinesia, seizures, autonomic instability or reduced consciousness [1] |
| Postpartum: anaemia, sepsis, thyroiditis | Postpartum causes |
| Stress and premorbid assessment | Prognosis |
| Suicide risk | High in acute polymorphic phase [1] |

### Differential diagnosis

| Condition | Distinguishing feature |
| --- | --- |
| Schizophrenia / schizophreniform | Longer duration; insidious onset; negative symptoms; hypofrontality |
| Bipolar mania or mixed state | Sustained elevated or irritable mood, overactivity; family history of BPAD |
| Psychotic depression | Pervasive low mood; mood-congruent delusions |
| Delirium | Clouded consciousness, fluctuating attention, medical cause |
| Autoimmune encephalitis | Catatonia, seizures, dyskinesias, autonomic instability [1] |
| Substance-induced psychosis | Temporal link; toxicology |
| Dissociative trance/possession | Culturally patterned, often episodic, with amnesia |
| BLIPS (clinical high risk) | Under 1 week, spontaneous resolution; ~70% of BLIPS also meet ATPD criteria [1] |

### Management details
- **Acute:** a safe environment; IM lorazepam or haloperidol + promethazine for severe agitation; low-dose oral haloperidol, risperidone or olanzapine; ECT if catatonic or life-threatening [1].
- **Duration:** no formal guidelines exist [1]. Clinical practice is 1–3 months after remission, then taper over weeks. Many patients stay well without medication [1].
- **Recurrent ATPD:** consider longer prophylaxis; review the diagnosis (bipolar or cycloid course); lithium if there is an affective component.
- **Postpartum ATPD:** manage as postpartum psychosis (mother–baby unit, check breastfeeding compatibility of medication).
- **Psychosocial:** address stressors (marital, migration); family psychoeducation; avoid stigmatising labels; plan follow-up visits at 3 and 6 months, then yearly.

### Critical appraisal
- **Validity:** ATPD passes several Robins–Guze/Feighner validators (distinct clinical picture, follow-up, family studies, early biological markers), but biological data come from small, often single-centre Indian studies [1,2].
- **Heterogeneity:** ICD-10 subtypes had poor validity in field trials; ICD-11 simplified this. Schizophrenia-like and delusional forms behave more like schizophrenia [1].
- **Duration criteria are arbitrary.** DOSMeD data suggest natural episodes may last up to 28 weeks, longer than the 3 months ICD allows [2].
- **Culture and context:** higher incidence and better outcome in LMICs may reflect true biology, social factors, or diagnostic habits.
- **Neuroinflammatory hypothesis:** some ATPD may be undiagnosed autoimmune or infective encephalitis [1].
- **CHR overlap:** BLIPS and ATPD overlap diagnostically and prognostically, which raises the question of whether ATPD belongs in a high-risk framework [1].
- **No treatment guidelines or RCTs** exist specifically for ATPD [1].

### Indian context
India is the most important source of ATPD research: the PGIMER Chandigarh studies, the ICMR multicentre study, and NIMHANS neurobiology [1,2]. ATPD is common in Indian emergency and inpatient settings, especially among women, rural patients and postpartum mothers. Clinicians must avoid labelling these patients "schizophrenia" too early, because that label brings stigma and consequences for marriage and employment. MHCA 2017 provisions on supported admission and emergency treatment (72 hours) apply in the acute phase.

### Recent advances
- **Established:** ICD-11 simplification (6A23, single vs multiple episode).
- **Emerging:**
  - PET hypermetabolism, P300 hyperarousal and inflammatory markers as candidate biomarkers [1].
  - Routine autoimmune screening in atypical acute psychosis [1].
  - Integration with the CHR-P framework [1].
- **Investigational:** immunomodulatory treatment in antibody-positive acute psychosis; dimensional (HiTOP/RDoC) approaches across ATPD, schizophrenia and bipolar disorder.
