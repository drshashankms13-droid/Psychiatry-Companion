## Exam answer

**Definition.** **MHCA 2017, Section 2(1)(i):** informed consent is "**consent given for a specific intervention, without any force, undue influence, fraud, threat, mistake or misrepresentation, and obtained after disclosing to a person adequate information including risks and benefits of, and alternatives to, the specific intervention in a language and manner understood by the person**" [1].

It expresses the ethical principle of **respect for autonomy** and the legal doctrine that touching or treating a person without valid consent is battery or negligence.

### Elements of valid consent
1. **Capacity (competence):** the ability to make the specific decision.
2. **Disclosure (information):**
   - nature and purpose of the intervention;
   - **benefits, risks** (material risks), **alternatives**, including no treatment;
   - prognosis with and without treatment;
   - who will do it.
3. **Understanding:** in **a language and manner understood by the person**, using simple language, sign language or visual aids (MHCA Sections 2(1)(i), 4(2)) [1].
4. **Voluntariness:** free from force, undue influence, fraud, threat, mistake or misrepresentation [1].
5. **Decision and authorisation:** the person agrees or refuses. Consent is **specific** (to a particular intervention), **ongoing**, and **can be withdrawn**.

### Capacity under MHCA 2017 (Section 4) [1]
- **Presumption of capacity:** every person, **including a person with mental illness**, is deemed to have capacity if able to:
  - (a) **understand** information relevant to the decision; **or**
  - (b) **appreciate** reasonably foreseeable consequences of a decision or lack of decision; **or**
  - (c) **communicate** the decision by speech, expression, gesture or other means.
- **Section 4(3):** a decision that others think inappropriate or wrong **does not by itself mean the person lacks capacity**.
- Capacity is **decision-specific and time-specific**. A diagnosis of mental illness does not equal incapacity.
- **Clinical tests:**
  - **Appelbaum & Grisso's four abilities:** understanding, appreciation, reasoning, expressing a choice;
  - **MacCAT-T**;
  - MHCA guidance documents (Section 81 Expert Committee) provide a capacity assessment format [general knowledge].

### Types of consent
- **Implied** (e.g., offering an arm for a blood test). Under the TPG 2020, a **patient-initiated teleconsultation implies consent** [2].
- **Express:** **verbal or written**.
  - Written consent is needed for ECT, procedures and research, and for caregiver-, health worker- or RMP-initiated teleconsultations [2, 3].
- **Proxy / surrogate consent:**
  - **nominated representative (NR)** for people who lack capacity;
  - **guardian** for minors.
- **Supported decision-making** (MHCA's model): the NR **supports** the person to make decisions rather than replacing them [1].

### Consent in Indian mental health law (MHCA 2017) [1]
| Situation | Consent rule |
| --- | --- |
| **Independent patient (Sections 85–86)** | Admission and treatment **only with the patient's informed consent**. "An independent patient shall not be given treatment without his informed consent" (86(5)) |
| **Minor (Section 87)** | Treated with the **informed consent of the nominated representative** (usually the guardian) |
| **Supported admission (Sections 89–90)** | Treatment with the **informed consent of the patient with the support of the NR**, taking into account any **advance directive**. **Capacity reviewed every 7 days** (Section 89); if the patient regains capacity, their consent governs |
| **Advance directive (Sections 5–13)** | A capacitous adult specifies treatment wishes and the NR. Invoked only when capacity is lost. **A current capacitous decision overrides the AD** (5(4)) |
| **Emergency treatment (Section 94)** | Without the patient's consent, but **subject to the NR's informed consent where available**, to prevent death or irreversible harm, serious harm to self or others, or serious property damage. **Limited to 72 hours** (7 days in a declared disaster). **No ECT** (94(3)) |
| **ECT** | Informed consent. **Minors:** guardian's consent plus **prior Board permission** (95(2)). Unmodified ECT prohibited (95(1)(a)) |
| **Psychosurgery (Section 96)** | **The patient's own informed consent plus Board approval**. No proxy |
| **Research (Section 99)** | **Free and informed consent**. For those unable to consent who do not resist: State Authority permission, NR consent, and strict conditions. Consent **may be withdrawn at any time** |
| **Restraint (Section 97)** | No seclusion or solitary confinement. Physical restraint only as the only means of preventing imminent harm, authorised by the psychiatrist, for the shortest time; recorded; **NR informed within 24 hours**; reported to the Board monthly [1] |

### Exceptions to informed consent (general medical law)
- **Emergencies** (doctrine of necessity; MHCA Section 94).
- **Therapeutic privilege:** withholding information that would itself cause serious harm. This is narrow and contested.
- **Waiver:** a patient chooses not to know.
- **Lack of capacity:** proxy or NR consent, or best-interest decision.
- **Statutory exceptions:** court orders; public health law.

### Indian case law
- **Samira Kohli v. Dr Prabha Manchanda (2008, Supreme Court):** consent must be **specific** to the procedure. Consent for diagnostic laparoscopy does not cover hysterectomy. India follows a **"reasonable doctor" (Bolam-type) standard of disclosure**, not the full "prudent patient" standard [general knowledge].
- **Common Cause v. Union of India (2018):** advance directives and passive euthanasia; autonomy as part of the **right to life with dignity (Article 21)** [general knowledge].

### Special issues in psychiatry
1. **Fluctuating capacity:** psychosis, mania, delirium, intoxication, dementia. Reassess regularly; involve the NR.
2. **Coercion vs persuasion:** families pressure patients; the doctor's authority. "Undue influence" invalidates consent [1].
3. **Long-term treatments:**
   - **disclose tardive dyskinesia, metabolic effects, clozapine monitoring, lithium toxicity, valproate teratogenicity** (written consent advisable);
   - depot antipsychotics.
4. **ECT:** written consent separately for the course; disclose cognitive effects; right to withdraw.
5. **Psychotherapy:** consent covers the nature, duration, limits of confidentiality, and possible emotional distress.
6. **Telepsychiatry:**
   - identity verification;
   - consent for recording;
   - caregiver authorisation letter;
   - capacity assessment by video (TOG 2020) [2, 3].
7. **Children and adolescents:** guardian consent plus the **child's assent**. Explain confidentiality limits, including **POCSO mandatory reporting**.
8. **Research with vulnerable people:** ICMR National Ethical Guidelines (2017) and MHCA Section 99.
9. **Off-label prescribing:** disclose it.
10. **Documentation:** what was disclosed, the patient's questions, capacity assessment, and the decision.

**Conclusion.** Informed consent is a **process, not a form**. It needs capacity, adequate disclosure, understanding, voluntariness and a specific decision. MHCA 2017 embeds a **rights-based, capacity-presumptive, supported decision-making** model:
- capacity under Section 4;
- independent and supported admission;
- nominated representatives;
- advance directives;
- strict limits on emergency treatment, ECT, psychosurgery and research.

For psychiatrists, the challenge is to respect autonomy while managing fluctuating capacity and risk, and to document carefully.

## In depth

### Background
- **Nuremberg Code (1947):** "the voluntary consent of the human subject is absolutely essential."
- **Declaration of Helsinki (1964, revised).**
- **Belmont Report (1979):** respect for persons, beneficence, justice.
- **Legal cases:**
  - Schloendorff (1914): "every human being of adult years and sound mind has a right to determine what shall be done with his own body";
  - Canterbury v. Spence (1972; prudent patient);
  - Montgomery (UK, 2015; material risk).
- **UNCRPD (2006), Article 12:** equal recognition before the law; supported decision-making. MHCA 2017 was drafted to comply [general knowledge].

### Epidemiology / empirical data
- Most psychiatric inpatients retain decision-making capacity. Studies suggest about **29% of psychiatric inpatients lack capacity**, highest in mania and psychosis and lower in depression (Okai 2007 review) [general knowledge].

### Classification of decision-making models
| Model | Description |
| --- | --- |
| Paternalistic | Doctor decides |
| Informative | Doctor provides facts; patient decides |
| Interpretive / deliberative | Shared exploration of values |
| **Shared decision-making** | Recommended standard |
| **Supported decision-making** | MHCA/UNCRPD: support rather than substitution |

### Mechanism: how mental illness affects capacity
- **Psychosis:** delusions about treatment (e.g., "medicine is poison") impair appreciation.
- **Mania:** impaired reasoning and appreciation of risk.
- **Depression:** hopelessness distorts appreciation of benefit ("nothing will help").
- **Dementia and delirium:** impaired understanding and retention.
- **Anorexia nervosa:** appreciation impaired despite intact understanding.

### Clinical features: assessing capacity (practical)
1. Explain the information simply.
2. Ask the person to **explain it back** (understanding).
3. Ask how it applies to them (appreciation).
4. Ask how they weigh the options (reasoning).
5. Ask for a choice (communication).
6. Document their verbatim responses.

### Assessment tools
- **MacCAT-T** (treatment), **MacCAT-CR** (research).
- **Aid to Capacity Evaluation (ACE).**
- **MHCA capacity assessment guidance**: NIMHANS / Expert Committee format [general knowledge].

### Differential: capacity vs competence
- **Capacity** is a clinical determination.
- **Competence** is a legal determination by a court (e.g., testamentary capacity, fitness to stand trial). See also testamentary capacity: understanding the nature of a will, extent of property, claims of beneficiaries, and freedom from delusions affecting disposition (Banks v. Goodfellow, 1870) [general knowledge].

### Management: consent workflow on admission
1. Assess capacity (Section 4; MHCA guidance).
2. If present: independent admission or treatment with informed consent.
3. If absent: check for an advance directive and NR; supported admission (Section 89) if criteria are met; NR-supported consent; Board intimation.
4. Reassess capacity every 7 days (Section 89).
5. Emergency (Section 94): up to 72 hours, NR consent where available, no ECT.

### Critical appraisal
- MHCA Section 4's "**or**" wording (any one of understand, appreciate or communicate) appears to set a **low threshold**. Critics argue it is unclear and inconsistent with clinical models requiring all abilities [general knowledge; debated].
- **NR-supported consent** may become de facto family substitution in practice.
- **Resource constraints:** time for proper consent processes in busy OPDs.
- Written consent forms do not guarantee understanding, so use **teach-back**.

### Indian context
- **MHCA 2017** and **Mental Healthcare (Rights of Persons with Mental Illness) Rules 2018**.
- **ICMR National Ethical Guidelines for Biomedical and Health Research (2017):** audio-visual recording of consent for certain trials (New Drugs and Clinical Trials Rules 2019) [general knowledge].
- **Cultural factors:** family-centred decision-making; low health literacy; multiple languages.
- **Consumer Protection Act:** negligence claims for lack of consent.

### Recent advances
- **Digital and multimedia consent tools.**
- **Shared decision-making aids** for antipsychotic choice.
- **Psychiatric advance directives** research (joint crisis plans).
