## Exam answer

**Introduction.** Sexual dysfunction is common in psychiatric patients and is a **major, under-reported cause of non-adherence and poor quality of life** [1].
- **In schizophrenia**, global prevalence is **56% in men and 60% in women** (2023 meta-analysis). Only **37% report it spontaneously**, but **46%** have problems when asked directly [1].
- **In depression**, 40–50% have **reduced libido and arousal problems before treatment**, but only **15–20% have orgasm problems** before an antidepressant. Orgasmic dysfunction is therefore more likely drug-induced [1].
- **The illness itself contributes:** up to **82% of men and 96% of women** with first-episode schizophrenia report problems, and **17–70% of unmedicated** patients have dysfunction [1].
- **Treating the illness can improve sexual function.** In STAR*D, sexual dysfunction was a problem in **21% of remitters vs 61% of non-remitters** on citalopram [1].

### The human sexual response and drug mechanisms [1]
| Phase | Physiology | How drugs impair it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Desire** | Testosterone; dopamine ↑ desire; prolactin ↓ desire; psychosocial context | **D2 blockade**, **hyperprolactinaemia**, serotonin excess, sedation, weight gain |
| **Arousal** | Testosterone or oestrogen; dopamine; cholinergic/adrenergic balance; **nitric oxide** | **Anticholinergic** effects, **α1 blockade** (erection), NO inhibition |
| **Orgasm** | Oxytocin | **↑ serotonin (5-HT2)**, ↑ prolactin, α1 blockade (ejaculation; retrograde ejaculation) |
| **Resolution** | Passive | **Priapism** (α1 blockade: trazodone, chlorpromazine, thioridazine, risperidone, olanzapine) |

### Drug-specific risk [1]
**Antipsychotics:**
- Risk tracks **prolactin elevation**: **risperidone > haloperidol > olanzapine > quetiapine > aripiprazole**.
- **Prolactin explains only about 40%** of antipsychotic-related dysfunction [1].
- **High risk:**
  - **risperidone and paliperidone** (potent prolactin raisers; α1-mediated ejaculatory problems; 60–70%);
  - **amisulpride and sulpiride**;
  - haloperidol (up to 70%);
  - phenothiazines (anticholinergic, α1);
  - thioxanthenes.
- **Low risk:**
  - **aripiprazole** (it can improve function when switched to or added);
  - brexpiprazole, cariprazine;
  - **quetiapine**, lurasidone, asenapine, **lumateperone**, pimavanserin;
  - clozapine (no prolactin effect but α1 and anticholinergic).

**Antidepressants (Maudsley Table 3.17) [1]:**
- **High:** **SSRIs**, **venlafaxine**, duloxetine, **TCAs (especially clomipramine)**, MAOIs.
- **Low:**
  - **agomelatine, bupropion, vortioxetine** (best evidence; vortioxetine at 10 mg/day or less similar to placebo);
  - **mirtazapine**, moclobemide;
  - vilazodone (possibly);
  - trazodone (low overall, but priapism risk).

**Others:**
- **lithium** (modest);
- **valproate and carbamazepine** (enzyme induction lowers free testosterone);
- **benzodiazepines** (reduced libido and arousal);
- **opioids** (hypogonadism);
- **beta-blockers**;
- alcohol.

### Assessment [1]
1. **Ask directly** at baseline and follow-up. Spontaneous reports underestimate the problem.
2. **Establish a baseline before starting treatment.** Compare function on the drug with function **before the drug**, not before the illness [1].
3. **Identify the phase affected:** desire, arousal (erection, lubrication), orgasm or ejaculation, pain.
4. **Rating scales:**
   - **Arizona Sexual Experiences Scale (ASEX)**;
   - CSFQ;
   - PRSexDQ-SALSEX;
   - IIEF (men);
   - FSFI (women).
5. **Exclude other causes:**
   - **depression or psychosis** itself;
   - diabetes, hypertension, **cardiovascular disease**, obesity;
   - hypogonadism, hypothyroidism;
   - alcohol and substances;
   - other drugs (diuretics, β-blockers);
   - relationship and psychosocial factors [1].
6. **Investigations:** **prolactin**; testosterone (morning; men); TSH; glucose/HbA1c; lipids; consider a pituitary MRI if prolactin is markedly high (e.g., above 2,000–3,000 mIU/L) without an adequate drug explanation [general knowledge].

### Management (stepwise) [1]
**1. Treat the illness and other causes.** Remission of depression or psychosis often improves function. Treat comorbid physical conditions [1].

**2. Watchful waiting:** spontaneous remission occurs in about **5–10%** and can take **4–6 months**. This is the **least effective** strategy, reasonable only for mild cases [1].

**3. Dose reduction** once in remission (dose dependence is likely for antidepressants, less clear for antipsychotics) [1].

**4. Drug holidays (antidepressants):**
- skipping 1–2 doses before planned sexual activity, or halving the dose for 2 days;
- **not effective with fluoxetine** (long half-life);
- risk of **discontinuation symptoms** and relapse [1].

**5. Switch to a lower-risk drug:**
- **antidepressant:** to **bupropion, agomelatine, vortioxetine**, mirtazapine or moclobemide [1];
- **antipsychotic:** to **aripiprazole**, quetiapine, lurasidone, brexpiprazole, cariprazine [1].

**6. Add-on ("antidote") strategies:**
| Problem | Options [1] |
| --- | --- |
| **Antipsychotic hyperprolactinaemia** | **Add aripiprazole 5–10 mg** (normalises prolactin, improves function). Dopamine agonists (bromocriptine, cabergoline, amantadine) carry a **risk of psychotic relapse** |
| **Erectile dysfunction (any cause)** | **PDE-5 inhibitors (sildenafil, best evidence; tadalafil)**, effective even when prolactin is high; alprostadil |
| **SSRI-induced low libido or anorgasmia** | **Bupropion 300 mg/day** (better in women); buspirone; cyproheptadine (sedation; may reverse the antidepressant effect); mirtazapine (mixed evidence); yohimbine; **transdermal testosterone** (women with SSRI/SNRI-induced low libido; men with low testosterone) |
| **TCA anorgasmia or arousal problems** | Bethanechol before sex |
| **Augmentation in TRD** | Aripiprazole and brexpiprazole are associated with improved sexual function; **pimavanserin** add-on improved sexual functioning |
| **Hypoactive sexual desire disorder in premenopausal women** | **Flibanserin** and **bremelanotide** (FDA-approved; no data for antidepressant-induced dysfunction) |

**7. Psychological and behavioural:**
- psychoeducation;
- couple therapy;
- sensate focus;
- scheduling sex around dosing;
- sexual dysfunction clinics (engagement may be harder for people with mental illness) [1].

**8. Special issues:**
- **Priapism** is a **urological emergency** (trazodone, α1 blockers);
- **post-SSRI sexual dysfunction (PSSD)** (below).

### Post-SSRI sexual dysfunction (PSSD) [1]
- **Persistent** sexual dysfunction **after stopping** SSRIs or SNRIs:
  - low libido, erectile dysfunction, delayed ejaculation, anorgasmia, vaginal dryness;
  - **genital numbness, pleasureless orgasm**.
- Estimated risk about **1 in 216** treated patients.
- Mechanism unknown; diagnostic criteria proposed by Healy and colleagues.
- **No proven treatment** (anecdotal: serotonin antagonists, dopaminergic agents, vortioxetine). Manage with a multidisciplinary team (urology, endocrinology).
- **Counsel patients** about it, given its medicolegal and ethical importance.

### Positive uses of drug sexual effects [1]
- **Premature ejaculation:** serotonergic antidepressants (clomipramine, paroxetine), **dapoxetine** (licensed).
- **Paraphilias:** SSRIs.
- **Psychogenic erectile dysfunction:** trazodone (systematic review).

**Conclusion.** Psychotropic-induced sexual dysfunction is **common, under-asked, and a key driver of non-adherence**. Manage it by:
- **routine direct enquiry and a baseline**;
- **separating illness from drug effects**;
- then **dose reduction**, **switching** to low-risk agents (aripiprazole, bupropion, agomelatine, vortioxetine, mirtazapine);
- or **targeted add-ons** (aripiprazole for prolactin, **sildenafil** for erectile dysfunction, bupropion for SSRI-related dysfunction).

Warn patients about PSSD and priapism.

## In depth

### Background
- Early trials **under-ascertained** sexual effects (spontaneous reporting only), which underestimated rates. Studies using direct questioning show far higher figures [1].
- Sexual health is part of **quality of life and recovery** in SMI. Women with psychosis also have **reduced fertility**, often prolactin-related [1].

### Epidemiology
- **SSRI-induced dysfunction:** reported rates range from **25–80%** depending on how it is assessed [general knowledge].
- **Schizophrenia:** about 56–60% overall [1].
- **Gender differences:**
  - men report reduced desire, erectile dysfunction, premature ejaculation;
  - women report low libido and orgasmic dysfunction [1].

### Classification
- **DSM-5-TR:** **substance/medication-induced sexual dysfunction**.
- **ICD-11:** sexual dysfunctions (**HA00–HA0Z**), moved to the **"Conditions related to sexual health"** chapter, with an aetiological qualifier for "associated with medication".

### Mechanism (expanded)
- **Prolactin:** D2 blockade in the **tuberoinfundibular** pathway raises prolactin. This suppresses **GnRH**, lowering LH/FSH, testosterone and oestrogen, and so libido; it also causes amenorrhoea, galactorrhoea and **bone loss** [1].
- **Serotonin:**
  - 5-HT2 stimulation inhibits spinal ejaculatory reflexes and dopamine release;
  - 5-HT2A/2C antagonists (mirtazapine, cyproheptadine) counteract this;
  - **vortioxetine**'s multimodal action (5-HT3, 5-HT7 antagonism; 5-HT1A agonism) is thought to spare sexual function.
- **NO inhibition** (paroxetine) impairs erection.
- **Genetic susceptibility** (e.g., 5-HT2A, GNB3 polymorphisms) [1].

### Clinical features
- Patients may present with **non-adherence**, dropping out, or relationship conflict rather than a sexual complaint.

### Assessment in detail
- Sensitive, private, normalising questions.
- Use **gender-appropriate** language.
- Involve the partner with consent.

### Differential diagnosis
- Primary sexual dysfunction.
- **Depression-related anhedonia.**
- Hypogonadism.
- Vascular erectile dysfunction.
- **Prolactinoma** (prolactin much higher than expected for the drug; a visual field defect).
- Relationship problems.
- Performance anxiety.
- Substance use.

### Management details
- **Hyperprolactinaemia management** (Maudsley "Hyperprolactinaemia" section, pp. 190–194):
  - switch to a prolactin-sparing drug or add aripiprazole;
  - consider **bone density** in long-term hyperprolactinaemia [general knowledge].
- **Sildenafil dosing:** 50 mg about 1 hour before sex (range 25–100 mg). Avoid with **nitrates**; caution with α-blockers [general knowledge].

### Critical appraisal
- The **antidote evidence is poor**: small samples, short trials, confounders, varied outcome tools [1].
- **Switching** risks relapse. Weigh this against the impact on quality of life.
- **Women's sexual dysfunction** is under-studied.

### Indian context
- **Stigma and reluctance** to discuss sex (especially women, older patients, in front of family) mean clinicians must create privacy and ask directly.
- Sildenafil and tadalafil are cheap generics in India. Bupropion and vortioxetine are available; agomelatine is available [general knowledge].
- **Marital pressures** (fertility expectations) make prolactin-sparing choices important for young women with psychosis.

### Recent advances
- **PSSD** recognised by the **EMA (2019)** in SSRI/SNRI labelling [general knowledge].
- **Bremelanotide** and flibanserin for HSDD [1].
- **Pimavanserin** augmentation [1].
- **Prolactin-sparing newer antipsychotics** (lumateperone, cariprazine, brexpiprazole) [1].
