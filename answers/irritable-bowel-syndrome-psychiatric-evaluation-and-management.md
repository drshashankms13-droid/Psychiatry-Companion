## Exam answer

### Concept [1, 2]
- IBS is a **functional somatic syndrome (FSS)**: a recognised cluster of bodily symptoms attributed to **one organ system**, without structural pathology that explains the symptoms [1].
- **Classification:**
  - ICD-10 places IBS in the **gastroenterology** chapter, with research criteria (the **Rome criteria**);
  - in mental-health classifications, patients with marked psychobehavioural features may meet **DSM-5 somatic symptom disorder (SSD)** or **ICD-11 bodily distress disorder (BDD)** [1].
- **A single IBS diagnosis** fits patients with few, specific GI symptoms and no major psychobehavioural features.
- **"Complicated bodily distress"** (many symptoms across systems, more than one FSS, health anxiety) is better captured by SSD/BDD and **needs different treatment** [1].
- **DSM-5-TR:**
  - many people with IBS **do not meet SSD Criterion B** (excessive thoughts, feelings or behaviours) [2];
  - **"psychological factors affecting other medical conditions"** applies when psychological factors clearly worsen a functional syndrome such as IBS [2].
- About **half** of people with an FSS have additional symptoms in other systems, and FSS **co-occur** more than chance, sharing an **affective** (depression/anxiety) and a **sensory** (chronic widespread pain) dimension [1].

### Psychiatric comorbidity [1, 2]
- FSS such as IBS carry **higher rates of depression and anxiety** than comparable organic diseases (e.g., **inflammatory bowel disease**). But many patients have neither, so IBS is **not simply "masked" depression or anxiety** [1].
- **Personality disorders** are frequent in more severe cases [1].
- **Panic disorder** is significantly comorbid with IBS; the direction of cause is unclear [2].
- **Generalized anxiety disorder** is frequently accompanied by stress-related conditions such as IBS [2].
- The **number of bodily symptoms** predicts disability and healthcare use better than any single symptom. **Health anxiety** independently predicts healthcare use [1].

### Aetiological model [1]
- **Predisposing:**
  - (epi)genetics (up to **30%** of variance);
  - **adverse childhood experiences** (up to **4-fold** odds);
  - **anxious or avoidant attachment**, poor emotion regulation;
  - culture.
- **Triggering:** acute organic illness (e.g., **viral infection**), work stress, life events.
- **Maintaining:**
  - health anxiety, illness conviction, **avoidance and deconditioning**;
  - operant reinforcement;
  - **iatrogenic factors** (repeated investigations, inappropriate treatment, delayed diagnosis);
  - **dualistic doctor–patient communication**.
- **Perception model:** bodily distress as a **disorder of interoception**, in which overly precise top-down predictions of distress shape how bodily signals are perceived ("predictive coding"). So **both central and peripheral treatments** are needed.

### Psychiatric evaluation [1, 3]
1. **Attitude:** take the symptoms seriously; avoid "mental **or** physical" dualism; **do not equate with malingering**; slow down and listen [1].
2. **Whole symptom pattern:** screen for other bodily symptoms (**PHQ-15**), **depression and anxiety (panic, GAD)**, **suicidal ideation**, **medication or alcohol misuse** [1, 3].
3. **Beliefs and behaviour:**
   - illness beliefs, catastrophising, **health anxiety** (**Whiteley index**) [3];
   - body checking, avoidance, healthcare use;
   - expectations and motivation [1].
4. **Function**: work, relationships, quality of life.
5. **Stressors and maintaining factors:** trauma, **domestic violence**, factitious symptoms, litigation (severe cases) [1].
6. **Medical liaison:** the initial work-up finds organic disease in **up to 8%**, but on follow-up only **0.5%** of FSS diagnoses change to definite medical disease. So **avoid repetitive, risky investigations** done only to reassure [1].
7. **Grade severity** (mild, moderate, severe) and decide whether this is a single FSS or complicated bodily distress (SSD/BDD) [1].

### Management: stepped care [1, 3]

**Step 1, mild:**
- empathic relationship;
- **explain** symptoms and frame test results in a calming, non-trivialising way;
- healthy lifestyle, activity, sleep hygiene.

**Step 2, moderate:**
- a **blame-free explanation** linking physical and psychosocial mechanisms; stressors as **amplifiers**, not causes;
- build on the patient's resources;
- relaxation, graded activity, **self-help**; realistic goals;
- **symptomatic measures** (digestives) explained as temporary;
- **antidepressant** if pain or depression predominates;
- **scheduled regular appointments** rather than symptom-contingent ones [1, 3].

**Step 3, severe:**
- address trauma and maintaining factors;
- **carefully framed referral** to psychotherapy or psychiatry (while the GP or gastroenterologist keeps seeing the patient);
- liaison;
- **multidisciplinary integrated care** if outpatient care fails [1].

**Psychotherapy principles:**
- acknowledge the patient's initial organic view;
- **do not try to "reattribute"** symptoms to a purely psychological cause;
- a **symptom-context diary** (not in very high health anxiety);
- **coping and adaptation, not cure**, as the goal [1, 3].

### Evidence for IBS treatments (Table 1, composite grade: +++ strong, ++ moderate, + low, o none) [1]

| Category | Treatments and grade |
| --- | --- |
| **Peripheral drugs** | **Linaclotide (IBS-C) ++**; spasmolytics +; lubiprostone, polyethylene glycol, laxatives (IBS-C) +; **loperamide (IBS-D) +**; rifaximin +; **alosetron (5-HT3 antagonist; IBS-D) +**; cilansetron +; bulking agents o; mebeverine o; renzapride/cisapride (IBS-C) o |
| **Central drugs** | **Antidepressants +**; **tricyclics +**; SSRIs +/o |
| **Psychological/activating** | **Hypnosis ++**; psychological therapies +/++; hypnotherapy +; **CBT +**; guided self-help/psychoeducation +; mindfulness +; yoga +; minimal-contact psychological treatment +/o |
| **Passive physical** | Manipulative therapy + |
| **Diet/complementary** | **Fibre, peppermint oil +**; fibre +; **restricted diet +**; probiotics o/+; Lactobacillus +; pre/synbiotics o; Chinese herbal medicine (IBS-D) +; acupuncture o/+ |

**Overall:**
- most IBS drug trials target bowel function, with **low or no evidence** for most;
- **low-level evidence for antidepressants**;
- **psychological and activating therapies** (CBT, hypnotherapy, mindfulness, yoga) show **low to moderate** efficacy;
- across FSS, **active, patient-involving, centrally acting** treatments do better than **passive, peripheral** ones [1].

### Update from Levenson 2019: Rome IV, pathophysiology and treatment evidence [4]
**Rome IV criteria (as summarised by Levenson):**
- **recurrent abdominal pain related to defecation**, with a **change in stool form or frequency**;
- pain **at least once a week** during the **last 3 months**, with onset **at least 6 months** before diagnosis;
- **no alarm features** (fever, weight loss, rectal bleeding).

**Subtypes:** **IBS-D**, **IBS-C**, mixed.

**Associated symptoms:** bloating, distension, urgency, straining, incomplete evacuation, mucus.

**Comorbidity:** GERD, functional dyspepsia, pelvic pain, **CFS**, **fibromyalgia**, TMJ, interstitial cystitis, headache, anxiety, depression, sleep disturbance.

**Epidemiology:**
- Global prevalence is **10–15%**: highest in South America (21%), **lowest in South Asia (7%)**.
- **Women are affected 1.5–3 times more**, **except in South Asia**, South America and Africa.
- Commoner **under 50**.
- Only **30% seek care** (80% of these have IBS-D).
- It is the **commonest gastroenterology outpatient diagnosis**. Quality of life is as poor as in asthma, migraine and IBD (even diabetes or ESRD), and patients miss 3–4 extra work days a year.

**Pathophysiology** (no single abnormality is specific to all IBS):
- **Brain–gut axis** (bidirectional CNS–enteric nervous system).
- Altered **motility**; **visceral hypersensitivity** (symptom severity rises with balloon-distension sensitivity, **independent of anxiety and depression**).
- Intestinal permeability; **altered microbiota**; inflammation.
- **Serotonin:** high plasma 5-HT in IBS-D, low in IBS-C.
- Autonomic dysfunction; genetics.
- **Diet:** **FODMAPs** (fermentable, osmotic, H2 and methane), so a **low-FODMAP diet** reduces symptoms in RCTs (long-term safety unknown).
- **Psychological:**
  - **trauma** history in **26–44%** of referred FGID patients;
  - **depression and anxiety each double the risk** of developing IBS;
  - illness behaviour, sleep problems and somatic symptoms predict onset;
  - **life events and hypochondriasis predict post-infectious IBS**;
  - fMRI shows defective visceral pain processing and fear of pain.

**Psychiatric comorbidity:**
- About **50%** have clinically relevant psychiatric symptoms; lifetime psychiatric disorder **38–100%** (**over 90%** in studies not excluding them).
- **By disorder:**
  - **depression 6–70%** (commonest);
  - **anxiety 5–50%**;
  - DSM-IV somatisation 15–47%;
  - **panic 0–41%**;
  - trauma-related 8–36%.
- Increased **bipolar disorder** (meta-analysis).
- **Bidirectional:** IBS in **17–47% with panic**, 17–37% with GAD, 17–59% with depression.
- Psychiatric disorders **often predate** IBS. They predict **more severe symptoms, impairment and worse prognosis**.
- **Catastrophising and neuroticism** are raised. Psychological factors correlate with severity better than somatic ones.

**Psychological treatment:**
- **CBT** is **first line**, with strong evidence: **NNT 3**. Components: psychoeducation on stress and gut symptoms, insight into cognitive and behavioural responses and fear of symptoms, and distress-reduction skills. Benefits persist; PET suggests **reduced visceral hypervigilance**.
- **Gut-directed hypnotherapy** (North Carolina protocol or **Manchester** approach): **NNT 4**, with long-term benefit **equal to a low-FODMAP diet**.
- **Psychodynamic therapy:** **NNT 3.5**; equal to antidepressants and better than usual care; useful with **trauma history**.
- Mindfulness, relaxation and stress management have also been trialled.

**Antidepressants:**
- **Rationale:** comorbidity, **central pain modulation**, and effects on visceral sensitivity and motility. It is not established whether mood improvement explains the response.
- **TCAs** (low-dose imipramine, amitriptyline, desipramine, doxepin, trimipramine):
  - **NNT 4**; over 740 patients pooled;
  - **NNH 9** (drowsiness, dry mouth); high dropout.
- **SSRIs:** NNT 4 in one meta-analysis but heterogeneous; another found **no benefit**; a third found **insufficient evidence without psychiatric comorbidity**.
  - **Paroxetine:** improved well-being but not pain.
  - **Fluoxetine 20 mg:** one positive RCT in **IBS-C**, one negative.
  - **Citalopram:** mixed.
  - SSRIs are **better tolerated**, so use them if other treatment fails.
- **Subtype logic:** **TCAs for IBS-D** (constipating) and **SSRIs for IBS-C**, though this is theoretical with few data.
- **SNRIs:** small open-label studies only.

**Related Rome IV category:** **centrally mediated abdominal pain syndrome (CAPS)**:
- near-continuous pain **unrelated to food, defecation or menses**;
- prevalence 0.5–2%; women affected about twice as often;
- comorbid fibromyalgia or CFS; history of loss or abuse;
- treat with education, collaboration and psychological therapy.

⚠️ **Discrepancies (not resolved):**
- **Antidepressants:** Henningsen [1] grades antidepressants and TCAs as **low (+)** and **SSRIs +/o**. Levenson [4] reports **NNT 4** for TCAs and SSRIs but conflicting SSRI meta-analyses.
- **CBT:** Henningsen grades **CBT +** (low); Levenson calls the evidence **strong**, with NNT 3. **Hypnosis:** Henningsen gives ++; Levenson gives NNT 4.

**Conclusion.** IBS is a functional somatic syndrome with frequent but not universal anxiety (especially panic disorder and GAD), depression and, in severe cases, personality disorder. When accompanied by multiple symptoms and health anxiety, it shades into somatic symptom/bodily distress disorder. Psychiatric evaluation:
- the whole symptom pattern (PHQ-15), mood and anxiety, health anxiety (Whiteley index), beliefs, function and maintaining stressors;
- avoiding unnecessary repeat investigation (only 0.5% are later reclassified as organic).

Management is stepped and biopsychosocial:
- explanation and lifestyle;
- symptomatic gut treatment (linaclotide for IBS-C, loperamide or alosetron for IBS-D; fibre or peppermint oil);
- antidepressants (TCAs) for pain or comorbid depression;
- CBT and hypnotherapy;
- multidisciplinary care for severe cases, with coping rather than cure as the aim.
