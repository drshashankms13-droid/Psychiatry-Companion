## Exam answer

### Introduction [1]
- **Commonest sporadic encephalitis** in the Western world: incidence **1 in 250,000 to 1 in 1,000,000 per year**. Acute encephalitis overall is 3.5–7.4 per 100,000 per year.
- **Severe:** **untreated mortality about 70%**, with half of survivors left with sequelae. With acyclovir, mortality is **about 20%**.
- **Bimodal age:** a third under 20 years; **half over 50** (probably reactivation of latent virus); 37% of encephalitis at age 60–65. No seasonal or sex pattern.
- **Seroprevalence:** antibodies in **80–90% of adults**. Proof of causation came from **Cowdry type A inclusions** and viral isolation from brain (Smith 1941).
- **Psychiatric importance:** marked psychological disturbance in the acute phase and as a major sequel. **Up to 20% present misleadingly**.

### Pathology [1]
- Perivascular lymphocytes and histiocytes, microglial proliferation, glial nodules.
- **Severe haemorrhagic necrosis**, often **asymmetrical**, with a predilection for the **medial temporal and orbitofrontal** regions (the limbic system).
- Explains "acute necrotising", "inclusion body" and "haemorrhagic" encephalitis.
- Viral antigen can be shown on early biopsy (immunofluorescence/immunoperoxidase).

### Clinical features [1]
**General encephalitic picture:**
- flu-like prodrome, then **headache, fever** (91% high fever on admission), nausea and vomiting, irritability, photophobia, neck stiffness;
- disturbed consciousness from somnolence to coma; **disorientation 76%**, **behavioural change 41%**;
- **seizures** (all ages).

**HSV-associated features:**
- **focal** encephalitis (weakness, sensory signs, **aphasia/dysphasia**, field defects, cranial nerve palsies) more than diffuse;
- **temporal lobe signs strongly suggest HSV**;
- herpes labialis history is uncommon;
- no feature reliably distinguishes HSV from other encephalitides.

**Psychiatric presentations:**
- **early behavioural aberrations** while only mildly unwell (Drachman & Adams, 5 of 6 cases): packing a case a week early, dressing at night for an imagined funeral, **failing to recognise a spouse**, bizarre sudden actions;
- a **delirious phase** before coma, with **vivid hallucinations resembling delirium tremens**;
- restless **hyperactivity** on recovery;
- **anosmia**, **olfactory and gustatory hallucinations** and **disproportionate memory disturbance** (temporal and orbitofrontal);
- encephalitis presenting with **delusions (54%), hallucinations (auditory 44%)**, depression (21%): Caroff's review. Cases admitted as **schizophrenia** were probably mostly HSV;
- rare: temporal **mass-like presentation**, benign aseptic meningitis, **recurrent organic psychosis** with herpes labialis.

### Investigations [1]
1. **CT before LP** (safety). CT may be normal early, later showing **low-density temporal lobe(s)**, mass effect, enhancement. It also excludes abscess or tumour.
2. **MRI** is **more sensitive early**. **Cingulate gyrus plus contralateral temporal involvement is extremely suggestive**.
3. **CSF:** exclude other infections. **HSV PCR: sensitivity 98%, specificity 99%**; distinguishes recurrent infection from post-infectious immune disease. Intrathecal antibody synthesis is retrospective only (takes weeks).
4. **EEG:** early non-specific slowing, later **periodic lateralised epileptiform discharges (temporal)**. **Repeat** if needed (findings may be transient).
5. **Brain biopsy:** the former gold standard (Cowdry A inclusions); risky, and a negative result does not exclude HSV; now rarely needed.
6. Rising serum antibody titres: retrospective support only.

### Differential diagnosis [1]
- **Tumour or abscess** (temporal mass).
- **Subdural haematoma or head injury** (low fever, asymmetric signs).
- **Meningitis** (fulminant).
- **Delirium tremens** (vivid hallucinations).
- **Wernicke's encephalopathy** (drowsiness, confabulation, fits).
- **Primary psychosis** (behavioural onset, few signs).
- End-state: **Korsakoff's syndrome** or **neurosyphilis**.

### Management [1]
1. **Urgent treatment.** **Start acyclovir as soon as possible** on strong clinical, imaging and CSF suspicion, without waiting for confirmation. *(Dose and duration: not given in the source.)*
2. **Immunocompromised patients:** acyclovir resistance occurs; **foscarnet** is the alternative.
3. **Control seizures.**
4. **Raised ICP:** **corticosteroids and mannitol** (benefit still under study); **surgical decompression** where appropriate.
5. **Prognosis:** best predicted by **time to treatment**; also age, consciousness at presentation, viral load, duration. Cognitive outcome is predicted by the duration of **transient encephalitic amnesia** (analogous to PTA).
6. **Psychiatric management:** delirium and agitation in the acute phase; later psychiatric sequelae (see below).
7. **Follow-up neuropsychology and counselling** even after an "excellent" recovery.

### Sequelae [1]
- **Cognitive** (2–4 times the risk of other viral encephalitides):
  - **dense anterograde amnesia in 25–75%** ± retrograde amnesia (a Korsakoff-like picture; medial temporal atrophy with thalamus relatively spared);
  - **dysexecutive features in about 40%**;
  - **category-specific anomia** or semantic impairment;
  - dementia in adults and intellectual disability in children;
  - dysphasia;
  - even "good recoveries" show language and memory deficits.
- **Behavioural:**
  - **personality and behavioural disturbance in 45%**;
  - **Klüver–Bucy-like** behaviour;
  - apathy, emotional lability;
  - rarely **schizophreniform psychosis**, sometimes long after discharge.
- **Seizures.**
- **Encephalitis Society survivors:** frustration and anger 68%, anxiety 67%, mood swings 59%, depression 58%.

**Conclusion.** HSV encephalitis is the commonest sporadic encephalitis: a necrotising, asymmetric medial temporal and orbitofrontal infection. It may present with **behavioural change, psychosis or delirium** before focal signs (dysphasia, seizures). Diagnosis rests on early **MRI**, **CSF HSV PCR** (98%/99%) and EEG (temporal PLEDs). **Acyclovir must be started immediately on suspicion**, with seizure and ICP control. Mortality has fallen from 70% to 20%, but **amnesia (25–75%), dysexecutive syndrome, semantic deficits and behavioural change (45%)** are common, so rehabilitation and long-term psychiatric follow-up are required.
