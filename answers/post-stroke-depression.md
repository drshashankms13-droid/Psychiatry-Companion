> ⚠️ **Discrepancies flagged, not resolved:**
> - **Treatment efficacy.** Lishman (Hackett 2004 review): no strong evidence that antidepressants increase **remission**, though rating-scale scores improve; the effect is smaller than in primary depression. Maudsley (2025): antidepressants are "**clearly effective**" and treatment should not usually be withheld.
> - **Prophylaxis.** Lishman: evidence does **not justify routine** prophylaxis. Maudsley: pooled data suggest a prophylactic effect, but **routine prophylaxis is still not recommended**; fluoxetine's risks may outweigh the benefit.
> - **Prevalence.** Lishman: about **one-third** (pooled). Maudsley: **at least 30–40%**.
>
> **Gaps:** DSM-5-TR "depressive disorder due to another medical condition" criteria, specific screening tools (PHQ-9, HADS cut-offs, aphasia-friendly scales), the AHA 2017 scientific statement, and rTMS/ECT data for PSD are **not in these sources**.

## Exam answer

### Introduction [1, 2]
- **Post-stroke depression (PSD)** is the commonest psychiatric sequel of stroke.
- **Prevalence:**
  - **about one-third** have significant depressive symptoms, in community, hospital or rehabilitation samples (Hackett & Anderson 2005) [1];
  - **at least 30–40%** [2];
  - one-third to half are depressed within the first year [1].
- **Comparisons:** stroke patients are more depressed than age-matched, orthopaedic or TBI controls with equal disability (Folstein 1977; Robinson & Szetela 1981), but not more than post-MI survivors (Aben 2003) [1].
- **Bidirectional:** depression also **increases the risk of stroke** [1, 2].

### Aetiology (Box 8.5; RCP 2005) [1]
1. **Psychological reaction** to sudden disability:
   - fear and isolation, especially with communication problems;
   - frustration, dependency, the invalid role;
   - loss of job, status and finances.
2. **Direct biological effect** of the brain lesion.
3. **Pre-existing vulnerability** (previous depression).

**Biological vs social over time:** biological factors are probably most important **in the early weeks**; **social and interpersonal factors** dominate later (Nys 2005).

### Risk factors [1]
- **Stroke severity** (physical disability and cognitive impairment): the best predictor.
- **Social isolation** and adverse social factors.
- **Premorbid personality:**
  - striving, self-sufficient people react worse;
  - previous anxious or depressive reactions to stress;
  - **neuroticism** (NEO-FFI, Aben 2006).
- Lack of family support (spouse dead or infirm, children moved away). Religious belief may protect.
- **Lesion laterality is unresolved:**
  - Robinson: **left anterior** lesions;
  - Carson 2000 systematic review: no effect;
  - Bhogal 2004: left-sided effect only in **early/hospital** samples;
  - recent studies: no effect.
- Age, previous depression and stroke subtype are inconsistent across studies.

### Course [1]
- Rates are similar early and late (cross-sectional data).
- Early depression often **remits within the first year**, but **new cases arise months later**.
- Some longitudinal studies show a decline over 1–2 years.

### Clinical features and diagnosis [1]
- Compared with primary depression: **more physical symptoms, less melancholia**.
- **Mimics (Box 8.5):**
  - **apathy** and **emotionalism**;
  - **somatic symptoms** of stroke or hospitalisation (low energy, appetite and libido; sleep change; poor concentration);
  - **abnormal expression** (facial immobility, flat speech, slowness).
- Somatic symptoms are not necessarily confounders: depressed patients endorse fatigue and sleep disturbance much more (Williams 2005).
- **Masked** by stoicism or insidious onset; **missed in aphasia**.
- **Differentiate from:**
  - simple loss of confidence;
  - the emotional lability of intellectual impairment;
  - **realistic resignation** in very frail patients ("their wish 'not to bother' deserves to be treated with proper respect");
  - **abulia** from left frontal lesions (improves in hospital, relapses at home: Förstl & Sahakian).

### Consequences [1, 2]
- **Suicidal thoughts in about 10%**; **suicide risk about doubled**.
- Poor motivation and cooperation with rehabilitation; slower functional recovery [2].
- Worse outcomes; more healthcare use; **carer depression**.
- **Increased mortality** over subsequent years.
- Deteriorating depression predicts dependence at 15 months (Pohjasvaara).
- PSD often goes **untreated** [2].

### Emotionalism (post-stroke pathological crying) [1, 2]
- **Definition:** increased crying (rarely laughing) with little warning and **inability to control** it, usually triggered by **sad or sentimental stimuli**.
- **Frequency:** **21% at 6 months, 11% at 12 months** (House 1989). Onset at 4–6 weeks; improves over a year.
- **Associations:** more depression, cognitive impairment, larger lesions; **left anterior** lesions are more persistent; irritability, ideas of reference, intrusive thoughts.
- **Traditional view:** pseudobulbar palsy (bilateral corticobulbar lesions), but it is broader than that.
- **Treatment:**
  - TCAs (Lawson & MacLeod) and SSRIs work even without depression; the systematic review (House 2004) found large but inconsistent effects. Most clinicians try an **SSRI** [1];
  - **citalopram, nortriptyline, sertraline**: rapid response; **dextromethorphan–quinidine** effective (FDA-approved); dextromethorphan–fluoxetine; valproate [2].

### Prevention [1, 2]
- **Antidepressant prophylaxis:**
  - sertraline (Rasmussen 2003; 50% dropout) positive; sertraline (Almeida 2006) negative; mirtazapine (Niedermaier, unblinded) positive;
  - Cochrane (Hackett 2005): **insufficient evidence**; possible role in **high-risk** patients [1];
  - Maudsley: nortriptyline, fluoxetine, escitalopram, duloxetine, mirtazapine and sertraline appear preventive, but **routine prophylaxis is not recommended**. Fluoxetine carries risks of fractures, falls and seizures. Individual cases may be justified [2].
- **Psychotherapy:** a small effect on GHQ distress but no effect on depression rates [1].
- **Exercise programme:** depression 14% vs 35% at 3 months; not significant at 9 months (Lai 2006) [1].

### Treatment [1, 2]
**1. Assess** severity, suicide risk, cognition and aphasia; distinguish the mimics above.

**2. Antidepressants:**

| Drug | Notes [2] |
| --- | --- |
| **SSRIs** (fluoxetine, **citalopram/escitalopram**, sertraline; paroxetine in network MAs) | Most studied; few pharmacokinetic interactions. **Bleeding risk with aspirin or anticoagulants**: add a **PPI**. **Caution after haemorrhagic stroke**. With **warfarin or DOACs**, prefer **citalopram/escitalopram** at the lowest dose. Risks: falls, fractures, seizures. SSRIs may protect against embolic and provoke haemorrhagic stroke (weak evidence) |
| **Nortriptyline** | Effective; **does not increase bleeding**: an alternative when bleeding risk is high. TCAs are contraindicated more often overall |
| **Mirtazapine** | Effective (ranked first in a Chinese network MA). A cohort study linked mirtazapine and venlafaxine to a higher risk of new stroke |
| **Agomelatine** | Better tolerated; no effect on clotting |
| **Vortioxetine** | Possible cognitive benefit; no warfarin or aspirin interaction; one pilot study only |
| **Reboxetine** | Doubtful overall effect |
| **Mianserin** | Ineffective |
| **Central post-stroke pain** | Amitriptyline or duloxetine |

- **Box 3.3 recommended:** SSRIs, mirtazapine, nortriptyline [2].
- **Lishman's pragmatic stance** [1]:
  - antidepressants improve mood but less than in primary depression, and may increase **anxiety**;
  - SSRIs have few side effects, so a **closely monitored trial** is reasonable even in possible depression (e.g., poor motivation);
  - have a **clear protocol to stop** if there is no benefit.
- **Possible extra benefits** (cognition, motor recovery, mortality): Maudsley cites these [2]; Lishman considers it **too early to be convinced** [1].

**3. Psychological treatment** [1]:
- CBT by a community psychiatric nurse (Lincoln & Flannaghan 2003): **no effect**;
- **care management** ("Activate–Initiate–Monitor") reduced depression at 12 weeks, largely through better antidepressant adherence;
- antidepressants are best given **within a clear management plan** (RCP 2005 guidance).

**4. Rehabilitation and social support** [1]:
- motivation and team encouragement;
- motivational interviewing improved mood at 3 months;
- family work, social worker, discharge planning.

**5. Carers** [1]:
- **about 40% of carers are depressed**;
- problem-solving training reduced carer depression but not burden.

**Conclusion.** Depression affects about a third of stroke survivors and arises from reaction, lesion and vulnerability. Its strongest predictors are stroke severity and social isolation. Laterality is disputed. It doubles suicide risk, impairs rehabilitation and raises mortality. Diagnosis must separate it from apathy, emotionalism, somatic stroke symptoms and realistic resignation, and must not miss it in aphasia. Treatment:
- **SSRIs (citalopram/escitalopram with anticoagulants; a PPI with antiplatelets), mirtazapine or nortriptyline**, as a monitored trial within a care plan;
- rehabilitation, social support and carer support;
- prophylaxis only for selected high-risk patients.

Lishman and Maudsley differ on how effective antidepressants are (see the flag).
