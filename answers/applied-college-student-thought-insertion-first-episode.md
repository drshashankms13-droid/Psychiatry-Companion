## Exam answer

**Case summary.** A 19-year-old female college student is brought by her **roommate** with **6 months** of strange behaviour. For **1 month** she has described **another person's thoughts entering her mind (thought insertion)**. Her **grades are falling**, she **talks less (poverty of speech)**, and her speech **strays from the topic and is hard to follow (derailment/tangentiality)**. She says a **television reporter told her the government has a special message for her** and she must listen to the radio for instructions (**delusion of reference** with possible auditory hallucinations).

**Provisional diagnosis: first-episode schizophrenia** — delusions (thought insertion, reference), disorganised speech and negative symptoms, with functional decline, for 6 months including at least 1 month of active symptoms (meets **DSM-5**; **ICD-11** requires only 1 month) [1]. Organic, substance-induced and mood disorders must be excluded first.

### 1. Further clarifications needed in the history

**Psychopathology**
- **Thought insertion:** does she experience the thoughts as **alien, placed in her mind by an outside agency** (a Schneiderian first-rank symptom), or as her own intrusive thoughts (obsessions)? [1]
- Other **first-rank symptoms**: thought withdrawal and broadcasting, voices commenting or arguing, made feelings/impulses/actions, somatic passivity, delusional perception [1].
- **TV reporter:** did she **hear the reporter's voice** speaking to her (hallucination) or interpret an ordinary broadcast as personal (delusion of reference)? Any commands? Does she plan to act on "instructions"?
- Persecutory, grandiose, religious delusions; other hallucinations.
- **Disorganisation:** examples of speech; odd behaviour; self-neglect.
- **Negative symptoms:** avolition, anhedonia, blunted affect, social withdrawal [1].
- **Mood:** depression, suicidal thoughts; elation, reduced need for sleep, increased activity (mania).
- **Insight** and her own explanation of experiences.

**Course**
- Mode of onset (acute vs insidious), precipitants (exams, relationship, leaving home, trauma), progression; **prodromal** changes before the 6 months.
- Sleep, appetite, menstruation (pregnancy test before treatment), weight.

**Substances**
- **Cannabis**, alcohol, stimulants, hallucinogens, synthetic drugs (common in college settings); timing relative to symptoms.

**Medical history**
- Fever, headache, seizures, abnormal movements, recent infection (**autoimmune encephalitis** — young women, psychosis, seizures, dyskinesias), head injury, thyroid symptoms, SLE features, medications (steroids, isotretinoin).

**Background**
- Family history of psychosis, bipolar disorder, suicide.
- Premorbid personality, academic record, friendships, developmental history.
- Social support: family contact details and relationship; who knows; financial and accommodation situation.

**Risk**
- Suicide, self-harm, acting on "government instructions", wandering, vulnerability to sexual or financial exploitation, aggression.

### 2. Differential diagnosis

| Diagnosis | For | Against / check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Schizophrenia (first episode)** | Thought insertion, delusions of reference, disorganised speech, negative symptoms, functional decline, 6 months | Exclude organic, substances, mood disorder [1] |
| **Schizophreniform disorder** (DSM-5) | If total duration were under 6 months | Here 6 months already [1] |
| **Acute and transient psychotic disorder** | Sudden onset, polymorphic, within 2 weeks | Gradual 6-month course [1] |
| **Schizoaffective disorder** | If prominent mood episodes run concurrently | Ask about mood [1] |
| **Bipolar disorder / psychotic depression** | Psychosis with mood episode | Mood symptoms dominate, mood-congruent delusions |
| **Substance-induced psychotic disorder** | Cannabis, stimulants common in students | Temporal relation, urine screen, resolution with abstinence |
| **Psychosis due to a medical condition** | Anti-NMDAR encephalitis, temporal lobe epilepsy, SLE, thyroid disease, Wilson's disease, HIV, syphilis, brain tumour | Neurological signs, seizures, fever, catatonia, abnormal investigations |
| **Delusional disorder** | — | Thought disorder and negative symptoms argue against |
| **Schizotypal disorder** | Odd beliefs | No frank delusions or decline |
| **OCD** | Intrusive thoughts | Thoughts recognised as own and resisted |
| **Dissociative/trance states** | Culturally shaped | Episodic, context-bound |

### 3. Ethical aspects

1. **Autonomy and capacity:** she is an **adult**; her consent is needed for assessment and treatment unless she lacks **capacity** (MHCA 2017, Section 4 — ability to understand, appreciate consequences and communicate a decision). Capacity is **decision-specific** and must be assessed and documented.
2. **Confidentiality:** information cannot be shared with the roommate, college or parents without her consent. **Exceptions:** serious risk to herself or others, or when she lacks capacity and a nominated representative must be involved (MHCA Section 23).
3. **Involving the family:** families are central to care in India; seek her permission to contact parents; if she lacks capacity, her **nominated representative** (default order under Section 14 — usually a relative) is involved.
4. **Admission:** independent admission (Section 86) if she has capacity and agrees; **supported admission (Section 89)** if she lacks capacity, has a severe mental illness and risk of harm to self or others or inability to care for herself; **emergency treatment (Section 94)** for up to 72 hours only to prevent death or serious harm.
5. **Beneficence and non-maleficence:** start treatment early (long DUP worsens outcome) while minimising adverse effects; respect informed consent for antipsychotics (explain weight gain, menstrual disturbance, sexual side effects).
6. **Right to education and non-discrimination:** college must not expel or exclude her because of mental illness; reasonable accommodation under the **RPwD Act 2016**; certificates should state only what is necessary.
7. **Role of the roommate:** acknowledge her concern, but she is not entitled to information; thank her and give general guidance.
8. **Women's safety:** assess vulnerability to exploitation; ensure a female staff member or chaperone during physical examination; pregnancy test before treatment.
9. **Advance directive and nominated representative:** once she recovers capacity, offer to record an advance directive and choose a nominated representative for future episodes.

### 4. Approach to management

**Step 1 — Setting and safety**
- Outpatient care if she accepts treatment, has family support and low risk; **admission** if there is high risk (acting on instructions, suicide), severe disorganisation, no support, or need for observation and work-up.

**Step 2 — Investigations (first episode)**
- CBC, glucose/HbA1c, lipids, LFT, renal function, electrolytes, calcium, TSH, B12, HIV, VDRL, **urine drug screen**, **pregnancy test**, prolactin baseline, ECG, weight/BMI/waist/BP [2].
- **MRI brain** and **EEG** if neurological features, atypical course or poor response; **CSF with anti-NMDAR antibodies** if red flags (seizures, dyskinesia, catatonia, autonomic instability, reduced consciousness).

**Step 3 — Pharmacological treatment** [2]
- Agree the drug choice with her (and family with consent); if not possible, start a **second-generation antipsychotic available as a long-acting injection**.
- **Start at the first-episode minimum effective dose**: e.g. **aripiprazole 10 mg**, **risperidone 2 mg**, **olanzapine 5 mg**, **paliperidone 3 mg** [2].
- For a young woman, consider **prolactin** (risperidone, paliperidone, amisulpride → amenorrhoea, galactorrhoea, bone loss) and **weight** (olanzapine). Aripiprazole is often a good first choice.
- Assess over **2–3 weeks**; increase dose or switch if no effect; if partial response continue to about **10 weeks** [2].
- Short-term **benzodiazepine** (lorazepam) for agitation or insomnia rather than antipsychotic PRN [2].
- Two failed adequate trials → **clozapine** [2].
- Consider switching to an **LAI** before discharge for relapse prevention [2].
- Maintenance for at least **1–2 years** after a first episode; taper slowly (hyperbolic reduction) if stopping [2].
- Monitor weight, BMI, glucose, lipids at baseline, 3 months and annually; prolactin if symptoms [2].

**Step 4 — Psychosocial interventions**
- **Psychoeducation** for her and her family: nature of illness, treatment, early warning signs.
- **Family intervention** — reduces relapse.
- **CBT for psychosis** for distressing delusions and to improve adherence.
- **Supported education:** with consent, work with the college for leave, exam rescheduling and a graded return; later **IPS** for employment.
- Social skills training; cognitive remediation if cognitive deficits persist.
- Address substance use if present.

**Step 5 — Follow-up**
- Early intervention service or close follow-up (weekly at first) with case management; relapse-prevention plan; suicide-risk review at each visit.
- Plan for the **critical period** (first 2–5 years).

**Conclusion.** This is most likely a first episode of schizophrenia in a young woman whose prodrome has already affected her studies. After excluding organic, substance and mood causes, management combines a low-dose SGA chosen with her, early consideration of an LAI, family and psychological interventions, and educational support. Throughout, her autonomy, capacity, confidentiality and right to education must be respected under MHCA 2017 and the RPwD Act 2016.

## In depth

### Background

- **Kurt Schneider** proposed first-rank symptoms, including thought insertion, as highly suggestive of schizophrenia in the absence of organic disease. Later research showed they are **neither specific nor necessary**: they occur in mania and other psychoses, and DSM-5 removed the special weight given to bizarre delusions and certain hallucinations [1].
- **Bleuler** emphasised loosening of associations (thought disorder) as fundamental; this patient's derailment fits his description [1].

### Epidemiology

- Peak onset in women is slightly later than in men (mid- to late 20s) with a second peak around menopause; women tend to have better premorbid function and a more favourable course (author's knowledge).
- Clinical high-risk and first-episode services report high rates of distress, suicide risk and victimisation early in illness: annual violent victimisation in adults with psychotic disorders is about 16%, nearly five times the general population rate [1].

### Classification and criteria

| Criterion | DSM-5 | ICD-10 / ICD-11 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Symptoms | 2 or more of: delusions, hallucinations, disorganised speech, disorganised/catatonic behaviour, negative symptoms (one must be from the first three) | ICD-11: 2 or more core symptoms, at least one being persistent delusions, hallucinations, thought disorder or experiences of influence/passivity/control |
| Duration | **6 months** including at least 1 month active phase; prodrome and residual phases count | **1 month**; prodrome not counted [1] |
| Function | Decline required | Not required [1] |
| Mood exclusion | Mood episodes brief relative to psychosis | Psychotic symptoms before mood episode allowed [1] |

**Delusions of reference** vs **ideas of reference:** DSM-5 distinguishes them, but K&S notes the distinction is of little clinical value and the terms are often used interchangeably [1].

### Mechanism in detail

- **Thought insertion** is understood as a failure of **self-monitoring** (efference copy): internally generated thoughts are not tagged as self-produced, so they are experienced as alien.
- **Delusions of reference** reflect **aberrant salience**: dysregulated mesolimbic dopamine assigns significance to neutral events (a TV broadcast), which the person explains with a delusion.
- **Disorganised speech** is linked to semantic network and executive dysfunction; negative symptoms and cognitive decline reflect prefrontal dysfunction.

(Mechanism summary from the author's knowledge, consistent with the dopamine and cognitive models described in K&S Section 12.)

### Clinical features in detail

- **Derailment** (loosening of associations): ideas slip to unrelated topics; **tangentiality**: replies off the point; **poverty of speech** is a negative symptom, whereas poverty of content is a disorganisation feature [1].
- In young people, early signs often include falling grades, withdrawal, irritability and odd preoccupations before overt psychosis [1].

### Assessment in detail

| Tool | Use |
| --- | --- |
| PANSS | Severity of positive, negative and general symptoms [1] |
| Calgary Depression Scale | Depression in schizophrenia |
| Columbia Suicide Severity Rating Scale | Suicide risk |
| MacArthur Competence Assessment Tool for Treatment (MacCAT-T) | Structured capacity assessment |
| Cognitive screening (MoCA, BACS) | Baseline cognition [1] |

### Management details

- **Minimum effective doses (first episode):** aripiprazole 10 mg, haloperidol 2 mg, olanzapine 5 mg, risperidone 2 mg, paliperidone 3 mg, quetiapine about 150 mg, lurasidone 40 mg HCl, amisulpride about 300 mg [2].
- **FGAs** should probably be reserved for second- or third-line use (higher TD risk, possibly poorer outcome) [2].
- **Olanzapine**, because of slight superiority in several analyses, should probably be tried before clozapine if there is prior failure [2].
- **Adherence check before labelling resistance:** use an LAI or plasma drug levels; non-adherence is often undetected [2].
- **Maintenance:** relapse after stopping is very common — in one older series almost 80% at 1 year and 98% at 2 years; a Swedish population study (2019) showed lower hospitalisation the longer treatment continued [2].
- **Contraception and pregnancy planning:** discuss early; valproate is not relevant here, but prolactin-raising drugs affect fertility.

### Evidence table

| Evidence | Finding |
| --- | --- |
| Maudsley first-episode algorithm | Response usually apparent within 2–3 weeks; continue 10 weeks if partial [2] |
| First-episode relapse after discontinuation | Large majority relapse within 1–2 years [2] |
| LAIs in first episode | Lower relapse and readmission; acceptable to patients [2] |
| Early clozapine | More likely to succeed than any alternative after two failures [2] |

### Critical appraisal

- **First-rank symptoms** are not pathognomonic; overreliance on them can misdiagnose mania or organic psychosis as schizophrenia [1].
- **DSM-5 vs ICD duration:** under ICD this would have been diagnosable after 1 month of active symptoms; under DSM-5 the 6-month rule includes prodrome, which affects research comparability [1].
- **Capacity in psychosis** is often assumed absent; studies show many patients retain decision-making capacity, and assessment should be formal and documented.
- **Confidentiality vs family involvement** is a recurring tension in India, where families expect to be informed; MHCA 2017 strengthens the patient's rights.

### Indian context

- **MHCA 2017:** Sections 4 (capacity), 5 (advance directive), 14 (nominated representative), 18–28 (rights including confidentiality, Section 23), 86 (independent admission), 89 (supported admission), 94 (emergency treatment).
- **RPwD Act 2016:** mental illness is a specified disability; educational institutions must provide reasonable accommodation.
- College counselling services and the **Tele-MANAS** helpline (14416) can support students; many Indian universities now have wellness centres.
- Families from other towns may need to be contacted urgently; financial and accommodation needs of a student living away from home must be addressed.
- Stigma about marriage prospects for young women with schizophrenia is a major family concern; psychoeducation and careful confidentiality matter.

### Recent advances

> From the author's knowledge, not the uploaded sources — check before marking `reviewed`.

- **Xanomeline–trospium** (FDA 2024) — a non-D2 antipsychotic with fewer motor and prolactin effects; not yet widely available in India.
- **Coordinated specialty care** (RAISE-ETP, NAVIGATE) improves outcomes in first-episode psychosis, especially with short DUP.
- Routine screening for **anti-NMDAR antibodies** in atypical first-episode psychosis is increasingly recommended.
