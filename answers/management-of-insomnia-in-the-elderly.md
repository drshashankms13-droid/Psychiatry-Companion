## Exam answer

### Normal sleep changes with age [1]
- **Total sleep time:** about 8 hours in young adults falls to about **6 hours at 90**.
- **More night-time awakenings**; **stage 4 (slow-wave) sleep** halves by 30 and is largely absent by 60.
- **REM** stays about 22% (preserved REM correlates with preserved intelligence; reduced in dementia).
- Insomnia frequency and severity **increase with age**; women complain twice as often as men.
- **Normal age-related deterioration may itself explain much insomnia in older people** (Regelstein).

### Causes in the elderly [1, 2]
- **Medical:** pain (arthritis, ulcer), **nocturnal angina, asthma, COPD**, nocturia, renal failure, thyroid disease, menopause.
- **Neurological:** **dementia** (exaggerated age changes, nocturnal confusion), **parkinsonism**, stroke, PSP.
- **Sleep disorders:**
  - **obstructive sleep apnoea** (**up to 50% over 65**);
  - **periodic limb movements** (2% of elderly, 15% of insomniacs);
  - **restless legs**;
  - **advanced sleep phase** (early sleep, waking at 3–5 a.m.);
  - **REM sleep behaviour disorder**.
- **Psychiatric:** **depression** (early waking), anxiety (sleep-onset), bereavement; worry about health, money, death.
- **Lifestyle:** lack of regular activity, **little exercise, daytime naps**, staying in bed too long.
- **Substances and drugs:**
  - **alcohol** (fragmentation, REM rebound), **caffeine**, nicotine;
  - **hypnotic withdrawal/rebound** (worse in the elderly);
  - β-blockers, methyldopa, diuretics, bronchodilators, MAOIs, stimulants, phenytoin.

### Assessment [1]
1. **Pattern:**
   - **sleep-onset** delay (over 30 minutes): anxiety, depression, environment, drugs;
   - **maintenance** insomnia: drugs, alcohol, medical illness, apnoea, restless legs, PLMS, nightmares;
   - **early waking:** depression;
   - phase disorders.
2. **Duration:** **transient** (recovers in **2–4 weeks**) vs **chronic**.
3. **Bed-partner history** (snoring, apnoeas, kicking, dream enactment).
4. **Sleep–wake log.**
5. **Medication and substance review.**
6. Screen for depression, dementia and pain.
7. **PSG** only if apnoea, PLMS or RBD is suspected. Laboratory studies rarely reveal a cause of simple insomnia.

### Management [1, 2]
**1. Treat the cause:**
- pain, nocturia, cardiorespiratory illness;
- **depression** (Lishman notes that **sedative antidepressants and antihistamines should not be used primarily for insomnia**: long half-lives and peripheral effects);
- sleep apnoea (weight loss, avoid evening alcohol and sedatives, **CPAP**);
- RLS/PLMS.

**2. Non-pharmacological first line** [1, 2]:
- **sleep hygiene**: regular routine, **daytime activity and exercise**, **avoid daytime naps**, limit time in bed, **avoid caffeine, alcohol and nicotine** in the evening;
- address worries; **relaxation and behaviour therapy** (especially with fear of sleeplessness);
- **behavioural therapies (e.g., CBT for insomnia or sleep restriction) before hypnotics** [2];
- for **dementia**, established sleep hygiene methods are **first line** [2].

**3. Hypnotics: cautiously, if needed** [1, 2]:
- **short-term only**, **lowest effective dose**, **briefest period**; **intermittent** use (e.g., every third night) limits tolerance [1];
- benzodiazepines are effective short term but suppress REM (rebound on stopping). **Z-drugs** have RCT support up to 6 months [2];
- **avoid** routinely continuing hospital hypnotics at discharge [2];
- **elderly-specific hazards:**
  - **slower absorption, metabolism and elimination**; altered protein binding and sensitivity;
  - **choose a drug whose half-life (including metabolites) is under 24 hours** to avoid accumulation and **confusion**;
  - **falls, delirium**, possibly dementia (benzodiazepines) [2];
  - **respiratory depression**: avoid in sleep apnoea and COPD;
  - **paradoxical reactions**, disinhibition, rage in dementia;
  - **rebound insomnia** after short-acting drugs (worse in the elderly) [1];
- **match pharmacokinetics to the complaint:** a short-acting drug for **sleep-onset** insomnia; a short-acting drug does not help early waking [1];
- **never combine two sedatives** [1];
- **overuse of sedatives in the elderly is harmful**: daytime sedation, agitation, confusion and disturbed behaviour may **resolve when sedatives are stopped** [1].

**4. Dementia** [2]:
- **melatonin** (2 mg, occasionally up to 10 mg modified release) is safe and used with good effect, though Cochrane found no clear benefit;
- **trazodone** (50 mg at night improved sleep);
- **orexin antagonists** (suvorexant, lemborexant) have some evidence;
- Z-drugs are used for insomnia in late-onset AD;
- **no RCTs** for widely used benzodiazepines and Z-drugs in dementia;
- **avoid sedating anticholinergics** (see the BPSD answer).

**5. Circadian disorders:**
- **advanced sleep phase** is commoner with age;
- sleep–wake log; sleep hygiene. *(Light therapy timing is not detailed in the source.)*

**6. Reduce or withdraw long-term hypnotics** gradually (to avoid rebound insomnia and withdrawal).

### Update from Levenson 2019: assessment, CBT-I and hypnotic choice [3]
**Context:**
- **Insomnia disorder (DSM-5)** no longer separates "primary" insomnia from insomnia secondary to another disorder.
- **Prevalence about 10%**; commoner in **women**.
- **Older age:** sleep complaints rise **mainly because of comorbid conditions**, not primary sleep disorders (Vitiello). So **treat the underlying disease first**.
- **Causes (Table 14-3):**
  - **medical:** OSA, COPD, asthma, reflux, angina, hypoglycaemia, **heart failure (orthopnoea, PND)**, hyperthyroidism, pain;
  - **neurological:** central sleep apnoea, **dementia**, **RLS**, fatal familial insomnia;
  - **psychiatric:** medication (xanthines, stimulants), anxiety, mood disorders, **withdrawal** (alcohol, benzodiazepines), irregular sleep–wake rhythm, paradoxical insomnia;
  - **environmental:** noise, temperature, vibration.

**Assessment:**
- a diagnostic interview covering **Table 14-2** (sleep schedule and environment, naps, snoring and pauses, movements, reflux, nocturia, mood, **drugs, alcohol and caffeine**, pain, hangover and amnesia) **plus the bed partner**;
- **examination:** BMI, neck circumference, airway, thyroid, heart, lungs, **cognition**;
- **tests:** **TSH, ferritin, B12, folate, FBC**;
- **rating scales:** Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index, **Insomnia Severity Index**, Dysfunctional Beliefs and Attitudes about Sleep Scale;
- **PSG is generally not useful** for insomnia disorder;
- **paradoxical insomnia** (formerly sleep-state misperception): the complaint is not matched by objective sleep, so PSG must show **normal** sleep. Management is to **discuss the discrepancy**; behavioural techniques and hypnotics have also been used.

**Lifestyle and environment (Table 14-9):**
- **Circadian:**
  - **no daytime naps**;
  - **time in bed limited to 8 hours**;
  - daily exercise finished **at least 4 hours before bed**;
  - a **regular wake time 7 days a week**;
  - avoid evening bright light; **seek morning light**.
- **Less disruption:**
  - little evening fluid; minimal caffeine; no alcohol;
  - a quiet, dark, comfortable bedroom; a relaxing bedtime ritual;
  - write worry lists during the day; turn off devices;
  - **use the bed only for sleep and sex**;
  - no large late meals;
  - treat reflux, pain and nausea.
- **Avoid clock-watching.**

**CBT-I (strongest evidence for sustained benefit):**
- It is effective and feasible in **medical comorbidity**: cancer, COPD, arthritis, heart failure, fibromyalgia.
- **Stimulus control:**
  - do not "try to sleep";
  - if awake in bed, **get up**, do a quiet activity elsewhere until sleepy, then return;
  - repeat as needed.
- **Sleep restriction:**
  - time in bed equals the estimated sleep of the previous night;
  - when the patient sleeps through most of that time for several nights, **add half an hour**.
- **Brevity:** even **1–2 sessions** can help.
- **Digital CBT-I:** SHUTi (sleep hygiene, restriction, stimulus control, cognitive restructuring, relapse prevention) improved sleep initiation and wake after sleep onset at **9 weeks and 1 year**.
- Drugs and behavioural therapy both work early, but **behavioural gains last longer**. Having medication available may **reduce motivation** for behavioural work.

**Hypnotics and sedating drugs:**
- **General approach:**
  - hypnotics suit **short-term or intermittent** insomnia;
  - in chronic insomnia, try non-drug treatment first;
  - **avoiding or minimising sedatives is especially important in the elderly**.
- **Z-drugs** (zaleplon, zolpidem, eszopiclone):
  - **short half-life**, so little hangover; driving alertness maintained;
  - **complex sleep behaviours** (especially zolpidem, at high doses or with alcohol);
  - **no physical dependence** reported with FDA-approved short-term use;
  - **2013 FDA: lower zolpidem dose in women** (slower clearance).
- **Benzodiazepines:** **dependence** and **next-morning cognitive impairment**, so they are used less than before.
- **Antidepressants:**
  - **mirtazapine** (antihistaminergic at **15 mg or less**; weight gain);
  - **trazodone**;
  - both are valuable **with comorbid mood or anxiety disorder**;
  - **low-dose doxepin** for selected patients (watch **cardiac and anticholinergic** effects).
- **Antihistamines** (e.g., diphenhydramine): a **poor choice**, with rapid tolerance and **confusion in the medically ill and elderly**.
- **Ramelteon** (melatonin agonist): well tolerated; no morning residual effect.
- **Suvorexant** (dual orexin antagonist): **avoid in narcolepsy**.

**Half-lives and effects (Table 14-10):**

| Flurazepam | 40+ | Yes | Yes | Yes |
| Temazepam | 10+ | Yes | Yes | No |

⚠️ **Discrepancies (not resolved):**
- **Sedating antidepressants:** Lishman [1] says sedative antidepressants and antihistamines **should not be used primarily for insomnia**. Levenson [3] calls **mirtazapine and trazodone valuable** (especially with mood or anxiety comorbidity) and gives **low-dose doxepin** a role. Both sources reject antihistamines.
- **Zolpidem:** Levenson's text says z-drugs **minimise hangover**, yet its Table 14-10 marks zolpidem **"Yes"** for rebound, tolerance and carry-over sedation. This inconsistency is internal to Levenson.
- **Cross-reference:** Levenson's text sends readers to **Table 14-4** for causes of insomnia, but the causes of insomnia are in **Table 14-3** (14-4 lists causes of excessive sleepiness).
- **Dependence:** Lishman advises **intermittent** hypnotic use to limit tolerance. Levenson reports **no physical dependence** with short-term z-drug use.
- **Not in Levenson:** **age-specific doses**, and trials of CBT-I in older adults specifically.

**Conclusion.** Insomnia in older people reflects normal age-related changes (less slow-wave sleep, more awakenings, about 6 hours of sleep) compounded by medical, neurological, psychiatric and drug causes and by primary sleep disorders (sleep apnoea in up to 50% over 65, PLMS, RLS, advanced phase, RBD). Management is:
- careful assessment of pattern, cause and medication;
- treatment of the underlying disorder;
- sleep hygiene, activity and behavioural therapy as first line;
- only cautious, short, intermittent use of short-half-life hypnotics, mindful of falls, delirium, accumulation, respiratory depression and rebound;
- in dementia, non-drug measures first, then melatonin, trazodone or an orexin antagonist.
