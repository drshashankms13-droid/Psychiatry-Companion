## Exam answer

**Definition.** Prolonged grief disorder (PGD) is an **atypical grief response**. It is characterised by severe and persistent **yearning or preoccupation**, emotional pain, identity disturbance, loss of meaning and purpose, other cognitive, physical or behavioural disruptions, and **functional impairment beyond social, cultural and religious norms** [1].
- It is now in **ICD-11 (2018)** and **DSM-5-TR (2022)** [1].
- Typical onset of the coherent syndrome is **6–12 months after the death** of a close person [1].

**Related terms** [1]:
- **Bereavement:** the objective situation of having lost a close other through death.
- **Mourning:** the cultural, social and religious practices for expressing and coping with grief.
- **Grief:** the emotional response to loss.

### Historical evolution [1, 2]
- **Earlier ICD and DSM:** atypical grief was not formally recognised. It was classified under adjustment disorders or misdiagnosed as depression, with only Z or V codes for bereavement.
- **DSM-III (1980):** introduced the bereavement exclusion for MDD (within 2 months of loss).
- **DSM-5 (2013):** "Persistent complex bereavement disorder", placed in conditions for further study.
- **ICD-11 and DSM-5-TR:** formal PGD criteria.

### Diagnostic criteria compared [1, 3]

| | ICD-11 | DSM-5-TR (F43.8) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Event and time** | Death of a partner, parent, child or other close person; grief persisting atypically long, beyond norms (responses under **6 months** do not qualify) | Death at least **12 months** ago (**6 months** in children and adolescents) |
| **Core (separation distress)** | Persistent, pervasive **longing for** or **preoccupation with** the deceased | Intense **yearning/longing** or **preoccupation** with thoughts or memories, nearly every day for at least the last month |
| **Accessory** | Intense emotional pain, e.g. sadness, guilt, anger, denial, blame; difficulty accepting the death; feeling one has lost part of oneself; inability to feel positive mood; emotional numbness; difficulty engaging in activities | **≥3 of 8:** identity disruption, marked disbelief, avoidance of reminders that the person is dead, intense emotional pain, difficulty reintegrating, emotional numbness, life feels meaningless, intense loneliness |
| **Impairment** | Significant impairment, or functioning kept only through substantial extra effort | Clinically significant distress or impairment |
| **Cultural** | Clearly exceeds social, cultural or religious norms | Duration and severity clearly exceed norms |
| **Exclusion** | — | Not better explained by MDD, PTSD, another disorder, a substance or a medical condition |

### Epidemiology and risk factors
- **Normal course:** in a prospective study, the most common trajectory (**45%**) showed grief largely resolved by 18 months. **Fewer than a quarter** remained elevated [2].
- **Prevalence:**
  - German representative sample: **3%** (DSM-5-TR) and **4%** (ICD-11) [1].
  - Probability samples about **5%** vs convenience samples about **16%** [1].
  - DSM-5-TR quotes a pooled **9.8%** (older, ≥6-month definitions, heterogeneous) [3].
- **Risk factors:**
  - Strongest predictors: **pre-loss grief symptoms** (ESr 0.39) and **depression** (ESr 0.30) [4].
  - Smaller associations: unexpected or violent death, low education, low income, female gender, anxious attachment, death of a child or partner [4].
  - Prior trauma or loss, prior mood or anxiety disorder, insecure attachment, being a caregiver for the deceased, violent death (e.g. suicide), lack of social support [2].
  - Childhood separation anxiety uniquely predicts PGD [2].
  - Dependency on the deceased, economic stressors [3].

### Mechanisms (theories) [1]
- **Psychoanalytic and attachment:**
  - Freud (1917): mourning vs melancholia.
  - Bowlby: inability to reconnect with others plus persistent yearning.
  - Shear: mismatch between the reality of the death and the mental representation of the attachment figure as available.
  - Maccallum & Bryant: disrupted self-identity impairs integration of the loss into autobiographical memory.
- **Cognitive-behavioural:**
  1. Insufficient integration of the **irreversibility** of the loss.
  2. **Maladaptive beliefs** and misinterpretation of grief reactions.
  3. **Anxious and depressive avoidance.**

  Rumination and experiential avoidance are also central.
- **Neurobiological ("gone-but-also-everlasting"):** grieving is learning. The representation of the deceased is updated from "everlasting" to "gone". PGD shows differential **reward-network activation** and altered **oxytocin** signalling.
- **Sociological:** bereavement as social deprivation (role confusion, disconnection).

### Differential diagnosis [1, 2, 3]

| | PGD | Major depression | PTSD |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Focus of distress** | Separation from the deceased | Global, pervasive misery | Threat related to the death event |
| **Thoughts** | Bittersweet preoccupation with many aspects of the relationship | Pessimistic rumination, worthlessness | Intrusions fixed on the death event; perceptual, "here and now" |
| **Guilt** | Self-blame specific to the death | Global guilt and worthlessness | Guilt or shame |
| **Interest** | Sustained interest in the deceased; reunion would satisfy | Broad loss of interest | — |
| **Avoidance** | Of reminders that the person is gone | General withdrawal | Of reminders of the traumatic event |
| **Hallmark emotion** | Yearning, emptiness | Low mood | Fear, hyperarousal |

Also distinguish from:
- **normal grief:** severity and duration; anniversary surges alone don't count;
- **separation anxiety disorder:** separation from *living* figures;
- **psychosis:** hallucinations of the deceased are common in normal grief and are often hypnagogic [3].

### Assessment
- **Instruments** [1, 2]:
  - Validated interviews or self-report: PG-13-R, TGI-SR+ and the Aarhus PGD scale cover **both ICD-11 and DSM-5-TR**.
  - Inventory of Complicated Grief (19 items; score >25–30 suggests PGD); International Grief Questionnaire; IPGDS.
  - **Brief Grief Questionnaire** (5 items): 5–7 subthreshold, **≥8 PGD likely**.
- **Self-report** tends to overestimate, so structured interviews are being developed [1].
- **Screen at intake:** over one-third of general psychiatric outpatients have at least moderate PGD symptoms [2].
- **Suicide risk:** PGD carries a **6–11 times greater risk of suicidality**, even after controlling for depression and PTSD [2].

### Management
- **Tiered model of bereavement care** [1]:
  - printed psychoeducation for **low-risk** people;
  - community groups and trained volunteers for **at-risk** people;
  - **specialist** care for high-risk people and those with PGD.
- **Don't treat normal grief.** Grief therapies give no lasting benefit for normative grief. Benefit is clear in severe, prolonged grief [2].
- **Psychotherapy is the main treatment** [1]. **Grief-targeted CBT** is the best supported:
  - Shear's complicated grief treatment beat IPT [2];
  - exposure + cognitive restructuring (Boelen 2007) beat supportive counselling [2];
  - behavioural activation [2];
  - group CBT (Rosner 2011) [2];
  - internet-based writing therapy [2].
- **Core components** [2]:
  - psychoeducation about grief;
  - repeated emotional processing of the reality of the loss;
  - social re-engagement, including avoided loss reminders;
  - new meaningful aspirations;
  - cognitive restructuring.
- **Pharmacotherapy** [1, 2]:
  - Only **one RCT** (citalopram plus therapy): depressive symptoms fell, but there was **no additive effect on grief**.
  - A TCA treated bereavement-related depression but **not grief**.
  - Antidepressants may help comorbid depression and make therapy more tolerable (dropout 9% medicated vs 42% unmedicated).
- **Unproven so far:** EMDR, compassion-focused therapy and mindfulness [1].

**Conclusion.** PGD is a validated, reliable diagnosis: criteria reliability r = 0.86, higher than PTSD or MDD. It differs from typical grief in **severity and chronicity, not quality**, and is distinct from MDD and PTSD. Specialised grief-focused CBT within a tiered model is the treatment of choice. Medication is for comorbid depression [1, 2].

## In depth

### Background — the debate [1]
- **Critiques of the diagnosis:**
  - **existential:** grief is a foundational human emotion linked to love and death;
  - **diagnostic culture:** pathologising suffering, misdiagnosis, overtreatment, neglect of traditional supports and of structural factors (e.g. migrants).
- **Counter-argument:** the same critiques apply to all disorders; PTSD after violence is not denied treatment.
- **A pragmatic stance** weighs severity, culture, access to care, supports and the person's wishes.
- The **ICD-11 6-month vs DSM-5-TR 12-month** difference illustrates the problem.

### Epidemiology
- **Algorithm dependence:** in the UK, a strict ICD-11 algorithm gave **2.4%** and a moderate one **7.9%** [1].
- **Rates rise** after loss of a partner or child [1, 3].
- **Bereaved US youth:** about **18%** for persistent complex bereavement disorder [3].
- **Older age** may raise risk; older adults with PGD may face cognitive decline [3].
- **Gender difference** is inconsistent [3].

### Validity and biology [1]
- **False-positive rate** with the Prigerson criteria: **1.4%**.
- **Validity:** predictive validity and stability confirmed; reliability **r = 0.86**.
- **Physiology:** higher PG-13-R scores go with greater BP rise and inflammatory response to stress.
- **Immune and gene expression:** grief severity is associated with immune biomarkers; innate antiviral gene-expression patterns differ in PGD.

### Comorbidity
- **Panel study (n=1,529)** [1]: of 312 people with PGD, **8.3% had PGD alone**. 26.3% also had PTSD, 7.7% depression, and 57.7% both.
- **An earlier sample** [2]: about half had depression and half PTSD, mostly predating PGD; a quarter had no Axis I comorbidity.
- **Common comorbidities** (DSM-5-TR): MDD, PTSD, substance use; separation anxiety disorder [3].

### Clinical features in detail (DSM-5-TR associated features) [3]
- **Cognitions and emotions:**
  - maladaptive self-cognitions and guilt about the death;
  - diminished life expectancy and goals;
  - bitterness, anger, restlessness, blaming others.
- **Physical:**
  - somatic complaints, sometimes mirroring the deceased's symptoms;
  - harmful health behaviours from reduced self-care;
  - poor sleep.
- **Perceptual:** hallucinations of the deceased are more common than in normal grief.
- **Children:**
  - distress shown in play and regression;
  - searching for the deceased;
  - somatic symptoms;
  - a wish to reunite physically;
  - giving up developmental aspirations.

### Consequences [2, 3]
- Impaired work and social functioning.
- Tobacco and alcohol use.
- **Cardiac disease, hypertension, cancer, immune deficiency.**
- Reduced quality of life.
- Suicidal ideation, especially after violent loss or the death of a child under 25.

### Cultural aspects [1, 3]
- **Core symptoms may vary:** yearning and preoccupation may be more prevalent in the Global North; somatic reactions may be core elsewhere.
- **Indian widows in Fiji:** prescribed mourning could mimic disorder.
- **Bali:** zero prevalence among 301 bereaved Balinese people, attributed to protective Hindu rituals and beliefs (reincarnation, karma).
- **Inability to perform funerary rituals** may worsen PGD.
- **Cultural tools:** the Bereavement and Grief Cultural Formulation Interview (BG-CFI) and the Structured Clinical Interview for Prolonged Grief (SCIP) consider mourning customs.
- **COVID-19:** rates rose. Severity was linked more to unnatural deaths than to COVID deaths; early isolation predicted PGD.

### Management details
- **Shear's treatment (16 sessions)** [2]:
  - introductory psychoeducation and life goals;
  - "revisiting" (narrating the death, recorded and replayed);
  - imaginal conversation with the deceased;
  - memories work;
  - restoration-focused goal plans;
  - in-vivo approach to avoided situations;
  - cognitive restructuring.
- **Boelen 2007** [2]: exposure produced more change than cognitive restructuring.
- **Behavioural activation** [2]: 12–14 sessions (Papa 2013), or even 5 sessions (Acierno 2012), produced large reductions.
- **Group therapy** [2]:
  - CBT group therapy is effective (Rosner 2011; Supiano; Maccallum & Bryant);
  - **psychodynamic interpretive and supportive groups were equivalent** (Piper 2001, 2007).
- **Internet (Wagner 2006)** [2]: 5-week email-based writing (exposure, self-compassion letters, integration), with gains maintained at 18 months.
- **Prevention** [1]:
  - generally inconsistent;
  - **tailored** interventions work: family therapy in cancer care, ICU family meetings.
- **Scalable care** [1]:
  - digital interventions, guided and unguided, have moderate-to-large effects; unguided versions have high dropout;
  - refugees: CBT and integrative adapt therapy were effective in Myanmar refugees, but not in a 5-week group for Syrian refugees.
- **Children** [1]: group CBT and family-based approaches, with a long-term effect on suicidal behaviour.
- **Timing** [2]: start PGD-targeted therapy once **6–12 months** have passed.

### Evidence table (studies as reported in the sources)

| Study | Finding |
| --- | --- |
| Currier 2008 meta-analysis (61 trials) [2] | No lasting benefit for normal grief; clear benefit in severe, prolonged grief |
| Wittouck 2011 meta-analysis [2] | Grief-targeted CBT beat control; effects grew at follow-up |
| Shear 2005 (n≈100) [2] | Complicated grief treatment beat IPT (medium effect) |
| Boelen 2007 [2] | Exposure + CR beat supportive counselling |
| Papa 2013; Acierno 2012 [2] | Behavioural activation gave large reductions |
| Rosner 2011 [2] | Group therapy for inpatients gave large reductions |
| Piper 2001, 2007 [2] | Interpretive = supportive group therapy |
| Wagner 2006 [2] | Internet writing therapy; gains held at 18 months |
| Reynolds 1999 [2] | TCA helped depression but not grief |
| Citalopram RCT [1] | No additive effect on grief |
| Exposure dismantling meta-analysis [1] | No added effect of exposure (few studies) |

### Critical appraisal
- **Exposure:** Jordan & Litz argue that repeated exposure to the moment of death rests on an untested trauma-conditioning assumption. They recommend starting with **restoration-focused** work (behavioural activation). The 2025 meta-analysis found no added effect of exposure [1, 2].
- **Algorithms and time criteria:** these differ between ICD-11 and DSM-5-TR and should be aligned [1].
- **Measurement:** self-report overestimates prevalence [1].
- **Cultural universality** of core symptoms is unproven [1].
- **Help-seeking:** most people with PGD don't seek help [2].
- **Note on the user's notes:**
  - The notes say "one RCT supporting SSRIs in addition to CBT". The source says that RCT found **no additive effect on grief symptoms**, only on depression.
  - The notes say "50% will have resolved grief by 18 months". The source figure is **45%**.

No Indian prevalence or treatment data are in the sources.

### Recent advances [1]
- PG-13-R, TGI-SR+ and the Aarhus scale map onto both diagnostic systems.
- Structured interviews (SCIP and others) are under validation.
- Digital interventions.
- Culturally adapted care for refugees.
- Neurobiological research and possible pharmacological targets, with a call for responsible use.
