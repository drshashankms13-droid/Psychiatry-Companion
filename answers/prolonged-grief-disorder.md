## Exam answer

**Definition.** Prolonged grief disorder (PGD; ICD-11 **6B42**, 2018; DSM-5-TR, 2022) is an atypical grief response after the death of a partner, parent, child or other close person. Its core features are persistent, pervasive **yearning for** and/or **preoccupation with** the deceased, with intense emotional pain (sadness, guilt, anger, denial, blame, difficulty accepting the death, feeling part of oneself is lost, numbness, inability to feel positive mood, difficulty engaging), significant functional impairment, and a duration that **clearly exceeds social, cultural and religious norms**. The minimum is **6 months** after the loss in ICD-11, or **12 months** in DSM-5-TR (6 months in children) [1].

### Mechanism
- **Attachment (Bowlby):** separation distress persists when the attachment system cannot accept loss.
- **"Gone-but-also-everlasting" (O'Connor):** grieving is new learning, in which the brain updates the representation of the loved one from "everlasting" to "gone". In PGD, reward circuits (nucleus accumbens) and oxytocin signalling continue to respond to cues of the deceased, maintaining yearning [1].
- **Cognitive-behavioural model (Boelen):**
  1. Poor integration of the loss into autobiographical memory.
  2. Negative global beliefs and catastrophic misinterpretation of grief reactions.
  3. **Anxious avoidance** (of reminders and the reality of the loss) and **depressive avoidance** (withdrawal), plus rumination [1].
- **Social:** bereavement as social deprivation (role loss, disconnection) [1].

### Diagnostic criteria (comparison)

| | ICD-11 6B42 | DSM-5-TR |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Time since death | ≥6 months (longer in some cultures) | ≥12 months (≥6 months in children) |
| Core | Longing for **or** preoccupation with the deceased | Intense yearning **or** preoccupation, nearly every day for ≥1 month |
| Accessory | Intense emotional pain (examples listed) | ≥3 of 8: identity disruption, disbelief, avoidance of reminders, intense emotional pain, difficulty reintegrating, numbness, meaninglessness, intense loneliness |
| Impairment | Significant | Clinically significant |
| Culture | Exceeds norms | Exceeds norms |
| Exclusion | — | Not better explained by MDD, PTSD, etc. |

Source: [1].

### Assessment
- Bereavement history: relationship, cause of death (sudden, violent, suicide, COVID), circumstances, mourning rituals.
- Symptom profile and duration; function; cultural norms.
- **Comorbidity:** depression, PTSD, anxiety, substance use; **suicide risk** (raised in PGD).
- Physical health: hypertension, immune changes [1].
- **Scales:** PG-13-R, Traumatic Grief Inventory-Self Report Plus (TGI-SR+), Aarhus PGD scale (all cover both ICD-11 and DSM-5-TR); Inventory of Complicated Grief; International Prolonged Grief Disorder Scale; Structured Clinical Interview for Prolonged Grief (SCIP); Bereavement and Grief Cultural Formulation Interview [1].

### Management (tiered model)
- **Tier 1 (all bereaved):** information, practical support, normalising grief.
- **Tier 2 (at risk):** community groups, trained volunteers, monitoring, early guided self-help.
- **Tier 3 (PGD):** specialist **grief-focused psychotherapy**:
  - **Grief-focused CBT** (Boelen): exposure to the reality of the loss, cognitive restructuring, behavioural activation.
  - **Complicated Grief Treatment** (Shear; 16 sessions): imaginal revisiting, situational revisiting, imaginal conversation with the deceased, restoration-oriented goals. In the Shear 2014 RCT, CGT was superior to IPT.
  - Internet-delivered CBT (guided ≈ unguided; unguided has high dropout) [1].
  - Family-focused and context-specific prevention (systemic family therapy in cancer care; ICU family meetings) [1].
- **Pharmacotherapy:** no drug is approved for PGD. In the only RCT (Shear 2016), **citalopram** reduced comorbid depressive symptoms but **did not improve grief** when added to CGT. Use antidepressants for comorbid MDD [1].
- **Risk:** suicide assessment and safety planning; treat PTSD and depression.

### Key evidence
- **Prevalence:** 3% (DSM-5-TR) and 4% (ICD-11) in a German representative bereaved sample; about 5% in probability samples vs 16% in convenience samples; higher after loss of a child or partner and after unnatural deaths [1].
- **Comorbidity:** only 8.3% of PGD cases had PGD alone; 57.7% also met criteria for PTSD and depression [1].
- **Reliability** of PGD criteria was high (r = 0.86, higher than PTSD or MDD); false-positive rate 1.4% [1].
- **Shear 2014 (JAMA Psychiatry):** CGT response 70% vs 32% for IPT in elderly patients.

**Conclusion.** PGD is now a valid, reliable diagnosis that identifies a minority (~3–10%) of bereaved people whose grief stalls, with real health and suicide risks. Diagnosis must be culturally informed so that normal mourning is not pathologised. Treatment is mainly grief-focused psychotherapy (CBT/CGT); antidepressants are only for comorbid depression.

## In depth

### Background
- **Freud, "Mourning and Melancholia" (1917):** "grief work" (detachment from the lost object) vs melancholia [1].
- **Lindemann (1944):** Cocoanut Grove fire; acute grief and "morbid grief reactions".
- **Bowlby (1960s–80):** attachment and loss; phases of numbing, yearning, disorganisation and reorganisation [1].
- **Kübler-Ross (1969):** stages, a popular but empirically weak model.
- **Stroebe & Schut, dual process model (1999):** oscillation between loss-oriented and restoration-oriented coping [1].
- **Diagnostic history:**
  - DSM-III (1980) introduced the bereavement exclusion for MDD.
  - Horowitz (1997) proposed complicated grief criteria.
  - Prigerson (2009) proposed PGD criteria.
  - DSM-5 (2013) removed the bereavement exclusion and put persistent complex bereavement disorder in Section III.
  - ICD-11 (2018) introduced PGD; DSM-5-TR (2022) added it [1].

### Epidemiology
- PGD affects about 3–10% of bereaved adults in representative samples. Strict ICD-11 algorithms give 2.4% and moderate ones 7.9% (UK) [1].
- Risk factors:
  - Loss of a child or partner; sudden, violent or unnatural death (suicide, homicide, accidents, overdose).
  - Insecure attachment, high dependency, prior mental illness.
  - Low social support and early social isolation (pandemic studies) [1].
- Typical onset 6–12 months after the death [1].
- Outcomes: hypertension, cardiovascular events, immune dysregulation, suicidality, reduced life satisfaction, high service use [1].

### Classification and criteria
- ICD-11 places PGD under **disorders specifically associated with stress** (6B4), with PTSD, complex PTSD, adjustment disorder, reactive attachment disorder and disinhibited social engagement disorder.
- DSM-5-TR places it under trauma- and stressor-related disorders.
- Disagreements between the systems: the time criterion (6 vs 12 months) and the algorithm (ICD-11 lists accessory symptoms as examples; DSM-5-TR requires ≥3 of 8). Prevalence differences between them are minor [1].
- **Cultural note:** yearning and preoccupation are core in the Global North; somatic reactions may be more central elsewhere. In Bali, 0 of 301 traffic-bereaved people met criteria, possibly reflecting protective Hindu rituals and beliefs in reincarnation [1].

### Mechanism in detail
- **Neural:** nucleus accumbens activation to reminders of the deceased (reward/craving) in complicated grief (O'Connor 2008); altered oxytocin signalling; PFC–amygdala changes [1].
- **Physiological:** greater BP and inflammatory responses to grief recall; antiviral gene-expression changes [1].
- **Memory:** intrusive, visceral and negatively coloured memories; proximity-seeking and ruminative coping predict later PGD (Smith and colleagues, three-wave study) [1].

### Clinical features in detail
Persistent yearning; searching; preoccupation with the deceased or the death; disbelief; avoidance of reminders or, conversely, proximity-seeking (keeping belongings untouched, frequent grave visits); bitterness, anger, guilt and self-blame; identity disruption ("part of me died"); loneliness; meaninglessness; wish to die to join the deceased; social withdrawal; inability to plan for the future.

### Assessment in detail

| Tool | Notes |
| --- | --- |
| PG-13-R | 13 items; covers DSM-5-TR and ICD-11; widely used [1] |
| TGI-SR+ | 22 items; both systems [1] |
| Aarhus PGD scale | Both systems [1] |
| ICG (Inventory of Complicated Grief) | 19 items; cut-off >25 |
| IPGDS | Includes cultural items [1] |
| SCIP | Structured interview; rates whether symptoms may be cultural customs [1] |
| BG-CFI | Cultural formulation supplement [1] |

Self-report questionnaires overestimate prevalence; clinical interviews are preferable [1].

### Differential diagnosis

| Condition | Distinguishing feature |
| --- | --- |
| Normal grief | Same symptoms, but less severe and time-limited; within cultural norms; function recovers [1] |
| Major depressive disorder | Pervasive low mood and anhedonia not focused on the deceased; global worthlessness and self-contempt [1] |
| PTSD | Threat-focused re-experiencing of the death event, hyperarousal; intrusions are fearful rather than bittersweet longing [1] |
| Adjustment disorder | Shorter, broader stressors |
| Separation anxiety disorder | Fear of separation from living attachment figures |
| Psychotic disorder | Hallucinations of the deceased are common in normal grief; distinguish from psychotic experiences with other features |

### Management details
- **CBT components:** psychoeducation; integrating the loss (writing assignments, exposure to the story of the death); cognitive restructuring of beliefs ("I can't live without him"; "if I let go, I betray her"); reducing avoidance; behavioural activation; meaning-making; continuing-bonds work [1].
- **CGT:** 16 sessions; dual process model; imaginal and situational revisiting; memory work; imaginal conversation; personal goals and relationships.
- **Prevention:** before-death family interventions in palliative care; ICU communication bundles [1].
- **Children and adolescents:** group CBT; family-based programmes (e.g., Family Bereavement Program), with long-term reductions in suicidal behaviour [1].
- **Refugees and disasters:** CBT and integrative adapt therapy for Myanmar refugees in Malaysia were effective; a brief group intervention for Syrian refugees was not [1].
- **Pharmacotherapy:** SSRI for comorbid MDD; avoid long-term benzodiazepines (they may impair grief processing). Naltrexone (reward hypothesis) is investigational [1].

### Critical appraisal
- **Pathologising grief:** critics (Frances, Cacciatore) argue the diagnosis medicalises normal sorrow, especially after losing a child [1]. The counter-argument is that the same reasoning would apply to PTSD; a dimensional, stepped model avoids over-treatment [1].
- **Time criteria** differ; 12 months may delay care, while 6 months may overcall [1].
- **Cultural validity:** Western-derived items; mourning norms differ (for example, Hindu rituals; widowhood restrictions in some Indian communities) [1].
- **Pharmacological evidence** is minimal [1].
- **Measurement:** inconsistent tools and self-report overestimate prevalence [1].

### Indian context
- Rich mourning rituals (13-day rituals in Hindu traditions, annual shraddha, Islamic iddah, Christian memorial services) provide structure, community support and continuing bonds, which may protect against PGD [1].
- Widowhood in some communities involves social restrictions that can mimic or provoke depression-like states. Interpret symptoms through a cultural lens [1].
- COVID-19 caused mass bereavement in India (2021), with disrupted rituals and sudden deaths, so PGD risk is likely raised.
- Farmer suicides, road traffic deaths and disasters leave bereaved families needing support. Tele-MANAS and NGO bereavement groups help.
- Grief therapy expertise is scarce; brief CBT-based protocols and digital tools in regional languages are needed.

### Recent advances
- **Established:** inclusion of PGD in ICD-11 and DSM-5-TR [1].
- **Emerging:** internet-delivered grief CBT; structured interviews (SCIP); cultural formulation tools [1].
- **Investigational:** naltrexone for grief (reward-pathway hypothesis); neuroimaging biomarkers; prevention programmes in palliative care [1].
