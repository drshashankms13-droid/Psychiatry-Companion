## Exam answer

### Classification of movement disorders (Box 1) [1]
- **Insufficient movement:** akinetic, hypokinetic or bradykinetic (akinetic–rigid) syndromes.
- **Too much movement (hyperkinesias or dyskinesias):**
  - **jerky:** myoclonus (including excessive startle), chorea (including ballism), tics;
  - **non-jerky:** dystonia (including athetosis), tremor.

**Burden** [1]:
- **Parkinson's disease:** 1% at ages 65–85 and 4.3% over 85.
- **Essential tremor:** 4% over 40 and 14% over 65.
- **Tics:** up to 21% of school-age children.

**Why classification matters** [1]:
- it focuses investigations, rather than a "scattergun" work-up;
- it has prognostic implications (e.g., essential tremor vs early PD);
- it has genetic implications (e.g., Huntington's disease);
- it guides treatment.

### Hyperkinetic disorders: definitions, keywords and examples [1]

| Disorder | Definition | Keyword | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Myoclonus** | Sudden, brief, **shock-like** involuntary movements. *Positive* (muscle contraction) or *negative* (loss of tone, e.g., asterixis) | "Shock-like" | Hypnic jerks (physiological); essential; epileptic; symptomatic (**hepatic or uraemic encephalopathy**, post-anoxic). Cortical (focal, action- or stimulus-sensitive), subcortical/brainstem (generalised, auditory-sensitive), spinal (propriospinal; often functional), peripheral. Excessive **startle** belongs to the myoclonus spectrum |
| **Chorea** | Abrupt, unpredictable, nonrhythmic movements from a continuous random flow of contractions, flitting between body parts ("fidgeting"). **Motor impersistence** ("milkmaid's grip"); hung-up reflexes. Patients may mask it in voluntary acts | "Randomly flowing jerks" | **Huntington's disease**; antiphospholipid syndrome; levodopa-induced dyskinesia. **Ballism**: large-amplitude proximal chorea, usually **hemiballism from a contralateral subthalamic nucleus lesion**; bilateral ballism is mostly metabolic |
| **Tics** | Recurrent stereotyped movements or sounds, with a premonitory urge, relief, **suppressibility** and rebound. Simple or complex; motor or phonic | "Stereotyped" | Tourette's syndrome. Differential: **stereotypies** (longer, patterned, rhythmic, purposeless; autism, intellectual disability, Rett syndrome, blindness) |
| **Tremor** | Involuntary, **rhythmic, sinusoidal** oscillation of one or more body parts. Amplitude varies; frequency fixed | "Rhythmicity" | Rest (**PD**); postural (physiological, enhanced, **essential tremor**); kinetic and intention (**cerebellar**); task-specific (dystonic writing tremor); isometric; **orthostatic (14–18 Hz)**; **Holmes/rubral (2–3 Hz; rest, postural and intention)**; dystonic tremor; functional (variable, distractible) |
| **Dystonia** | Involuntary sustained co-contraction of antagonists, causing **abnormal postures** and twisting, repetitive movements | "Abnormal posture" | Torticollis, writer's cramp, musician's dystonia, blepharospasm, DYT11 myoclonus–dystonia, dopa-responsive dystonia. **Athetosis** = distal "mobile dystonia" (slow, writhing) |

**Tremor classification (MDS consensus; Table 1)** [1]:
- **Rest:** best judged in a fully supported limb. PD tremor **re-emerges after a delay** when a posture is held ("resetting").
- **Action:**
  - **postural** (physiological, enhanced, essential);
  - **kinetic:** simple (essential), **intention** (cerebellar), task-specific (dystonic writing tremor);
  - **isometric.**
- **Combinations:** severe essential tremor, atypical parkinsonism, dystonic tremor, Holmes tremor.

**Clinical pearls** [1]:
- **Cogwheeling** is the palpable tremor itself. In essential or dystonic tremor there is no accompanying rigidity or akinesia, unlike PD.
- **Orthostatic tremor** can be heard with a stethoscope (a "helicopter" sound).
- **Dystonic tremor** can mimic PD (reduced arm swing), but there is **no true akinesia** and **DaT imaging is normal**. It is often misclassified as essential tremor. Look for "dinner-fork" posture or ulnar fingers pointing down.
- **Palatal myoclonus** is now called **palatal tremor** (it is rhythmic).
- **Mimics of myoclonus** (Box 2): tics, tremor, fasciculations, myokymia, chorea.

### Approach to a patient with dystonia

**1. Recognise dystonia** [1]:
- **Keyword:** "abnormal posture". Dystonia is the only movement disorder visible in a still image.
- **Consistent directionality:** e.g., torticollis does not switch direction.
- **Patterned**, repeatedly involving the same muscles.
- **Mobile early**, becoming more fixed over time.
- **Sensory trick (geste antagoniste):** e.g., touching the cheek relieves torticollis; chewing gum relieves oromandibular dystonia.
- **Action-induced** and often **task-specific** (writer's cramp; musician's dystonia). Leg dystonia may impair walking forwards but not backwards or running. Ask the patient to bring the instrument or a home video.
- **Fixed posture at onset suggests a functional cause**, though fixed dystonia can occur early in **corticobasal degeneration** [1].

**2. Classify** [1]:
- **By distribution:** focal, segmental, multifocal, generalised, hemidystonia.
- **By age at onset:** early (**26 or younger**) or late (26 or older).
- **By cause:**
  - **primary:** dystonia only, with or without tremor;
  - **dystonia-plus:** e.g., with parkinsonism in **dopa-responsive dystonia**; ataxia; **myoclonus–dystonia (DYT11, ε-sarcoglycan; alcohol-responsive)**;
  - **degenerative**;
  - **secondary:** other neurological features plus an identifiable cause.

**3. Exclude mimics (Box 2)** [1]:

| Region | Mimics |
| --- | --- |
| **Craniocervical (torticollis)** | Retropharyngeal abscess, atlanto-axial subluxation, congenital muscular torticollis, **ocular torticollis** (head tilt for cranial nerve palsy), posterior fossa mass, Sandifer syndrome, dropped-head syndrome |
| **Limb** | Contracture, spasticity, paresis or atrophy, myotonia or neuromyotonia, **pseudoathetosis** (sensory ataxia), **stiff-person syndrome**, tonic spasms, seizures or epilepsia partialis continua |
| **Facial** | Ptosis, trismus, hemimasticatory spasm, **hemifacial spasm**, myotonia, tetany, apraxia of eyelid opening |

**4. Ask the four key questions (Figure 1)** [1]:
1. Which movement disorders are present? Mixed disorders are the rule.
2. Which is **dominant**?
3. What are the **associated neurological and non-neurological features**?
4. What is the differential diagnosis, and therefore which **focused work-up**?

**Associated features that point to a cause** [1]:
- **Kayser–Fleischer rings** suggest **Wilson's disease** (tremor at rest and posture, dystonia, akinetic–rigid syndrome).
- **Ataxia** with dystonia: prioritise the work-up for ataxia, especially hereditary causes.
- **Alcohol-responsive** head jerks with torticollis: **DYT11**.
- **Attacks triggered by sudden movement:** paroxysmal kinesigenic dyskinesia.
- **Family history and ethnicity:** e.g., Filipino ethnicity with family history suggests **DYT3 (lubag)**.
- **Asymmetric akinesia, rigidity, myoclonus, dystonia and apraxia:** corticobasal degeneration.
- **Chorea, dystonia and bradykinesia:** Huntington's disease.

**5. Always consider drugs** [1, 3]:
- **Dopamine-blocking agents** cause acute dystonia within hours (minutes after IM), tardive dystonia after more than 3 months, and other movement disorders.
- **Anticholinergics** can cause chorea in primary dystonia.
- **Effects can persist for months** after the drug is stopped, so obtain a complete drug history [1].
- **Acute drug-induced dystonia:** treated with parenteral anticholinergics (benztropine 2 mg or diphenhydramine 50 mg) [3].

**6. Consider functional dystonia** [2]:
- **Typical features:** **fixed** ankle plantar-flexion/inversion, or fist with pincer grasp spared; pain; resistance; **no overflow or sensory trick**.
- **Response:** dramatic response to placebo or physiotherapy.
- **Post-traumatic dystonia:** most "post-traumatic" or CRPS dystonia is probably functional.
- **Prognosis:** functional dystonia has the worst FMD prognosis (about 80% unchanged or worse).

**7. Look for a cause: secondary dystonia** [4]:
- **Causes:** perinatal hypoxia or kernicterus; Huntington's; **Wilson's**; drugs (neuroleptics, levodopa); stroke; head injury (dystonia is the commonest movement disorder after severe head injury; mean latency about 2 years); tumour; delayed onset 1–14 years after anoxia or infarction; rarely after mild peripheral injury.
- **Pointers to a symptomatic cause:** asymmetry or unilaterality; seizures; intellectual impairment; pyramidal signs.
- **Yield by distribution:** a cause is found in about 45% of generalised or multifocal, 10% of focal and **over 80% of hemidystonia**.
- **Yield by age at onset:** symptomatic in 40% (childhood), 30% (adolescence), 13% (over 20).
- **Progression to generalised dystonia:** 60% (childhood onset), 25% (adolescent), 3% (adult). Children usually start in the legs.
- **Exclude Wilson's disease in everyone with onset below 50** (caeruloplasmin, slit lamp).
- **Give every child or adolescent a 3-month levodopa trial** to detect **dopa-responsive dystonia (Segawa; DYT5)**:
  - 5–10% of childhood or adolescent onset;
  - toe-walking gait;
  - diurnal fluctuation;
  - parkinsonian features;
  - dramatic, sustained response to small doses without dyskinesias.
- **Genetics:** DYT1 (torsin A; chromosome 9q; AD with reduced penetrance; early-onset generalised); DYT7 (chromosome 18) in some cervical dystonia; at least 10 genes known.

**8. Treat** [4]:

| Type | Treatment (Lishman) |
| --- | --- |
| **Generalised (dystonia musculorum deformans)** | **Levodopa trial first** (children/adolescents). Then **high-dose anticholinergic** (trihexyphenidyl; helps about half; titrate slowly over months; children tolerate up to 120 mg/day). Then diazepam, clonazepam, **baclofen**, carbamazepine, **tetrabenazine**, neuroleptics (pimozide, haloperidol). Severe cases: low-dose tetrabenazine + pimozide + trihexyphenidyl. Phenothiazines may worsen dystonia or cause TD. **Stereotactic surgery**: thalamotomy (bilateral risks dysphagia), **bilateral pallidotomy**, **pallidal deep brain stimulation** |
| **Cervical dystonia (spasmodic torticollis)** | **Botulinum toxin A** into the overactive sternomastoid, splenius capitis or trapezius is **the most useful treatment**: onset within a week, lasts 2–4 months, repeat injections. Side effects: transient neck weakness, dysphagia. About 7.5% develop secondary non-response (antibodies); **type B** works in type-A-resistant patients. Oral drugs (anticholinergics, benzodiazepines, neuroleptics) are disappointing and transient. Collars and braces don't help. **Behaviour therapy, EMG biofeedback**, psychotherapy help some. Surgery (root section, peripheral denervation) is now rarely needed |
| **Blepharospasm / oromandibular (Meige) dystonia** | **Botulinum toxin into orbicularis oculi is first line** for blepharospasm: peak 5–7 days, lasts about 3 (up to 6) months; local ptosis, diplopia. Anticholinergics, levodopa or lisuride, tetrabenazine, lithium, benzodiazepines, haloperidol or pimozide may help. Severe: facial nerve section or orbicularis stripping |
| **Laryngeal (spasmodic dysphonia)** | Botulinum toxin (drugs of little help). The **adductor** type (strangled voice) and **abductor** type (breathy whisper) may be mistaken for hysterical aphonia |
| **Hemifacial spasm** (not a dystonia) | Botulinum toxin; phenytoin, carbamazepine, clonazepam |
| **Acute or tardive (drug-induced)** | See the neuroleptic-induced movement disorders answer |

**9. Psychiatric aspects of dystonia** [4]:
- **Psychosensitivity:** dystonia is markedly worsened by stress, self-consciousness and startle, and improved by relaxation. Bizarre features (can run or walk backwards but not walk forwards; gestes antagonistes; remissions) mean **organic dystonia is often misdiagnosed as hysteria**: patients have had "years of psychotherapy for hysterical spasms". Only a small proportion are truly psychogenic.
- **Organic basis:** reduced output from the GPi/SNr disinhibits the thalamus and cortex. Imaging shows basal ganglia hypermetabolism and reduced striatal D2 binding. Controlled studies show no excess premorbid personality disorder.
- **Comorbidity:**
  - **cervical dystonia:** depression 15–42%; anxiety (social phobia, GAD), often **predating** the dystonia; pain in 70–80% drives disability;
  - **blepharospasm:** depression 37%; 71% with a lifetime psychiatric diagnosis;
  - **DYT1:** major depression **four times** more common, even in non-manifesting carriers, so part of the phenotype.
- **Management:** treat comorbid depression and anxiety; support adjustment (patient and family); supportive psychotherapy; patient societies.

### Update from Continuum 2022: the dystonias [5]
**Definition and clinical features:**
- Dystonia is the **third commonest movement disorder** after essential tremor and PD. Prevalence may be up to **1 in 1000** (underdiagnosed).
- **Mechanism:** sustained or intermittent **agonist–antagonist co-contraction**, giving twisting, **repetitive, patterned** movements and postures.
- **Pathophysiology:** a **network disorder** involving the basal ganglia, cerebellum, thalamus and sensorimotor cortex.
- **Core features:**
  - triggered by voluntary action;
  - **overflow** dystonia; **mirror** dystonia;
  - dystonic tremor (jerky, patterned, not strictly rhythmic);
  - a **"null point"**;
  - **sensory tricks (geste antagoniste)**: simple, natural, "elegant", **never forceful**;
  - worse with **stress, anxiety and fatigue**; better with relaxation; **gone in sleep**.
- **Functional dystonia** is suggested by bizarre "tricks" or dramatic suppression by examiner manoeuvres (e.g., a tuning fork on the head) (Table 10-1).
- **Tests:** imaging and laboratory tests are **usually normal**; genetics is the only definitive test in genetic forms.
- **Common misdiagnoses:** PD, essential tremor, myoclonus, tics, functional dystonia, headache, scoliosis.
  - **Dystonia in PD:** 30% have off-state dystonia; 10–15% have leg dystonia.
  - **Hallmarks of dystonia vs PD:** task- or position-specific tremor, head or voice tremor, **normal DaT-SPECT**, and no dopaminergic benefit (except dopa-responsive dystonia).

**2013 consensus classification (two axes):**
- **Axis I (clinical):**
  - **age at onset**;
  - **body distribution:** focal, **segmental** (2 or more contiguous parts), multifocal, **generalised** (trunk plus 2 other sites), **hemidystonia**;
  - **temporal pattern:** persistent, **task-specific**, **paroxysmal**, **diurnal** (dopa-responsive dystonia);
  - **associated features:** **isolated** (only dystonia, with or without tremor) vs **combined** (with myoclonus, parkinsonism, ataxia or chorea).
- **Axis II (aetiology):** nervous-system pathology on imaging and testing; **inherited, acquired or idiopathic**.

**Focal examples (Table 10-2):**
- **blepharospasm**;
- **oromandibular** dystonia (including task-specific embouchure dystonia);
- **spasmodic dysphonia**: adductor (strained, strangled) vs abductor (breathy);
- **cervical** dystonia: torticollis, laterocollis, retrocollis, anterocollis;
- **writer's and musician's cramp**;
- foot, runner's or cycling dystonia;
- **truncal**: camptocormia, opisthotonus, **Pisa syndrome**.
- **Segmental:** e.g., **Meige syndrome**.

**New genetic nomenclature (2022 update):**
- "DYT-" plus the gene name for **isolated** dystonias, e.g., **DYT-TOR1A** (formerly DYT1);
- a double prefix for **combined** forms, e.g., **DYT/PARK-TAF1** (formerly DYT3, X-linked dystonia-parkinsonism, Filipino);
- **PxMD** for paroxysmal forms.

| Gene (old name) | Key points |
| --- | --- |
| **DYT-TOR1A (DYT1)** | Commonest genetic dystonia; AD with reduced penetrance; childhood; focal **leg** onset then generalises; cranial involvement rare; Ashkenazi; **DBS highly effective** |
| DYT-THAP1 (DYT6) | Prominent **cranial**, laryngeal and cervical involvement |
| DYT-ANO3, DYT-GNAL | Adult cervical onset; tremor resembling ET |
| **DYT/PARK-GCH1 (DYT5a)** | **Dopa-responsive**; **diurnal variation**; may mimic cerebral palsy |
| DYT/PARK-TH, SPR | Dopa-responsive; oculogyric crises; developmental delay (SPR) |
| DYT/PARK-ATP1A3 (DYT12) | Rapid-onset dystonia-parkinsonism after fever or stress; **DBS ineffective** |

**Investigations:**
- **Idiopathic late-onset focal dystonia:** no work-up needed.
- **Otherwise:**
  - **copper, caeruloplasmin and 24-hour urinary copper** (Wilson's);
  - **MRI** for hemidystonia, generalised, rapidly progressive, combined or young-onset dystonia;
  - EEG if paroxysmal; CSF for metabolic or neurotransmitter disorders;
  - **gene panels** (with **genetic counselling**) for young-onset, combined or complex cases; exome or genome sequencing if suspicion stays high.

**Treatment:**
- **Pathogenesis-directed (Table 10-7):**
  - **Wilson's:** penicillamine, trientine, zinc;
  - **dopa-responsive dystonia:** low-dose levodopa. **Give every young-onset case (and atypical adult cases) a levodopa trial**;
  - **paroxysmal kinesigenic dyskinesia** (*PRRT2*): low-dose carbamazepine 50–200 mg/day or oxcarbazepine;
  - **paroxysmal non-kinesigenic:** low-dose clonazepam or diazepam;
  - **exercise-induced** (*SLC2A1*/GLUT1): ketogenic diet, triheptanoin;
  - metabolic diets; trigger avoidance.
- **Supportive measures:** encourage **sensory tricks**; physio, OT and speech therapy (splinting, stretching, sensorimotor retraining).
- **Botulinum toxin** is the **cornerstone for focal dystonia**:
  - serotypes **A** (ona-, abo-, incobotulinumtoxinA) and **B** (rimabotulinumtoxinB); **units are not interchangeable**;
  - effect lasts **12–24 weeks** (mean **15.5** in cervical dystonia); re-inject about **every 12 weeks**;
  - **EMG or ultrasound guidance**;
  - **neutralising antibodies are rare** (frontalis test);
  - daxibotulinumtoxinA (longer-acting) is in trials.
- **Oral drugs** (generalised or severe dystonia; better tolerated by the young):
  - **trihexyphenidyl** from 1 mg/day up to 2 mg three times daily or more: the **most effective oral drug (71% response, waning)**. Adverse effects: dry mouth, constipation, blurred vision, **cognitive change, hallucinations, anticholinergic delirium**, so avoid in the elderly;
  - **baclofen** 5 mg/day up to **30–120 mg/day**; intrathecal baclofen for spastic dystonia;
  - **clonazepam** (second or third line; myoclonus-dystonia). **Benzodiazepine misuse in 11%** of one cervical dystonia cohort;
  - levodopa 200–400 mg/day (dopa-responsive dystonia and some combined forms);
  - **VMAT2 inhibitors** (HD and **tardive** dystonia), **contraindicated with untreated depression or suicidality**;
  - **antipsychotics should be avoided** (sedation, EPS);
  - zolpidem 5–20 mg (blepharospasm); sodium oxybate (alcohol-responsive dystonia); zonisamide (myoclonus-dystonia).
- **Tardive dystonia:** stop the culprit and anticholinergics; VMAT2 inhibitors (valbenazine, deutetrabenazine, tetrabenazine); botulinum toxin if focal; **zolpidem for tardive akathisia**; amantadine for mild cases; DBS if refractory.
- **Surgery:**
  - **DBS**:
    - **GPi** is the main target; STN is similar; the **thalamus suits dystonic tremor**;
    - Class 1 evidence in refractory generalised or segmental dystonia, and in cervical dystonia;
    - about **20% non-responders**; slow benefit;
    - **better outcome:** isolated generalised dystonia (especially **DYT-TOR1A**), young age, short duration, mobile dystonia, normal MRI;
    - **worse outcome:** **functional dystonia**, fixed contractures, **psychiatric or cognitive comorbidity**, older age.
  - **Focused ultrasound:** unilateral only; may recur.
  - Peripheral denervation is **not recommended**.
- **rTMS and tDCS:** investigational.
- **Status dystonicus** (fatal in **10%**):
  - triggers: infection, medication change, **DBS hardware failure**;
  - differentials: **NMS**, serotonin syndrome, malignant hyperthermia;
  - **management:** treat triggers; oral tetrabenazine and trihexyphenidyl; then ICU with **IV midazolam**, then propofol; **rescue bilateral GPi DBS** (successful in 33.7%).

**Psychiatric relevance:**
- dystonia is frequently associated with **neuropsychiatric symptoms**, which should be treated;
- cognitive decline occurs only in **degenerative** dystonias.

⚠️ **Discrepancies with Lishman (above), not resolved:**
- **Antipsychotics:** Lishman lists **neuroleptics** (pimozide, haloperidol) among generalised-dystonia drugs; Continuum says antidopaminergics **should be avoided**.
- **Anticholinergic response:** Lishman says they help "about half"; Continuum says **71%**.
- **Botulinum toxin antibodies:** Lishman gives about **7.5%** secondary non-response; Continuum says neutralising antibodies are **rare**. Duration: Lishman gives 2–4 months; Continuum gives 12–24 weeks.
- **Gene names:** Lishman's DYT1/DYT5/DYT11 labels are now superseded by the gene-based names.

**Conclusion.** Hyperkinetic disorders are best recognised by keywords:
- myoclonus: "shock-like";
- chorea: "randomly flowing jerks";
- tics: "stereotyped" and suppressible;
- tremor: "rhythmic";
- dystonia: "abnormal posture".

The approach to dystonia has six steps:
1. recognise its features (directionality, patterning, sensory trick, task specificity);
2. classify it by distribution, age and cause;
3. exclude mimics;
4. use the four-question approach to define the syndrome and focused work-up;
5. always consider drug-induced dystonia;
6. consider functional dystonia;
7. look for a secondary cause (Wilson's under 50; levodopa trial in the young);
8. treat: botulinum toxin for focal dystonia, anticholinergics and others for generalised, surgery or DBS for refractory cases;
9. address psychiatric comorbidity.

## In depth

### Commonly seen mixed movement disorders (Table 2) [1]

| Combination | Possible aetiology |
| --- | --- |
| Tremor + akinesia | PD or atypical parkinsonism |
| Parkinsonism, ataxia, autonomic failure, spasticity, myoclonus | Multiple system atrophy |
| Vertical supranuclear gaze palsy, falls, symmetrical parkinsonism | PSP |
| Asymmetric akinesia, rigidity, myoclonus, dystonia, apraxia | Corticobasal degeneration |
| Chorea, dystonia, bradykinesia | Huntington's disease |
| Dystonia + tremor | Primary dystonia |
| Rest and postural tremor, dystonia, akinetic–rigid syndrome | Wilson's disease |
| Ataxia + myoclonus | Mitochondrial disease, coeliac disease, Unverricht–Lundborg disease |

### Psychiatric relevance (from the sources)
- **Misdiagnosis:** movement disorders are often misdiagnosed as psychogenic, and vice versa [1].
- **Parkinsonism mimics (Box 2):** depression, obsessive slowness and **catatonia** [1].

### Not covered by the sources (deliberately left blank)
- Botulinum toxin **unit doses** per muscle (Continuum gives intervals and formulations, not unit doses).
- MRI patterns of specific secondary dystonias.
- Specific treatment of myoclonus. (Chorea is covered in the Huntington's answer and tremor in the separate tremor answer, both from Continuum 2022.)
