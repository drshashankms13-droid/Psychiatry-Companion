## Exam answer

### Classification of movement disorders (Box 1) [1]
- **Insufficient movement:** akinetic, hypokinetic or bradykinetic (akinetic–rigid) syndromes.
- **Too much movement (hyperkinesias or dyskinesias):**
  - **jerky:** myoclonus (including excessive startle), chorea (including ballism), tics;
  - **non-jerky:** dystonia (including athetosis), tremor.

**Burden** [1]:
- **Parkinson's disease:** 1% at ages 65–85 and 4.3% over 85.
- **Essential tremor:** 4% over 40 and 14% over 65.
- **Tics:** up to 21% of school-age children.

**Why classification matters** [1]:
- it focuses investigations, rather than a "scattergun" work-up;
- it has prognostic implications (e.g., essential tremor vs early PD);
- it has genetic implications (e.g., Huntington's disease);
- it guides treatment.

### Hyperkinetic disorders: definitions, keywords and examples [1]

| Disorder | Definition | Keyword | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Myoclonus** | Sudden, brief, **shock-like** involuntary movements. *Positive* (muscle contraction) or *negative* (loss of tone, e.g., asterixis) | "Shock-like" | Hypnic jerks (physiological); essential; epileptic; symptomatic (**hepatic or uraemic encephalopathy**, post-anoxic). Cortical (focal, action- or stimulus-sensitive), subcortical/brainstem (generalised, auditory-sensitive), spinal (propriospinal; often functional), peripheral. Excessive **startle** belongs to the myoclonus spectrum |
| **Chorea** | Abrupt, unpredictable, nonrhythmic movements from a continuous random flow of contractions, flitting between body parts ("fidgeting"). **Motor impersistence** ("milkmaid's grip"); hung-up reflexes. Patients may mask it in voluntary acts | "Randomly flowing jerks" | **Huntington's disease**; antiphospholipid syndrome; levodopa-induced dyskinesia. **Ballism**: large-amplitude proximal chorea, usually **hemiballism from a contralateral subthalamic nucleus lesion**; bilateral ballism is mostly metabolic |
| **Tics** | Recurrent stereotyped movements or sounds, with a premonitory urge, relief, **suppressibility** and rebound. Simple or complex; motor or phonic | "Stereotyped" | Tourette's syndrome. Differential: **stereotypies** (longer, patterned, rhythmic, purposeless; autism, intellectual disability, Rett syndrome, blindness) |
| **Tremor** | Involuntary, **rhythmic, sinusoidal** oscillation of one or more body parts. Amplitude varies; frequency fixed | "Rhythmicity" | Rest (**PD**); postural (physiological, enhanced, **essential tremor**); kinetic and intention (**cerebellar**); task-specific (dystonic writing tremor); isometric; **orthostatic (14–18 Hz)**; **Holmes/rubral (2–3 Hz; rest, postural and intention)**; dystonic tremor; functional (variable, distractible) |
| **Dystonia** | Involuntary sustained co-contraction of antagonists, causing **abnormal postures** and twisting, repetitive movements | "Abnormal posture" | Torticollis, writer's cramp, musician's dystonia, blepharospasm, DYT11 myoclonus–dystonia, dopa-responsive dystonia. **Athetosis** = distal "mobile dystonia" (slow, writhing) |

**Tremor classification (MDS consensus; Table 1)** [1]:
- **Rest:** best judged in a fully supported limb. PD tremor **re-emerges after a delay** when a posture is held ("resetting").
- **Action:**
  - **postural** (physiological, enhanced, essential);
  - **kinetic:** simple (essential), **intention** (cerebellar), task-specific (dystonic writing tremor);
  - **isometric.**
- **Combinations:** severe essential tremor, atypical parkinsonism, dystonic tremor, Holmes tremor.

**Clinical pearls** [1]:
- **Cogwheeling** is the palpable tremor itself. In essential or dystonic tremor there is no accompanying rigidity or akinesia, unlike PD.
- **Orthostatic tremor** can be heard with a stethoscope (a "helicopter" sound).
- **Dystonic tremor** can mimic PD (reduced arm swing), but there is **no true akinesia** and **DaT imaging is normal**. It is often misclassified as essential tremor. Look for "dinner-fork" posture or ulnar fingers pointing down.
- **Palatal myoclonus** is now called **palatal tremor** (it is rhythmic).
- **Mimics of myoclonus** (Box 2): tics, tremor, fasciculations, myokymia, chorea.

### Approach to a patient with dystonia

**1. Recognise dystonia** [1]:
- **Keyword:** "abnormal posture". Dystonia is the only movement disorder visible in a still image.
- **Consistent directionality:** e.g., torticollis does not switch direction.
- **Patterned**, repeatedly involving the same muscles.
- **Mobile early**, becoming more fixed over time.
- **Sensory trick (geste antagoniste):** e.g., touching the cheek relieves torticollis; chewing gum relieves oromandibular dystonia.
- **Action-induced** and often **task-specific** (writer's cramp; musician's dystonia). Leg dystonia may impair walking forwards but not backwards or running. Ask the patient to bring the instrument or a home video.
- **Fixed posture at onset suggests a functional cause**, though fixed dystonia can occur early in **corticobasal degeneration** [1].

**2. Classify** [1]:
- **By distribution:** focal, segmental, multifocal, generalised, hemidystonia.
- **By age at onset:** early (**26 or younger**) or late (26 or older).
- **By cause:**
  - **primary:** dystonia only, with or without tremor;
  - **dystonia-plus:** e.g., with parkinsonism in **dopa-responsive dystonia**; ataxia; **myoclonus–dystonia (DYT11, ε-sarcoglycan; alcohol-responsive)**;
  - **degenerative**;
  - **secondary:** other neurological features plus an identifiable cause.

**3. Exclude mimics (Box 2)** [1]:

| Region | Mimics |
| --- | --- |
| **Craniocervical (torticollis)** | Retropharyngeal abscess, atlanto-axial subluxation, congenital muscular torticollis, **ocular torticollis** (head tilt for cranial nerve palsy), posterior fossa mass, Sandifer syndrome, dropped-head syndrome |
| **Limb** | Contracture, spasticity, paresis or atrophy, myotonia or neuromyotonia, **pseudoathetosis** (sensory ataxia), **stiff-person syndrome**, tonic spasms, seizures or epilepsia partialis continua |
| **Facial** | Ptosis, trismus, hemimasticatory spasm, **hemifacial spasm**, myotonia, tetany, apraxia of eyelid opening |

**4. Ask the four key questions (Figure 1)** [1]:
1. Which movement disorders are present? Mixed disorders are the rule.
2. Which is **dominant**?
3. What are the **associated neurological and non-neurological features**?
4. What is the differential diagnosis, and therefore which **focused work-up**?

**Associated features that point to a cause** [1]:
- **Kayser–Fleischer rings** suggest **Wilson's disease** (tremor at rest and posture, dystonia, akinetic–rigid syndrome).
- **Ataxia** with dystonia: prioritise the work-up for ataxia, especially hereditary causes.
- **Alcohol-responsive** head jerks with torticollis: **DYT11**.
- **Attacks triggered by sudden movement:** paroxysmal kinesigenic dyskinesia.
- **Family history and ethnicity:** e.g., Filipino ethnicity with family history suggests **DYT3 (lubag)**.
- **Asymmetric akinesia, rigidity, myoclonus, dystonia and apraxia:** corticobasal degeneration.
- **Chorea, dystonia and bradykinesia:** Huntington's disease.

**5. Always consider drugs** [1, 3]:
- **Dopamine-blocking agents** cause acute dystonia within hours (minutes after IM), tardive dystonia after more than 3 months, and other movement disorders.
- **Anticholinergics** can cause chorea in primary dystonia.
- **Effects can persist for months** after the drug is stopped, so obtain a complete drug history [1].
- **Acute drug-induced dystonia:** treated with parenteral anticholinergics (benztropine 2 mg or diphenhydramine 50 mg) [3].

**6. Consider functional dystonia** [2]:
- **Typical features:** **fixed** ankle plantar-flexion/inversion, or fist with pincer grasp spared; pain; resistance; **no overflow or sensory trick**.
- **Response:** dramatic response to placebo or physiotherapy.
- **Post-traumatic dystonia:** most "post-traumatic" or CRPS dystonia is probably functional.
- **Prognosis:** functional dystonia has the worst FMD prognosis (about 80% unchanged or worse).

**Conclusion.** Hyperkinetic disorders are best recognised by keywords:
- myoclonus: "shock-like";
- chorea: "randomly flowing jerks";
- tics: "stereotyped" and suppressible;
- tremor: "rhythmic";
- dystonia: "abnormal posture".

The approach to dystonia has six steps:
1. recognise its features (directionality, patterning, sensory trick, task specificity);
2. classify it by distribution, age and cause;
3. exclude mimics;
4. use the four-question approach to define the syndrome and focused work-up;
5. always consider drug-induced dystonia;
6. consider functional dystonia.

## In depth

### Commonly seen mixed movement disorders (Table 2) [1]

| Combination | Possible aetiology |
| --- | --- |
| Tremor + akinesia | PD or atypical parkinsonism |
| Parkinsonism, ataxia, autonomic failure, spasticity, myoclonus | Multiple system atrophy |
| Vertical supranuclear gaze palsy, falls, symmetrical parkinsonism | PSP |
| Asymmetric akinesia, rigidity, myoclonus, dystonia, apraxia | Corticobasal degeneration |
| Chorea, dystonia, bradykinesia | Huntington's disease |
| Dystonia + tremor | Primary dystonia |
| Rest and postural tremor, dystonia, akinetic–rigid syndrome | Wilson's disease |
| Ataxia + myoclonus | Mitochondrial disease, coeliac disease, Unverricht–Lundborg disease |

### Psychiatric relevance (from the sources)
- **Misdiagnosis:** movement disorders are often misdiagnosed as psychogenic, and vice versa [1].
- **Parkinsonism mimics (Box 2):** depression, obsessive slowness and **catatonia** [1].
