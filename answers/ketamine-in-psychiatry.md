## Exam answer

**Introduction.** Ketamine is an **uncompetitive NMDA receptor antagonist** and a **dissociative anaesthetic**. Over the past two decades it has emerged as a novel and effective **rapid-acting antidepressant** [1, p357].

**Pharmacology.** Ketamine is a **racemic mixture** of equal amounts of **(S)-ketamine (esketamine)** and **(R)-ketamine (arketamine)**. Esketamine binds the NMDA receptor more potently [1, p357].

**Licensing.**
- Racemic ketamine remains **off-label** for treatment-resistant depression (TRD).
- **Esketamine nasal spray (Spravato)** is approved for TRD, together with an oral antidepressant, in Europe and the USA [1, p357].
- Esketamine is not yet approved by NICE in the UK but is widely used elsewhere [1, p336].

### Landmark evidence [1, p357]
- **Berman et al., 2000:** an RCT of a single **subanaesthetic IV dose, 0.5 mg/kg over 40 minutes**, in people with MDD. The antidepressant effect appeared **within hours** and increased progressively **up to 3 days**.
- **Replication:** several trials in **unipolar and bipolar depression**, including treatment-resistant patients.
- **A 2023 trial in TRD:** antidepressant augmentation with **nasal esketamine gave greater benefit than quetiapine**. Benefits for both were evident immediately but **continued to build over 32 weeks**. Maudsley suggests the term "rapid-acting antidepressant" might be reconsidered in light of this.

### Mechanism (not fully clear) [1, p357]
1. Ketamine blocks **NMDA receptors on GABAergic interneurons**, which normally suppress glutamate release from glutamatergic neurons.
2. This **disinhibition** causes an **acute cortical glutamate surge**.
3. The surge activates **post-synaptic AMPA receptors**.
4. Downstream effects follow on **synaptogenesis and neuroplastic pathways**.

### Clinical uses described in the source

| Use | What Maudsley says |
| --- | --- |
| Treatment-resistant unipolar depression | Very rapid response (within hours), including on suicidality; high remission rate; some evidence of maintained response with repeated doses. Listed among "less commonly used treatments, well supported by published evidence" (Table 3.4) [1, p351] |
| Early-stage treatment resistance (after one failed antidepressant) | (Es)ketamine is among the adjuncts shown effective, with risperidone, lithium and aripiprazole [1, p346] |
| Augmentation in TRD | Recent meta-analyses suggest robust efficacy for lithium, quetiapine, D2 partial agonists and ketamine [1, p280] |
| Suicidality | Ketamine, in its various forms, may have rapidly apparent anti-suicidal effects [1, p339] |
| Refractory bipolar depression | IV 0.5 mg/kg is effective, with a very high response rate; dissociation is common but brief. "Now accepted as standard treatment for refractory bipolar depression." IV racemate is possibly more effective than intranasal esketamine. Switching to mania is a potential but probably remote risk [1, p321]. Recent meta-analyses: probably effective, but with **low certainty of evidence** [1, p319] |
| Psychotic depression | Possibly efficacious. Evidence is very small: case series, case reports, one chart review (n=12) and one secondary RCT analysis (n=10). Depressive and, in a few cases, psychotic symptoms improved; no worsening of psychosis was reported. A 2023 add-on study (0.5 mg/kg IV twice weekly, n=36) found improvement in all domains. "Not first-line agents" [1, pp363–364] |
| Postnatal depression prevention | Perinatal or prenatal ketamine/esketamine reduces the risk of postnatal depression by about **50–75%** [1, p357] |
| Comparison with ECT | Ketamine appears **broadly as effective as ECT** [1, p357] |
| As ECT anaesthetic | p357 says it may enhance the effect of ECT. The ECT chapter says ketamine/esketamine anaesthesia **does not ultimately improve outcome**, though ketamine may give short-term improvement early in the course [1, p384]. The two statements conflict; the p384 statement is the more specific |
| Acute behavioural disturbance | Widely used in emergency departments. In a review of 18 studies, a mean of 315 mg IM gave sedation in about 7.2 minutes, but over 30% of 650 patients were intubated and more than 1% had laryngospasm. Third line, at **2–5 mg/kg IM, only where intubation facilities exist** [1, p65] |

### Routes and doses (Table 3.6) [1, pp357–359]
The optimal route is not established. **IV 0.5 mg/kg over 40 minutes is the gold standard**, with the best evidence, and may be more effective than intranasal use.

| Route | Dose | Frequency and notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **IV** | 0.5 mg/kg over 40 minutes; up to 1.0 mg/kg if no response (limited support above 0.5); titrate from 0.25 mg/kg in older people | Induction once or twice weekly. Maintenance according to response: weekly, then every 2 weeks, or even monthly; may be supplemented with oral or sublingual doses. Clinical setting only |
| **SC** | As IV (bolus) | As IV; may be better tolerated than IV or IM |
| **IM** | As IV (bolus) | As IV |
| **Oral** | 0.5–5.0 mg/kg | Regular lower doses 0.5–2.0 mg/kg every 1–3 days, or intermittent higher doses 2.0–5.0 mg/kg once or twice weekly to maintain IV/SC/IM response. Can be taken at home; effect less rapid |
| **Sublingual** | 0.5–3.0 mg/kg | Regular lower doses 0.5–1.5 mg/kg every 1–3 days, or 1.5–3.0 mg/kg once or twice weekly. Held under the tongue for 5 minutes, then swallowed. Home use possible |
| **Intranasal esketamine** | 56–84 mg (28 mg in older people); 28 mg = two sprays | Twice weekly, then weekly, then every 2 weeks. Clinical setting; observe for about 2 hours |

### Adverse effects [1, pp351, 358]
- **Dissociation:** perceptual distortions, which can cause significant anxiety. Less likely with lower oral or sublingual doses.
- **Cognitive effects:** confusion, among others.
- **Cardiovascular:** transient rise in BP, tachycardia and arrhythmias.
- **Laryngospasm:** rare.
- **Diversion and illegal use:** a risk the prescriber must consider.
- **Ketamine-induced cystitis** with long-term illicit use: urinary pain, bladder epithelial damage, reduced bladder capacity, ureteric stenosis and kidney failure. Not widely reported in therapeutic use, but it remains a concern.
- Adverse effects **may have been underestimated**.

### Safety protocol [1, pp351, 358–359]
- **Before treatment:** physical examination, baseline BP, FBC, LFT, TFT and **ECG**.
- **Monitoring:** BP and heart rate during and after each dose.
- **Observation:** by a trained clinician during dosing and for **1 hour** after ketamine, or about **2 hours** after esketamine. The clinician must have **intermediate or advanced life support** training.
- **Home oral or sublingual use:** only after a **supervised test dose**. Patients should not drive, operate machinery or do other high-risk activities for at least 1 hour.
- **Repeated treatment** is necessary to maintain the effect.

**Conclusion.** Maudsley presents ketamine as follows:
- **Treatment-resistant depression:** well supported but **not first-line**.
- **Refractory bipolar depression:** standard treatment.
- **Suicidality and psychotic depression:** a possible role.

IV 0.5 mg/kg is the reference regimen, and nasal esketamine is the licensed alternative. Use is limited by the need for clinical administration, cardiovascular and dissociative effects, misuse potential and the need for repeated dosing.

## In depth

### Background [1, pp357–358]
- **Berman 2000** is the landmark RCT; it has been replicated in unipolar and bipolar TRD.
- **Esketamine** is licensed in Europe and the USA; racemic ketamine is off-label.
- **Novel oral formulations** are under development.

### Where ketamine sits in the TRD pathway
- **Table 3.3 — commonly used, well supported** [1, pp348–349]:
  - add aripiprazole;
  - add lithium;
  - olanzapine + fluoxetine;
  - add quetiapine;
  - SSRI + bupropion;
  - SSRI or venlafaxine + mianserin or mirtazapine.
- **Table 3.4 — less commonly used, well supported** [1, p351]:
  - **ketamine/esketamine** (IV 0.5 mg/kg over 40 minutes; intranasal esketamine 28–84 mg; oral 0.5–1.25 mg/kg effective but no licensed product);
  - lamotrigine;
  - ECT;
  - tri-iodothyronine.
- **Table 3.5 — other reported treatments, limited support** [1, pp353–354]: includes the NMDA antagonist **dextromethorphan + quinidine** ("promising novel treatment") and **nitrous oxide** (short-lived effect).

### Mechanism in detail
The source describes only the disinhibition model given above. It states that **the precise mechanisms are not clear** [1, p357].

### Practical points from Table 3.6 [1, pp358–359]
- Escalation above 0.5 mg/kg IV has limited support.
- Lower oral or sublingual doses are well tolerated but act less rapidly than IV, SC or IM doses.
- Higher intermittent oral or sublingual doses are a practical way to **maintain** response after parenteral induction. Titrate to response and adverse effects.
- Very-low-dose sublingual regimens (10 mg every 2–3 days or weekly) have limited evidence.

### Critical appraisal (points made in the source)
- **Route and dose:** neither is established; non-IV routes need more research [1, p357].
- **Bipolar depression:** meta-analytic certainty is low [1, p319], even though Maudsley calls ketamine standard treatment for refractory cases [1, p321].
- **The "rapid-acting" label:** challenged by the 32-week esketamine data [1, p357].
- **Psychotic depression:** evidence rests on very small samples, because such patients are usually excluded from trials over concern about transient worsening of psychosis [1, p363].
- **ECT anaesthesia:** the source's statements conflict (p357 vs p384).
- **Safety:** adverse effects may be underestimated, and cystitis remains a concern in therapeutic use [1, pp351, 358].

### Second source: NIMHANS Depression Primer [2, pp47–48]
- **Efficacy:** a meta-analysis of 21 double-blind placebo-controlled RCTs (IV, intranasal and oral) in TRD gave an effect size of **0.529**.
- **Duration of effect:** transient; symptoms return within a few days to a week. Ketamine is therefore reserved for **severe TRD with high risk of harm to self**.
- **IV protocol:** 0.5 mg/kg over 40 minutes, with **continuous monitoring of pulse, BP, ECG, respiratory rate and SpO2**. Twice- and thrice-weekly schedules are both used; there is limited data to favour either.
- **Maintenance is not currently recommended:** aim for gradual tapering and discontinuation. This **conflicts with Maudsley**, which gives maintenance schedules. Present both views in an exam.
- **Acute adverse effects:**
  - psychotomimetic: perceptual disturbance, depersonalisation, derealisation;
  - cardiovascular: raised heart rate and BP, dizziness, palpitations;
  - neurological: sedation, confusion, headache, acute memory disturbance.
- **Chronic-use effects:** urological toxicity, cognitive impairment, hepatotoxicity, abuse and dependence.
