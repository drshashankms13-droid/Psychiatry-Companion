## Exam answer

### Concept and framework [1]
- **Cognitive rehabilitation** is the planned use of therapy to reduce the **impact of cognitive impairment** after acquired brain injury (TBI, stroke and other causes).
- **WHO ICIDH framework:**
  - **impairment**: the direct effect on psychological performance;
  - **disability**: the effect on activities;
  - **handicap**: social consequences that depend on the person's circumstances. Lishman's example: the same left hemiplegia affects a violinist far more than a right-handed painter.
- **Two broad strategies** (Ylvisaker 2002):
  1. **Restorative:** reduce the **impairment** (e.g., memory drills and exercises, attention retraining).
  2. **Compensatory:** reduce **disability and handicap**:
     - electronic memory aids, **NeuroPage** (an individually programmed pager; reduced memory failures and improved planning: Wilson 2001);
     - **errorless learning**; mnemonics;
     - environmental adaptation (an electric cooker with a timer instead of gas).
- **Evidence:**
  - **compensatory approaches aimed at disability and handicap appear more effective** than those aimed at impairment (Wilson 2002);
  - some treatments may be effective (Cicerone 2000), but many studies measure **outcomes close to the intervention** (e.g., processing speed) rather than quality of life or handicap. Outcome measures must matter to patients and carers.

### General principles [1]
1. **Full psychometric assessment** first, to define deficits **and preserved functions to build on**. Memory is evaluated comprehensively from the outset.
2. **Secure the confidence and cooperation** of the patient; keep relatives informed.
3. **Optimism and enthusiasm**, with allowance for fatigue. The **therapist's personality** matters.
4. **Graded, realistic goals** acceptable to the patient (Wilson 2002). Early mastery of simple tasks builds self-esteem; **goal planning** underpins most programmes.
5. **Tailor tasks** to needs and inclinations.
6. **Maintain physical health; detect depression;** allow for personality change.
7. **Motivation:** encouragement from the team and awareness of progress. **Motivational interviewing** after stroke improved mood at 3 months but not disability (Watkins 2007).
8. **Simple language, methodical repetition, practical demonstration**, slow pace, and allowance for day-to-day variability.
9. **Family involvement** throughout; social worker early; discharge planning.

### Domain-specific techniques [1, 2]

| Domain | Techniques and evidence |
| --- | --- |
| **Memory** | Assume impairment may not change; use **compensatory strategies** to reduce disability. **General:** organising material, **chunking**, breaking skills into steps. **Specific:** first-letter cueing, rhymes, **visual imagery**, motor coding, **peg lists or loci**, embedding items in a short story. **Errorless learning is effective; vanishing cues less robust** (Kessels & de Haan 2003). External aids: notes, written instructions, NeuroPage. Few stroke-specific trials |
| **Attention** | Theory-driven drills (repetitive responses to stimuli) vs **functional training of specific skills**: the latter is generally **more effective** (Park & Ingles 2001 meta-analysis) |
| **Executive / problem-solving** | **Autobiographical cueing** (recalling specific past examples) was effective (Hewitt 2006). The **dysexecutive syndrome is often very difficult to treat** because of limited insight |
| **Language** | Communication strategies (pictures, cards, gestures, consistent instructions). Formal SLT efficacy is **unproven in RCTs** (Cochrane 2000) but helps morale (see the aphasia answer) |
| **Neglect** | Attentional cueing to the neglected side (theory-driven). **No good evidence** that stimulation or exercises beat spontaneous recovery (see the neglect answer) |
| **ADLs / praxis** | **Occupational therapy**, retraining in dressing, feeding, washing and cooking, simple mechanical aids. Community OT improves ADLs and mobility |
| **Behaviour** (with cognition) | Behavioural analysis (ABC diary); positive reinforcement; **time out / TOOTS**; **differential reinforcement of other behaviour (DRO)**; token economy. Needs a **consistent staff approach**; supported by single-case studies, not RCTs. Cognitive therapy techniques for maladaptive thinking; family education about **expressed emotion** |

### Service models [1]
- **Multidisciplinary team:** neurologists, rehabilitation physicians, psychologists, **psychiatrists**, physiotherapists, OTs, SLTs, social workers. Head-injured patients otherwise "fall between two stools".
- **Inpatient or day intensive rehabilitation:**
  - **early transfer** prevents boredom, agitation and sedation on general wards;
  - **holistic programmes** (Prigatano; Ben-Yishay) combine cognitive, behavioural and emotional work;
  - Burke et al.: even severe, behaviourally disturbed patients improved (half living independently, half employed).
- **Salazar 2000 RCT:** **home-based low-intensity** rehabilitation was **as effective** as inpatient for most moderate–severe TBI. Inpatient care was better for those **unconscious for over 1 hour**, or with poor safety awareness, behavioural problems or poor home support.
- **Transitional living units** (halfway houses), then the community.
- **Community outreach:** home-based therapy reduces disability **even years after injury** (Powell 2002 RCT). Structured weekly timetable; **return-to-work** planning.
- **Stroke:** organised **stroke-unit** care improves survival and independence. **Box 8.6 principles:**
  - prevent complications and maximise function;
  - secondary prevention;
  - early assessment;
  - standardised tools;
  - goal-based evidence-based interventions;
  - a coordinated team **with family as members**;
  - education;
  - community reintegration.
  - Home-based therapy after discharge spares about **7 per 100** a poor outcome. The EFNS task force (Cappa 2005) summarised cognitive-rehabilitation evidence.

### Other conditions [1, 2, 3]
- **Dementia:** validation, **reality orientation** and **cognitive stimulation therapy** (Spector 2003) have variable evidence (see the BPSD answer) [1].
- **Schizophrenia:** **cognitive remediation** improves cognitive test scores (partly practice effects) with **small gains in functioning**. No psychological treatment is yet approved for cognitive symptoms. Social-cognition training shows potential [2].
- **HIV-associated neurocognitive disorder:** "cognitive rehabilitation is an essential treatment component", alongside CNS-penetrant ART and treatment of depression and substance use [3].

**Conclusion.** Cognitive rehabilitation is a goal-directed, multidisciplinary process. It is grounded in assessment of deficits and strengths and is framed by impairment, disability and handicap. Compensatory and functional approaches (memory aids, NeuroPage, errorless learning, task-specific training) outperform impairment-level drills, and outcomes should matter to the patient. It must be integrated with behavioural, emotional and family work. It can be delivered as intensive inpatient, home-based or community outreach programmes. Its evidence is moderate for TBI and stroke but weak for neglect and formal aphasia therapy, and it is modest for schizophrenia (cognitive remediation).
