## Exam answer

**Introduction.** Vitamin B12 (cobalamin) is one of the eight B vitamins. Its metabolic role is closely intertwined with folate, and it is vital for DNA synthesis, methylation and mitochondrial metabolism [1].

- **Classic presentation:** effects are seen mainly in the **blood and nervous system**.
- **Neurological predominance:** neurological manifestations often predominate and can occur **without anaemia**. These include sensory and motor disturbances (especially in the legs), ataxia, **cognitive decline leading to dementia, and psychiatric disorders** [1].
- **Burden:** clinical deficiency is relatively uncommon. **Subclinical deficiency affects 2.5–26%** of the general population, depending on the definition [1].

### Causes (Box 1) [1]
- **Pernicious anaemia:** autoimmune loss of intrinsic factor. Affects 2–3% of people aged 65 and over; the major risk factor in high-income countries.
- **Gastric disease or surgery:** gastrectomy, gastric bypass.
- **Atrophic gastritis, and drugs that reduce gastric acid:** **proton pump inhibitors**, H2 antagonists, antacids.
- **Pancreatic disease; ileal disease or resection;** parasitic infestation; bacterial overgrowth.
- **Drugs:** **metformin**, cholestyramine.
- **Diet:** malnutrition, **vegetarian or vegan diet**, **chronic alcoholism**. This is the main cause in low-income countries.
- **Inherited disorders** of B12 transport and metabolism.
- **Other:** **HIV infection**, **nitrous oxide** anaesthesia. Nitrous oxide irreversibly oxidises methylcobalamin, so deficiency can be precipitous.
- **Contributing factors:** *H. pylori*, smoking, malaria, tuberculosis.

**Indian data:** in Pune, deficiency (<150 pmol/L) was found in 16% at age 6, 24% at 12 and 58% at 18 [1].

### Mechanisms of neuropsychiatric damage [1]
1. **Demyelination** of peripheral and central neurons causes the classic **myeloneuropathy**. The long white-matter tracts of the **posterior and lateral columns** (vibration and position sense) are especially vulnerable; motor myelination can also be affected.
2. **Methionine synthase failure:** loss of methyl-B12 leads to homocysteine accumulation and reduced methionine and **S-adenosylmethionine** (the methyl donor for DNA and histone methylation). This is considered the main cause of the neurological lesions.
3. **Homocysteine toxicity:** cellular stress, apoptosis, protein homocysteinylation. Microvascular disease from hyperhomocysteinaemia.
4. **Methylmalonic acid accumulation:** abnormal fatty acids. Theoretical, with scant evidence.
5. **Oxidative stress and inflammation:** reduced SIRT1, impaired heat-shock proteins, reactive oxygen species, ER stress.
6. **Neurodegeneration links:** reduced methylation of the promoters of the **amyloid precursor protein and γ-secretase genes** raises amyloid-β. Altered PP2A, NGF and TNF affect myelin and the **amyloid and tau pathways**.

### Clinical features (Box 3) [1]

| Domain | Manifestations |
| --- | --- |
| **Spinal cord** | **Subacute combined degeneration of the spinal cord** |
| **Peripheral nerve** | Impaired sensory and peripheral nerve function; paraesthesia |
| **Gait** | Ataxia |
| **Cognition** | **Cognitive impairment**; cognitive decline leading to **dementia**. In older adults, low B12 with high homocysteine predicts **accelerated brain atrophy** and incident cognitive impairment |
| **Mood and behaviour** | **Depression**. Mental symptoms such as **emotional lability, paranoia and irritability** (these respond quickly to treatment) |
| **Other** | Megaloblastic or macrocytic anaemia, pancytopenia, bone disease, hearing loss, macular degeneration |
| **Children** | Lower school performance; impaired mental and social development, short-term memory and attention |
| **Infants** | Stunting, **cerebral atrophy**, hypotonia, lethargy, developmental delay, abnormal EEG; difficulty swallowing; delayed verbal development. Maternal deficiency raises the risk of **neural tube defects** |

**Key clinical point:** neurological features **can precede, or occur entirely without, haematological changes** [1].

### Diagnosis [1]

**Who to test:**
1. Patients with clinical features: macrocytic anaemia and/or neurological symptoms.
2. A larger group with non-specific symptoms:
   - elderly people;
   - vegans;
   - impaired fertility;
   - gastrointestinal disease;
   - **unexplained neurological or haematological symptoms**.

**Elderly patients:**
- Deficiency is hard to recognise because its features are easily confused with other diseases of ageing, **most notably dementia**.
- Anaemia is present in fewer than half.
- MMA and homocysteine are less sensitive because of reduced kidney function.
- Management therefore relies heavily on clinical judgement.

**Biomarkers (Table 1):**

| Test | Reference range | Deficiency cut-off | Confounders |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Serum B12 (first line) | 200–600 pmol/L | <148 | Binding-protein changes. Spuriously normal with anti-intrinsic-factor antibodies; high in liver disease and myeloproliferative disorders |
| Holotranscobalamin (active B12) | 40–100 pmol/L | <35 | TCN2 variants, kidney function. Only marginally better than total B12 |
| Homocysteine | 8–15 µmol/L | >15 | Folate and B6 deficiency, kidney and thyroid function, age, sex |
| Methylmalonic acid | 0.04–0.37 µmol/L | >0.37 | Kidney function |
| 4cB12 (combined index) | −2.5 to 1.5 | <−0.5 | Corrected for folate and age; associates better with cognition and nerve conduction |

**Diagnostic approach:**
- Serum B12 or holotranscobalamin first. If the result is in the grey zone, measure MMA; homocysteine can substitute in folate-replete populations.
- Before committing to lifelong treatment, document the deficiency (raised MMA or homocysteine, intrinsic factor antibodies, or a relevant surgical history).
- **Empirical high-dose B12 while awaiting results does no harm.**
- **Firm clinical suspicion warrants immediate treatment.**

### Management (Box 4) [1]

| Situation | Regimen |
| --- | --- |
| Malabsorption: parenteral | **1,000 µg cyano- or hydroxo-B12 IM daily or alternate days for 1 week**, then weekly up to 8 weeks, then every 3–4 weeks. Hydroxocobalamin can be given every 2–3 months after initial treatment |
| Malabsorption: oral | Cyano-B12 **2,000 µg daily** until remission, then 1,000–2,000 µg daily. Elderly and post-bypass patients should continue daily high doses |
| Dietary deficiency | High oral dose daily to replace stores over 3–4 months, then at least 6 µg daily |
| Infants | 250–1,000 µg IM daily, then weekly until recovery; then 1–2 µg orally. Treat the mother as well |
| **Severe neurological abnormalities** | **Treat aggressively: daily injections for a week, then weekly until stable** |

**Other points:**
- **Oral vs injection:** high-dose oral B12 (1,000–2,000 µg) is equivalent or superior to injection in randomised studies. Only 0.5–4% of an oral dose is absorbed.
- **Forms:** there is no advantage of methyl- or adenosyl-B12 over the cyano or hydroxo forms.
- **Lifelong treatment:** for non-nutritional causes, patients must understand that treatment is lifelong.
- **High folate:** may aggravate B12 deficiency, by an unknown mechanism.

### Response and prognosis [1]
- **Blood:** reticulocytosis in about 5 days; red cell count normal in 4–6 weeks.
- **Neurological recovery:** slower. **The exception is mental symptoms (emotional lability, paranoia, irritability), which may improve rapidly.**
- **Transient worsening:** paraesthesia may transiently worsen or appear in the first week.
- **Outcome predictors:** the duration and severity of deficits before treatment predict outcome. Paraesthesia without sensory loss or weakness is the most likely to resolve completely. Spinal imaging shows rapid correction of demyelination.
- **Permanent deficits:** severe demyelination can leave permanent loss of proprioception, sensation and power. **Improvement generally stops after one year of adequate therapy.**
- **Progression despite treatment** suggests another cause.
- **Brain ageing:** low B12 is a risk factor for **age-related cognitive decline and cerebral atrophy** and is associated with incident dementia. B12-containing supplements **delay these changes**, and homocysteine-lowering vitamins reduced the rate of brain atrophy in one study.
- **Children:** prolonged deficiency in infants can cause permanent developmental disability.

**Conclusion.** B12 deficiency is a common, treatable cause of myeloneuropathy, cognitive decline, dementia and mood and behavioural disturbance, often **without anaemia**, especially in the elderly. Screen at-risk groups:
- older people;
- vegetarians;
- metformin or PPI users;
- alcohol misuse;
- nitrous oxide exposure;
- unexplained neuropsychiatric symptoms.

Treat promptly and aggressively when neurological features are present, because damage may be irreversible.

## In depth

### Why dementia and B12 overlap [1]
- **Biological link:** low B12 and high homocysteine reduce methylation of APP and γ-secretase gene promoters, raising amyloid-β. S-adenosylmethionine reverses this and improves spatial memory in experimental models.
- **Population evidence:** NHANES data show higher rates of anaemia and cognitive impairment in older adults with **low B12 combined with high serum folate**.
- **Brain levels:** B12 levels in the brain fall progressively across the lifespan.

### Associated autoimmunity and long-term care [1]
- **Autoimmune disease:** pernicious anaemia carries a risk of other autoimmune disease, especially **autoimmune thyroid disease**, which is relevant to neuropsychiatric work-up.
- **Gastric cancer:** chronic gastritis raises the risk of gastric carcinoid and adenocarcinoma.
- **Other deficiencies:** watch for iron deficiency.
