## Exam answer

### Introduction [1]
- **SLE:** a multisystem autoimmune connective tissue disease.
  - Female:male about **9:1**; mean onset age **30**.
  - Prevalence: under 10 per 100,000 in white Americans; **over 100 per 100,000 in Afro-Caribbean people**.
- **Neuropsychiatric involvement:** in **up to 60%** when patients are comprehensively studied and followed.
  - CNS disease ranks with **renal failure and accelerated atherosclerosis** among the commoner causes of death.
  - Hughes suggested CNS involvement may overtake renal disease as the major problem.
- **Psychiatric presentation:** **occasionally precedes other systemic features**, so screening tests belong in the work-up of some psychiatric patients. Three cases of SLE were found on routine screening of 296 acute psychiatric admissions.
- **Survival:** 5-year survival has risen from 50% (1950s) to over 90%.

### Pathogenesis [1]
1. **Autoimmunity:**
   - anti-DNA, antinuclear and other intracellular antibodies; immune complexes; complement; cytokines (interferon, **IL-6**);
   - antibodies appear **years before** onset;
   - triggers: EBV (Ro mimicry), sunlight, drugs;
   - defective clearance of apoptotic cells.
2. **Small-vessel vasculopathy:**
   - the commonest post-mortem finding is **non-inflammatory proliferative vasculopathy** (intimal proliferation, fibrinoid change) with **multiple small infarcts and microhaemorrhages** in the cortex and brainstem;
   - **true vasculitis in only about 10%**;
   - explains **patchy SPECT defects** and cognitive impairment.
3. **Antiphospholipid antibodies** (anticardiolipin, lupus anticoagulant): the **best evidence**. They cause large-vessel stroke, contribute to small-vessel disease, and may act directly on neurones (seizures).
4. **Brain-reactive antibodies:**
   - **anti-NMDA receptor** (a subset of anti-dsDNA): **high titres associated with depression** (6/11 vs 10/48); neuronal apoptosis in mice;
   - anti-ribosomal P: not confirmed.
5. **Secondary factors:** uraemia, electrolytes, **hypertension**, infection, **steroids**.
6. **Clinicopathological correlation is poor:** severe dysfunction can occur without lesions, and vice versa.

### Clinical features (Table 8.2, mean prevalence across six studies) [1]

| **Cognitive dysfunction** | **38** (3.5–80, test-dependent) | **Subcortical** pattern: attention, memory, **processing speed**, then executive and visuospatial. Usually mild, often **evanescent**, rarely progressive. 75% with CNS involvement vs 25% without. Related to disease activity, **depression**, infarcts and atrophy. Steroids not consistently implicated |
| **Headache** | 28 | Migraine-like (half with aura); rates may not exceed controls |

| **Cerebrovascular disease** | 12 | Stroke and TIA, especially with **antiphospholipid antibodies**; screen young stroke patients for SLE and APS |
| **Seizures** | 10 | More with anticardiolipin antibodies; often no visible lesion |
| **Psychosis** | **4** (point prevalence 2–5%; **12%** lifetime in Lim's series) | Often within **delirium**; may **coincide with flares** |
| **Acute confusional state** | 3 (formerly about **30%**: "lupus psychosis") | Quiet confusion or florid delirium with visual and auditory hallucinations; fluctuating; lasts hours to days |

| **Movement disorder** | 1 | **Chorea** commonest (mistaken for Sydenham's) |
| **Other** | — | **Fatigue** (with reduced fitness), sleep disturbance (pain, depression), transverse myelitis, optic neuropathy and INO (**mimics MS**), **posterior reversible encephalopathy syndrome** (hypertension, renal disease, immunosuppressants), **catatonia** [2] |

**Attribution:** many features are non-specific. Seizures, cerebrovascular disease, neuropathy and acute confusional states are the ones most clearly **attributable to SLE** (Hanly 2007).

**Antiphospholipid syndrome (Table 8.3, 1000 patients):**
- migraine 202, stroke 198, TIA 111, epilepsy 70;
- multi-infarct dementia 25, **chorea** 13, acute encephalopathy, transient amnesia, venous thrombosis;
- **SLE plus APS** carries the **highest neuropsychiatric risk** (epilepsy 13% vs 6% in primary APS).

### Investigations [1]
- **Serology:**
  - **ANA**: the most sensitive screen (over 95%);
  - **anti-dsDNA**: specific;
  - anti-Ro, anti-histone, antiphospholipid and anticardiolipin antibodies, lupus anticoagulant;
  - complement.
- **Bloods:**
  - **raised ESR with normal CRP** (characteristic);
  - haemolytic anaemia, lymphopenia, thrombocytopenia;
  - **renal function, electrolytes**; infection screen.
- **MRI:**
  - normal in up to 50%; abnormal in 25–50% without and **75% with NPSLE**;
  - commonest finding: **punctate subcortical or periventricular white-matter lesions** (small-vessel vasculopathy); also atrophy, infarcts, venous thrombosis;
  - T2 lesions may resolve with steroids (oedema).
- **Advanced imaging:**
  - **SPECT** abnormal in **86–100%** of major NPSLE (patchy MCA hypoperfusion), even with normal MRI;
  - MRS (reduced NAA), MTI, DWI.
- **EEG** (organic vs functional; seizures); **CSF** (oligoclonal bands; exclude infection).
- **Neuropsychological testing.**

### Differential diagnosis of a mental disturbance in SLE [1]
Active SLE accounted for **under a quarter** of 36 episodes of cerebral disturbance (Wong). Consider:
1. **Infection** (immunosuppressed).
2. **Steroid-induced** psychiatric disturbance. Only occasionally responsible: similar episodes predate steroids, and re-challenge often does not reproduce them.
3. **Hypertensive encephalopathy / PRES.**
4. **Uraemia, electrolyte disturbance.**
5. **Primary psychiatric disorder** or reactive depression and anxiety (rates similar to other rheumatology patients).
6. **Stroke** (antiphospholipid).

**Watch for subtle clouding or mild intellectual impairment** in apparently "functional" presentations.

### Management [1, 2]
**General (specialist-led):**
- avoid **sun exposure**; treat infections promptly;
- **avoid oestrogen-containing preparations** (flares, thrombosis);
- **prednisolone** (mainstay; also for some neuropsychiatric flares);
- **cyclophosphamide** (effective, toxic); **mycophenolate** (better tolerated; likely to replace it for renal SLE);
- **rituximab** for refractory disease.

**Neuropsychiatric:**
1. **Identify the cause:** active lupus vs infection, steroids, hypertension, metabolic disturbance; use EEG and MRI (helpful, not definitive).
2. **Delirium or confusion in a relapse:** **steroids help**. But **some psychoses improve on reducing the steroid dose**: judge each case.
3. **Thrombotic (antiphospholipid) events:** stroke management and anticoagulation. *(Anticoagulation specifics are not in the source.)*
4. **Seizures:** anticonvulsants (some anticonvulsants may induce autoantibodies).
5. **Psychosis:** antipsychotics as required. **Catatonia:** benzodiazepines or ECT and **treat the underlying lupus** [2].
6. **Depression and anxiety:** standard treatment; they often fluctuate with disease activity.
7. **Fatigue:** **exercise programme** (30–50 minutes, 3 or more times a week for 12 weeks) reduced fatigue (Tench RCT).
8. **Psychosocial:** home, finances, family; **good doctor–patient communication** improves quality of life.
9. Most neuropsychiatric episodes are **transient and self-limiting**.

**Conclusion.** Neuropsychiatric SLE affects up to 60% of patients and may precede systemic disease. Common features are:
- cognitive dysfunction (about 38%; subcortical, usually mild, non-progressive);
- headache; mood (about 26%) and anxiety disorders;
- seizures, cerebrovascular events (antiphospholipid), neuropathy;
- less commonly psychosis (2–5%), delirium (now about 3%), chorea, catatonia.

Mechanisms are small-vessel vasculopathy, antiphospholipid and brain-reactive (anti-NMDAR) antibodies, plus secondary factors. Assessment must exclude **infection, steroids, hypertension (PRES) and uraemia**: active lupus causes under a quarter of episodes. Treatment is immunosuppression (steroids, mycophenolate, cyclophosphamide, rituximab) for active NPSLE, steroid reduction when steroids are the cause, symptomatic psychotropics, exercise for fatigue, and psychosocial support.
