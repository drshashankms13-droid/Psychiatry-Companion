## Exam answer

**Introduction [1].**
- The original name, **"dementia praecox"** (premature dementia), shows how central cognitive decline was to the first conception of the illness. That suggests a **degenerative** model.
- The modern question is the **timing** of brain and cognitive abnormalities:
  - Are they present **premorbidly** (neurodevelopmental)?
  - Do they appear **at onset**?
  - Do they **accumulate with illness** (neurodegenerative)?

### Evidence for a NEURODEVELOPMENTAL disorder [1]
1. **Premorbid deficits:** "one of the few consistent findings". Children who later develop schizophrenia show **delayed milestones and social development**, and poorer school and cognitive performance.
   - **1946 UK birth cohort:** pre-schizophrenia children scored lower at 8, 11 and 15, with the deficit widening with age.
   - **1958 cohort:** a stable deficit of about **8–9 IQ points**.
   - **Swedish conscripts** (about 50,000; 362 cases): a **linear IQ–risk gradient**, about **10-fold** from low-borderline to superior IQ. The whole group was shifted down 5–10 IQ points. **This pattern is not seen in bipolar disorder.**
   - **Israeli conscripts** (536 cases): effect sizes 0.2–0.6 (4–8 IQ points).
2. **First-episode deficits:** impairment of **1–3 SD** is present **from the first episode**, and in **drug-free** patients. There is **no marked decline up to 5 years** after the first episode.
3. **Structural changes are present early:**
   - enlarged ventricles and reduced cortical volume are **present in first-episode patients** (Table 2.1, +++);
   - the **MZ twin study** (Suddath): affected twins had smaller temporal lobes and hippocampi, implicating **non-genetic early factors such as obstetric complications**;
   - **unaffected relatives** also have larger ventricles.
4. **Neuropathology (Harrison):**
   - volume reduction reflects **reduced neuropil and neuronal size, not neuronal loss**;
   - changes in synaptic, dendritic and axonal organisation;
   - **"absence of gliosis as an intrinsic feature"** (+++). The brain is not responding to an acquired degenerative insult, so this "**supports the hypothesis that schizophrenia is a disorder of prenatal neurodevelopment**, quite unlike Alzheimer's disease";
   - **AD is not commoner** in schizophrenia (++++).
5. **Developmental anomalies are commoner:** agenesis of the corpus callosum, cavum septum pellucidum, aqueduct stenosis, arachnoid cysts, cerebellar ectopia. Taken together they are "consistent with the view that **disruption in neurodevelopment is a key aspect if not an essential part** of the disorder".
6. **Susceptibility genes** (neuregulin, dysbindin, COMT, DISC1, RGS4, GRM3, G72) may converge on **synaptic plasticity and development and stabilisation of cortical microcircuitry**.

### Evidence for a NEURODEGENERATIVE element [1]
1. **Dementia praecox:** the historical concept of progressive deterioration.
2. **Serial MRI** shows a **general loss of brain substance (grey more than white) after the first episode**, which seems to be an **acceleration of normal age-related decline**. Lishman calls this "a strand of evidence in favour of a 'neurodegenerative' element".
3. **Post-onset cognitive change:**
   - Israeli conscripts retested after one episode **dropped in reasoning, speed and concentration**, or failed to show normal practice gains (Caspi);
   - **elderly patients** perform about as badly as those with **moderate AD**;
   - a **small number** show **massive intellectual decline** despite good symptom control, which "remains a mystery".

### Confounders against a "true" degenerative process [1]
- Progressive brain loss may reflect **medication effects**, especially **first-generation antipsychotics** (Lieberman). Caudate enlargement is attributed to neuroleptics.
- **Decline can partly be explained by:** lost opportunities, physical illness, **substance misuse**, medication, poor motivation, social isolation, selection factors.
- **No gliosis** argues against a classic neurodegenerative pathology.
- Non-specific focal abnormalities (small infarcts, white matter changes) in up to **50%** of brains are probably **coincidental** (Harrison).

### Synthesis
- **The weight of evidence favours a neurodevelopmental origin:**
  - premorbid cognitive and motor deficits;
  - first-episode structural change;
  - no gliosis;
  - developmental anomalies;
  - developmental genes.
- **A superimposed progressive component** (post-onset grey-matter loss, decline in some patients) is possible. It is **confounded by medication and illness-related factors**.
- **A combined model** (neurodevelopmental vulnerability with a later progressive phase in some patients) best fits the data. The evidence to settle it is still emerging.

**Conclusion.** Schizophrenia is best seen as **primarily neurodevelopmental**, with lifelong, non-specific cognitive compromise and early brain changes, and the **absence of gliosis**. Some evidence of post-onset progression (possibly partly medication-related) means a neurodegenerative element cannot be excluded.

## In depth

### Certainty in schizophrenia neuropathology (Table 2.1, Harrison 1999) [1]
| Finding | Strength |
|---|---|
| Enlarged lateral and third ventricles | ++++ (meta-analysis) |
| Decreased cortical volume | ++++ |
| Present in first-episode patients | +++ |
| Disproportionate temporal and hippocampal volume loss | +++ |
| Grey more than white matter loss | ++ |
| Basal ganglia enlargement secondary to antipsychotics | +++ |
| **Absence of gliosis** | +++ |
| Smaller cortical and hippocampal neurons | +++ |
| Fewer dorsal thalamic neurons | +++ |
| AD not commoner in schizophrenia | ++++ |

### Imaging numbers [1]
- **Ventricles:** median increase of about 40% (Lawrie and Abukmeil); 26% (Wright: 58 studies, 1588 patients).
- **Whole brain:** about 2–3% loss.
- **Medial temporal structures:** about 6% loss (effect size 0.4–0.69).
