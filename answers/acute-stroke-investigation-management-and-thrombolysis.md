## Exam answer

### Definitions and types [1]
- **Stroke** comprises **haemorrhage** (primary intracerebral; subarachnoid) and **infarction**.
- **Ischaemic stroke causes:**
  - **large-artery atherosclerosis about 50%**;
  - **lacunar (small vessel) about 25%**;
  - **cardioembolic about 20%**;
  - **rarer causes about 5%**.
- **Primary intracerebral haemorrhage:**
  - age 60–80; hypertension;
  - **deep** (basal ganglia, thalamus, pons, cerebellum) from **lipohyalinosis**;
  - **lobar** haemorrhage in the elderly from **cerebral amyloid angiopathy**.
- **Clinical differences** (onset during exertion, headache, vomiting, coma in haemorrhage; onset in sleep in thrombosis) are **not reliable in the individual**, so **imaging is essential**.

**Classification by location (Bamford 1991; Box 8.2):**

| Type | Features | 1-year outcome |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **TACI** | Hemiplegia + hemianopia + aphasia or neglect | **60% dead, 4% independent** |
| **PACI** | More restricted cortical deficit | **Early recurrence most likely** |
| **POCI** | Ipsilateral cranial nerve + contralateral motor/sensory, or cerebellar signs | High recurrence through the first year |
| **LACI** | Pure motor or sensory, ataxic hemiparesis; no cortical signs | **11% dead, 60% independent** |

**Classification by cause (Adams 1993; Box 8.2):** large-artery atherosclerosis, cardioembolism, small-artery occlusion (lacunar), other aetiology, undetermined.

### TIA [1]
- **Old definition:** focal deficit resolving within 24 hours.
- **Tissue-based definition (Albers 2002):** a **brief episode** of focal brain or retinal ischaemia, **typically under 1 hour**, **without acute infarction** (episodes of 4–24 hours usually show infarction on DWI).
- **Risk:** **10% stroke within 90 days**; 25% within 5 years.
- **High-risk features:**
  - long or **crescendo** TIAs (more than 3 in 3 days); TIAs despite antiplatelets;
  - cardioembolic source; age over 60; diabetes; hypertension;
  - weakness or speech disturbance.
- **Differential diagnosis (Box 8.3):**
  - migraine aura; structural lesion (subdural, tumour, aneurysm, haemorrhage, sinus thrombosis);
  - **Todd's paresis**, epilepsy; **hypoglycaemia**; faint; labyrinthine disease;
  - MS; **transient global amnesia**; infection; **panic attack**.

### Investigation [1]
**1. Urgent neuroimaging: the first priority:**
- confirm a vascular lesion; **exclude haemorrhage** (contraindicates thrombolysis);
- **CT:** quick and accessible; detects acute haemorrhage (isodense by about 10 days). Used in the **first 3 hours** to select thrombolysis candidates, but less sensitive for early infarction;
- **CT perfusion** (penumbra);
- **MRI:**
  - **DWI** shows the **core** (cytotoxic oedema; reduced ADC);
  - **PWI** shows hypoperfusion; the **DWI/PWI mismatch approximates the penumbra**;
  - gradient echo detects haemorrhage and **microbleeds** (whether microbleeds contraindicate thrombolysis is uncertain);
  - best for posterior fossa and lacunar strokes; shows occlusion or **dissection**;
- **MRA** (method of choice for **cerebral venous thrombosis**); angiography; Doppler.

**2. Ischaemic zones:**
- **core** (inevitable death);
- **penumbra** (blood flow 10–20 mL/100 g/min; **salvageable**);
- **oligaemia** (over 20 mL/100 g/min, normally about 50);
- the penumbra's size indicates the potential for recovery and for thrombolysis to help.

**3. Cause and risk factors:**
- **full blood screen**, glucose, lipids;
- **ECG**, **echocardiography** (transthoracic or transoesophageal);
- **carotid Doppler** (territory-dependent);
- 24-hour ECG.

**4. Young or recurrent stroke (Box 8.4):**
- MRI;
- **autoantibody screen, anticardiolipin antibodies**, **thrombophilia screen**, **sickle cell**;
- **homocysteine**, **serum lactate** (Box 8.4 labels this "CADASIL");
- **syphilis serology**, **drug screen**;
- 24-hour ECG, TTE/TOE, angiography.
- ⚠️ *Lishman's Box 8.4 pairs serum lactate with CADASIL, whereas its migraine section describes MELAS ("lactic acidosis") as the lactate-related disorder. Flagged for the reviewer, not corrected.*

### Management [1]
**A. Hyperacute (within about 3 hours):**
- **exclude haemorrhage** on imaging;
- **thrombolysis with recombinant tissue plasminogen activator (rt-PA)** if there is no haemorrhage: "**the sooner rt-PA is started, the greater the benefit**";
- the window is "**within about 3 hours**" of onset; a **large DWI/PWI mismatch** may justify it as late as **3–6 hours**.
- *(Dose, contraindications and later windows: not in the source.)*

**B. Ischaemic stroke and TIA:**
- **antiplatelets** (**aspirin or clopidogrel**) once haemorrhage is excluded: "the mainstay of medical management";
- **anticoagulation** only with a **cardiac embolic source** (AF, prosthetic valve, recent MI);
- **carotid endarterectomy** for **over 70% stenosis**;
- **malignant MCA infarction** (large DWI core, little mismatch): **decompressive hemicraniectomy** may reduce mortality;
- **neuroprotection: no evidence**.

**C. Intracerebral haemorrhage:**
- **excellent nursing care**, physiotherapy;
- watch for **re-bleeding and hydrocephalus**.

**D. Organised stroke-unit care:**
- **lower mortality**, and more patients alive and independent at 1 year (Stroke Unit Trialists);
- hydration, BP, **swallowing assessment**, prevention of complications (pressure sores, contractures, subluxation);
- early mobilisation and physiotherapy.

**E. Secondary prevention and rehabilitation (Box 8.6):**
- risk-factor control; early, standardised assessment;
- multidisciplinary team, family involvement, community reintegration;
- **screen for and manage neuropsychiatric sequelae** (depression, cognitive impairment, emotionalism, psychosis): see the neuropsychiatric stroke and post-stroke depression answers.

**Prognosis:** about **20% die** in the acute stage of infarction, 20% recover completely and **60% have residual disability**. Haemorrhage carries a worse prognosis.

**Conclusion.** Acute stroke requires immediate neuroimaging to separate haemorrhage (thrombolysis contraindicated) from infarction. CT is used for speed; MRI DWI/PWI defines the core and the salvageable penumbra. If there is no haemorrhage, **IV rt-PA within about 3 hours** (the sooner the better; possibly up to 6 hours with a large mismatch) is the key treatment. Then:
- antiplatelets (aspirin or clopidogrel);
- anticoagulation for cardioembolism;
- endarterectomy for over 70% carotid stenosis;
- hemicraniectomy for malignant MCA infarction;
- organised stroke-unit care.

Investigate the cause (ECG, echo, carotid Doppler, bloods; Box 8.4 in the young) and manage TIA urgently (10% stroke in 90 days). **Current thrombolysis criteria, doses, the 4.5-hour window and thrombectomy are not covered by the source.**
