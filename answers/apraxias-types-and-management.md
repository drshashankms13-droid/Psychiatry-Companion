## Exam answer

### Definition [1]
- **Apraxia:** inability to carry out **learned voluntary movements** or movement sequences, not explained by weakness, incoordination, sensory loss or involuntary movements. The same muscles work normally in other contexts.
- **Liepmann (1905)** established it as an entity distinct from aphasia and paralysis, arising at several stages of organising an action.
- **Often overlooked** (Geschwind & Damasio): patients rarely complain, and perform normally in natural settings with real objects. It is probably commoner than appreciated.
- **Characteristic dissociations:**
  - fails to command but succeeds on imitation, or a moment later when attention is elsewhere;
  - better with the **real tool** than in pantomime;
  - **whole-body** commands are often preserved while limb and facial commands fail.
- **Associations:** aphasia accompanies most cases; poor comprehension and agnosia can contribute.
- **Diffuse vs focal lesions:** more often seen with diffuse than focal lesions. Severe apraxia from a focal lesion can be **misdiagnosed as dementia**.

### Theories [1]
- **Brain (1965):** movements are organised by **schemata**, from the idea of the movement (highest level) down to selection of muscles (lowest).
- **Geschwind (1965), disconnection:**
  - a lesion between auditory and motor association cortex of the dominant hemisphere causes bilateral apraxia to command;
  - a left motor cortex lesion causes right hemiplegia plus **left-arm apraxia** (transcallosal pathway destroyed);
  - a **callosal lesion** causes left-sided apraxia to command **without aphasia**.
- **Milner & Goodale:** a **ventral "what"** stream (temporal; perception) and a **dorsal "where/how"** stream (parietal; action).
- **Mirror system:** observing or imagining an action activates the motor programmes used to perform it.
- **Chainay & Humphreys:** a **convergent-route model**, with semantic and direct perceptual routes to action and an "action output lexicon".

### Types [1]

| Type | Features | Lesion |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Limb-kinetic** | Loss of skill and delicacy in simple and complex movements (buttons, safety pin); may affect particular muscles or fingers; between paresis and apraxia, so often excluded from apraxia proper | Small lesion of the **contralateral premotor cortex** |
| **Ideomotor** | Knows the idea of the movement but cannot **execute** it to command (wave, salute); copying may also fail; the **same movement occurs automatically** (waving goodbye). A low-level executory defect | Usually **bilateral**, mostly arms. **Unilateral** almost always the **left arm**, with right hemiplegia and aphasia. Meaningful gestures to command: **left parietal** |
| **Ideational** | Cannot perform **sequences** (take a match and strike it) or use tools (comb, scissors); better holding the tool; variable. **Conception and planning** are disturbed ("programming" apraxia) | Always **bilateral**. If focal, **dominant parietal or temporal**. Commonest with **diffuse disease**, severe aphasia or generalised cognitive impairment |
| **Dressing** | Cannot relate garments to the body (back to front, wrong sleeve, buttons left undone). A useful clinical sign rather than a distinct apraxia; right–left disorientation, neglect and body-image disturbance contribute | Common in **dementia**; bilateral or **right** parieto-occipital > left |
| **Constructional** | Spatial arrangement of actions is altered without apraxia for individual movements (copying, drawing, building); has a perceptual component; often **satisfied with a poor copy**. Many regard it as visuospatial agnosia | **Left lesions:** oversimplified drawings. **Right lesions:** spatial distortion and **hemispatial neglect** |
| **Oral (buccofacial)** | Cannot protrude the tongue, open the mouth or cough on command, yet does so spontaneously (eating) | Left central operculum and insula |
| **Gait** | Difficulty walking | **Bilateral frontal** |
| **Speech** (pure word-dumbness) | Inability to articulate despite intact language and writing: "apraxia restricted to speech" | Beneath the insula |

**Empirical framework (McCarthy & Warrington 1990)** [1]:
1. Simple repetitive movements: unimanual (either hemisphere); bimanual coordination (frontal or supplementary motor area).
2. Unfamiliar actions and sequences: single hand positions (left parietal); sequences (left frontal or left parietal).
3. Familiar gestures to command (salute, wave): left parietal.
4. Object use and mime: left hemisphere, especially the **parieto-temporal junction**.
5. Body-part-specific (gait, oral): see the table.
6. Constructional: left vs right patterns, as in the table.

**Apraxia in neurological disease [1]:**
- **Corticobasal degeneration:** asymmetric **limb apraxia** and alien limb (see the tauopathies answer).
- **Callosal lesions:** left-sided apraxia.
- **Dementia:** dressing and ideational apraxia.

### Assessment [1]
1. **Exclude** weakness, incoordination, sensory loss, poor comprehension (many patients are aphasic) and unwillingness.
2. **Prove apraxia:** a movement fails under one condition (command) but succeeds under another (spontaneously or by imitation).
3. **Record exactly** what the patient does, how, and what fails (Critchley).
4. **Ideomotor:** to command, hold out the arms, show the teeth, nod; each hand separately for a fist, thumb–little-finger opposition, pronation/supination. Then imitate the examiner's postures and copy a posture from one limb to the other.
5. **Whole-body:** rise from a chair and turn round.
6. **Gestures and pantomime:** knock on a door, wave goodbye, show how to brush teeth, use a hammer, kick a ball.
7. **Ideational:** strike a match, wind a watch, cut with scissors, fold a letter into an envelope.
8. **Dressing:** watch the patient dress and undress.
9. **Constructional:** connect dots, bisect a line, draw simple figures, copy graded line drawings, draw a house, bicycle and **clock** (CLOX), stick or match patterns, jigsaw. Watch for **neglect** of one half of the page.
10. **Soft signs:** motor sequencing (fist–edge–palm).

### Management [1]
Lishman's general rehabilitation principles apply:
1. **Treat the underlying cause**; full psychometric assessment of deficits and **preserved functions to build on**.
2. **Occupational therapy:** fine motor control of the upper limbs; **retraining of ADLs** (dressing, feeding, washing, cooking); **simple mechanical aids**. Community OT improves ADLs.
3. **Use the natural context:** performance is better with real objects in their usual setting (a source observation, not a tested intervention).
4. **Compensatory over restorative:** research suggests rehabilitation aimed at disability and handicap works better than drills aimed at reducing impairment (Wilson 2002). Functional, task-specific training is generally more effective than theory-driven drills (attention studies).
5. **Principles:** secure confidence and cooperation; keep an **optimistic** approach; set **graded, realisable goals**; demonstrate practically when verbal instruction fails; go slowly; allow for day-to-day variability.
6. **Address associated problems:** aphasia, neglect, depression, motivation.
7. **Family:** explain the disability (it is easily mistaken for dementia or unwillingness); social worker; discharge planning.

**Conclusion.** Apraxia is a disorder of learned skilled movement, not of power or comprehension.
- **Ideomotor** (execution; left parietal) and **ideational** (conception and sequencing; dominant parieto-temporal or diffuse) are the core types.
- Limb-kinetic, dressing, constructional, oral and gait apraxia complete the list.
- Testing contrasts command, imitation and real object use.

Lishman offers no specific treatment; management rests on cause, occupational therapy, ADL retraining with aids, compensatory goal-based rehabilitation and family support.
