## Exam answer

**Summary.**
- A **28-year-old unmarried male engineer** in a corporate firm is brought by his parents **at his office's instruction** for psychiatric evaluation.
- For **6 months**: he reports **harassment at work**, he is very disturbed, **sleeps poorly**, has become **withdrawn** and **irritable**.
- He believes **team members are jealous** and are **hacking his phone and email**. He has complained to HR.
- **Regular cannabis use**, which he does not see as a problem.
- **No insight:** he wants a **fitness certificate**.

### Provisional diagnosis and differentials
1. **Psychotic disorder with persecutory delusions in a regular cannabis user.** The differential:
   - **Cannabis-induced psychotic disorder.** Cannabis causes "elation, **psychosis**, perceptual distortions, disturbance of memory/judgement" [3]. Acute paranoia and anxiety are recognised negative effects [2].
   - **Schizophrenia (or schizophreniform disorder) precipitated by cannabis.** Cannabis is a **component cause**: "neither a necessary nor a sufficient cause" [1]. It carries about a **twofold** increase in risk of schizophrenia [3], is **dose-dependent**, and **brings forward onset** (about 2.7 years earlier) [1].
   - **Delusional disorder (persecutory)**, if delusions are non-bizarre and functioning is otherwise preserved.
2. **Rule out real workplace harassment.** The claims must be verified with collateral (HR, family) before they are called delusional (application).
3. **Rule out other substances and organic causes** [2, 3].
4. **Cannabis use disorder:** tolerance and a withdrawal syndrome (irritability, aggression, reduced appetite, sleep disturbance) are well recognised [2]. His irritability and insomnia may partly be withdrawal.

### Assessment
1. **History, separately from patient and parents** [1, 2]:
   - onset and course of the beliefs; their relation to cannabis (onset, quantity, **potency**, daily use);
   - hallucinations; mood symptoms; sleep;
   - **age at first cannabis use**: earlier use, especially adolescent use, carries higher risk [1];
   - other substances;
   - **family history of psychosis**. Relatives of people with cannabis-associated psychosis have a **10-fold** higher risk of schizophrenia (McGuire) [1];
   - **childhood trauma, urban upbringing** (these interact with cannabis) [1];
   - premorbid personality and functioning;
   - **violence or threats** toward colleagues;
   - legal issues.
2. **Collateral from the employer** with his consent: performance, incidents, and whether the harassment is real [4].
3. **Mental state:** delusions (content, conviction, systematisation, acting on them), hallucinations, **insight**, risk to self or others.
4. **Capacity (MHCA s4):** can he **understand** information about his condition and treatment, **appreciate** consequences, and **communicate** a decision? [4]
5. **Investigations:** urine drug screen. Cannabis is detectable for about **3 days after a single use** and up to **30 days with chronic heavy use** [3]. Plus routine bloods.

### Management
**1. Engagement:** he does not see a problem and wants a certificate. Build trust and avoid confrontation over the beliefs (application).

**2. Treat the psychosis [3]:**
- No single first-line antipsychotic suits everyone. In first-episode studies, **olanzapine and amisulpride** show small efficacy advantages and **haloperidol** performs poorly.
- Start at the **minimum effective first-episode dose**.
- Watch for metabolic and EPS adverse effects.
- **Duration:** antipsychotics are usually recommended for **1–2 years after a first episode of schizophrenia**. Relapse is far higher on discontinuation (26% vs 61% at 6–12 months). **Cannabis use is the one factor linked to poor outcome after discontinuation** [3].

**3. Treat the cannabis use [1–3]:**
- **Psychoeducation:**
  - cannabis increases psychosis risk;
  - **high-THC, low-CBD** strains ("skunk", sinsemilla) carry the highest risk;
  - continued use worsens symptoms, relapse and social impairment [1, 2].
- **Psychosocial treatment:** **CBT, motivational enhancement therapy and abstinence-based incentives**. Maudsley lists these, and states that **no pharmacological treatments are currently available**, in its cannabis entry (pregnancy table) [3].
- **Integrated dual-diagnosis care:** dual diagnosis is "the norm rather than the exception". Unmotivated patients may not be followed up by separate addiction services [3].
- Manage **withdrawal irritability and insomnia** [2].

**4. Psychosocial and occupational care:**
- Family psychoeducation.
- Address the work situation (leave, phased return) once he has improved (application).

**5. The fitness certificate [4]:**
- **Do not issue a certificate of fitness while he is actively psychotic** and assessment is incomplete (application).
- **Confidentiality (s23):** he has a right to confidentiality of his mental health information. Release to the employer requires his consent, except to protect others from **harm or violence**, to prevent threat to life, in the interest of **public safety**, or on the order of a competent authority. Then release **only what is necessary**.
- **Communicate** the outcome to him honestly and plan a review for fitness once he is in remission.

**6. Risk and legal:** assess the risk of **violence toward colleagues**, since he believes they are persecuting him. If he has high support needs and is at risk, consider **MHCA admission** [4].

**Conclusion.** This is most likely a **cannabis-associated psychosis with persecutory delusions**, either cannabis-induced or an emerging schizophrenia-spectrum disorder in which cannabis is a component cause. Steps:
1. Verify the claims and assess risk and capacity.
2. Treat with an antipsychotic plus **integrated CBT- and motivation-based cannabis treatment**.
3. Educate patient and family about the cannabis–psychosis link.
4. Withhold a fitness certificate until he has recovered, while respecting MHCA confidentiality.

## In depth

### Epidemiological evidence linking cannabis and psychosis [1]
| Study | Sample | Follow-up | Odds ratio |
|---|---|---|---|
| Andreasson 1987 | 45,570 Swedish conscripts | 15 years | 6.0 (heavy use) |
| Zammit 2002 | 50,087 Swedish conscripts | 27 years | 3.1 |
| Arseneault 2002 | Dunedin birth cohort | 15 years | 3.1 |
| van Os 2002 | NEMESIS (Netherlands) | 3 years | 2.8 |
| Fergusson 2003 | Christchurch cohort | 3 years | 1.8 |
| Henquet 2005 | EDSP (Germany) | 4 years | 1.7 |
| Manrique-Garcia 2012 | Swedish conscripts | 35 years | 3.7 |
- **Moore 2007 systematic review:** OR **1.4** for any psychotic outcome, adjusted for about 60 confounders, with a dose–response effect.
- About **13–14%** of psychosis cases might be averted if cannabis use were prevented.

### Mechanisms [1]
- **Dopamine:** disrupted retrograde endocannabinoid signalling causes excess **mesolimbic dopamine**. However, chronic users may show **reduced** dopamine synthesis capacity (conflicting).
- **Aberrant salience:** THC increases misattribution of salience.
- **Neurodevelopment:** adolescent exposure disturbs endocannabinoid-guided maturation.
- **Gene × environment interactions:**
  - **COMT Val/Val**: RR 10.9 with adolescent use (not replicated);
  - **AKT1** rs2494732;
  - **DAT1**;
  - interactions with childhood abuse and urban upbringing.
- **CBD** opposes THC and may be antipsychotic.

### Counterpoints (Lishman) [2]
- The magnitude of risk is hard to establish. Pre-existing illness or predisposition may explain chronic psychoses.
- In Halikas's study, almost all psychiatric illness among users **began before** first cannabis exposure.
- **No strong structural brain abnormalities** with cannabis (review of more than 100 studies).
