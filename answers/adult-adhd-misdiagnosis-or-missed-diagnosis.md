## Exam answer

**Introduction and definition.** **Adult ADHD** is a persistent pattern of **inattention and/or hyperactivity-impulsivity** that impairs functioning in adulthood. DSM-5/DSM-5-TR criteria:
- **at least 5 symptoms** (rather than 6) in either domain for people aged 17 and over;
- **several symptoms present before age 12**;
- symptoms in **two or more settings**;
- not better explained by another disorder.

ICD-11 (6A05) similarly classifies ADHD as a neurodevelopmental disorder that may persist into adulthood. **Prevalence is about 2.5–4% of adults**, and the sex ratio approaches 2:1, compared with 3–4:1 in children [1, 2].

The question asks whether adult ADHD is **under-recognised** (a "missed diagnosis") or **over-diagnosed** (a "misdiagnosis"). **Both are true in different populations and settings.** The answer depends on careful assessment.

### Mechanism and phenotype
- **The neurodevelopmental model:**
  - Heritability about **70–80%**; polygenic risk.
  - Fronto-striatal and fronto-parietal network dysfunction; dopamine and noradrenaline signalling.
  - Executive and reward deficits.
- **The adult phenotype:**
  - Hyperactivity becomes **inner restlessness**.
  - Disorganisation, procrastination, poor time management.
  - Emotional dysregulation.
  - Occupational and relationship difficulties [1].
- **Comorbidity is high:** MDD about 30%, anxiety about 50%, SUD 40–50% [1].

### Arguments that adult ADHD is a MISSED diagnosis
| Argument | Detail |
| --- | --- |
| **Persistence from childhood** | About two-thirds of childhood cases have impairing residual symptoms in adulthood; about 15% meet full criteria at 25 (Faraone 2006 meta-analysis) |
| **Masking** | Hyperactivity fades; adults develop compensatory strategies; symptoms are mistaken for personality, laziness or anxiety |
| **Women and girls** | Inattentive presentation, social camouflaging, referral bias towards hyperactive boys, so many women are diagnosed late or misdiagnosed as anxious or depressed [1] |
| **Comorbidity overshadowing** | Depression, anxiety, SUD and bipolar disorder are diagnosed and treated while the ADHD is missed [1] |
| **Real impairment** | Accidents (2–4× more motor vehicle accidents), job loss, divorce, criminality, early mortality. Medication reduces accidents and criminality (Swedish registry studies, e.g., Chang 2014; Lichtenstein 2012) [1] |
| **Low recognition in LMICs** | India: little adult ADHD awareness; few clinicians assess it; stimulant access is restricted |
| **Effective treatment exists** | Stimulants (large effect sizes, SMD about 0.5–0.9), atomoxetine, CBT for adult ADHD |

### Arguments that adult ADHD is a MISDIAGNOSIS
| Argument | Detail |
| --- | --- |
| **The Dunedin cohort (Moffitt 2015)** | **90% of adult ADHD cases had no childhood ADHD.** Only 3 of 61 childhood cases (5%) still met criteria at 38. Adult cases lacked childhood **neurocognitive deficits** and **polygenic risk** for childhood ADHD [2] |
| **Replications** | E-Risk (Agnew-Blais 2016) and the Brazilian Pelotas cohort (Caye 2016) also found "late-onset" adult ADHD largely distinct from childhood ADHD |
| **Symptom non-specificity** | Inattention, restlessness and impulsivity occur in **depression, anxiety, bipolar disorder, borderline PD, substance use, sleep deprivation, sleep apnoea, thyroid disease, PTSD** and normal variation |
| **Reliance on self-report** | Retrospective recall of childhood is unreliable. Informant confirmation is often missing. **Feigning** happens to obtain stimulants or exam accommodations; validity tests detect symptom exaggeration in 10–40% of student evaluations |
| **Lowered thresholds** | DSM-5 dropped the onset age from 7 to 12 and reduced the adult threshold to 5 symptoms, which raises prevalence |
| **Commercialisation** | Marketing ("ADHD: own it"), online and telehealth diagnosis, social-media self-diagnosis, stimulant shortages [2] |
| **Risks of overtreatment** | Stimulant misuse and diversion; cardiovascular effects; psychosis or mania in vulnerable people |

### Assessment: getting it right
1. **Structured interview:** DIVA-5, ACDS, or CAADID.
2. **Rating scales:** ASRS v1.1 (6-item screen), CAARS (self and observer), Wender Utah Rating Scale (retrospective childhood).
3. **Establish childhood onset:** **collateral history** (parents), **school reports**.
4. **Pervasive impairment** across settings: work, home, relationships.
5. **Exclude or address** depression, anxiety, bipolar disorder, substance use, sleep disorders, PTSD, personality disorders, thyroid disease, medication effects.
6. **Assess effort and validity** where there is secondary gain.
7. **Neuropsychological testing:** not diagnostic, but can help profile deficits.

### Management (when diagnosed)
- **Psychoeducation; CBT** (organisation, planning); coaching; workplace accommodations.
- **Medication:**
  - Methylphenidate (start 5–10 mg two to three times daily; long-acting up to about 54–72 mg) or lisdexamfetamine/amphetamines (where available).
  - **Atomoxetine** 40→80–100 mg, preferred with SUD or anxiety.
  - Guanfacine, bupropion as alternatives.
- **Before stimulants:** check BP, heart rate and cardiac history; screen for SUD and psychosis.
- **Treat comorbidities** in sequence: stabilise severe depression, mania and SUD first.

**Conclusion.** Adult ADHD is **both missed and misdiagnosed**. A substantial group of adults with genuine, childhood-onset ADHD (especially women, people with comorbid disorders, and people in low-resource settings) go **unrecognised** and miss effective treatment. At the same time, prospective cohorts (Dunedin) show that many adult presentations do not come from childhood neurodevelopmental ADHD, and lax self-report-based diagnosis risks **over-diagnosis** and stimulant misuse. The answer is **rigorous assessment**: documented childhood onset, collateral information, cross-setting impairment, and exclusion of mimics, so diagnosis is neither denied nor handed out freely.

## In depth

### Background
- **1902:** George Still described "defects of moral control" in children.
- **1937:** Bradley noted the effect of benzedrine.
- **DSM-II (1968):** hyperkinetic reaction of childhood.
- **DSM-III (1980):** ADD with or without hyperactivity.
- **DSM-III-R (1987):** ADHD, considered childhood-only [2].
- **1970s:** Wender's Utah criteria described adult "minimal brain dysfunction".
- **DSM-IV (1994):** permitted adult diagnosis.
- **DSM-5 (2013):** formal adult criteria (5 symptoms; onset before 12) [2].
- **ICD-11 (2022):** 6A05 ADHD, lifespan.

### Epidemiology
- **Children:** worldwide prevalence about 5–7% (Polanczyk 2014).
- **Adults:** about 2.5% (Simon 2009 meta-analysis), and about 2.8% in the WHO World Mental Health surveys (Fayyad 2017). Symptomatic (subthreshold) adult ADHD is about 6.8%.
- **Dunedin:** 6% in childhood (79% male); 3% at 38 (61% male, no significant sex difference) [2].
- **India:** child ADHD prevalence estimates vary (about 1.3–28% in different school studies). Adult data are scarce.

### Classification and criteria
| Feature | DSM-5-TR | ICD-11 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Symptom threshold | ≥6 (children); ≥5 (17+) in inattention or hyperactivity-impulsivity | No fixed count; "significant" |
| Onset | Several symptoms before 12 | During the developmental period, typically early to mid childhood |
| Settings | ≥2 | Multiple |
| Presentations | Combined, predominantly inattentive, predominantly hyperactive-impulsive | Same, plus "other specified" |
| Comorbid ASD | Allowed (since DSM-5) | Allowed |

### Mechanism in detail
- **Genetics:** heritability about 74% from twin studies. The PGC GWAS (Demontis 2019) identified 12 loci, with genetic correlation to depression, obesity and education.
- **Neuroimaging:** delayed cortical maturation (Shaw 2007, peak thickness about 3 years later); smaller subcortical volumes (ENIGMA); altered default mode–task network interaction.
- **Neuropsychology:** executive dysfunction, delay aversion, reaction-time variability. These are heterogeneous, and not every patient has them.
- **Late-onset adult ADHD (the Dunedin puzzle)** [2]. Possible explanations:
  - a different aetiology (e.g., substance use, depression, anxiety);
  - childhood symptoms masked by high IQ or support;
  - a different disorder that resembles ADHD;
  - measurement artefact.

### Clinical features in detail: the Dunedin adult ADHD group [2]
- **They had:** elevated adult inattentive and hyperactive symptoms, confirmed by informants; impairment (accidents, speeding, underachievement); **substance dependence (48%)**, tobacco dependence and treatment contact.
- **They did not have:** childhood ADHD (90%); childhood neurocognitive deficits; childhood ADHD polygenic risk.
- **The childhood ADHD group:**
  - Mostly remitted by 38, but **continued to have life difficulties**.
  - Showed expected comorbidity (conduct disorder 59%, anxiety 54%, depression 28%), polygenic risk and early neurocognitive deficits [2].

### Assessment in detail
| Tool | Purpose |
| --- | --- |
| ASRS v1.1 | WHO 18-item scale; 6-item screener (Part A) |
| DIVA-5 | Semi-structured interview, child and adult symptoms with examples; free in many languages |
| CAARS | Self and observer ratings; inconsistency index |
| WURS | Retrospective childhood symptoms (25-item) |
| Collateral and school reports | Corroboration of childhood onset |
| Symptom validity tests (e.g., Infrequency index) | Detect overreporting |

### Differential diagnosis
| Condition | Distinguishing points |
| --- | --- |
| Depression | Episodic; low mood and anhedonia; concentration poor only during episodes |
| Bipolar disorder | Episodic elation, reduced need for sleep; grandiosity |
| Anxiety / GAD | Worry-driven distraction |
| Borderline PD | Identity disturbance, abandonment fears, self-harm; impulsivity in interpersonal context |
| SUD | Intoxication or withdrawal effects; onset after use |
| Sleep disorders (OSA, insomnia, DSWPD) | Sleep history; PSG |
| PTSD | Hyperarousal, intrusion, avoidance |
| ASD | Social communication deficits; restricted interests (can co-occur) |
| Hypothyroidism, medication effects | Laboratory tests; drug history |
| Normal variation / life stress | No childhood onset; context-bound |

### Management details
- **Evidence:**
  - Network meta-analysis (Cortese 2018, Lancet Psychiatry): in adults, **amphetamines and methylphenidate** were most effective in the short term. Methylphenidate is preferred for adults in many guidelines.
  - Long-term **registry studies** show reduced accidents, crime, suicidal behaviour and mortality during medication periods (within-individual analyses).
- **Safety:** monitor BP and heart rate; caution with cardiovascular disease, psychosis or bipolar disorder, and SUD (use long-acting forms or atomoxetine). Watch for diversion.
- **Non-pharmacological:** CBT (Safren), mindfulness, coaching, psychoeducation for partners.
- **India:** methylphenidate (Schedule X; NDPS-controlled) and atomoxetine are available. Lisdexamfetamine is not marketed (check current status).

### Critical appraisal
- **Moffitt's limitations** (stated by the authors) [2]:
  - Not hypothesised in advance.
  - DSM-III childhood diagnoses.
  - No assessments in the participants' 20s.
  - Small adult group (n=31).
  - Personality disorder not assessed; no neuroimaging.
  - Untreated community cohort.
- **Counter-evidence:** clinical samples (referred adults) more often show childhood onset on careful history. Some late-onset cases may be childhood ADHD compensated by high IQ or support.
- **Implications if replicated:** adult ADHD's place in the neurodevelopmental category must be reconsidered, and its aetiology researched [2].
- **Practical stance:** treat impairing symptoms, but be cautious about labelling. Document onset and exclude mimics.

### Indian context
- **Under-recognition:** adult psychiatric services rarely screen for ADHD. Stigma and the "lazy" label are common. Comorbid SUD is common in de-addiction settings (ADHD prevalence in Indian SUD samples is high).
- **Regulation:** stimulants are restricted (methylphenidate is a psychotropic substance under the NDPS Act), so diversion risk is lower but access is harder.
- **Assessment tools:** DIVA has Hindi and other Indian-language versions. ASRS is validated in Indian samples.
- **Education and work:** RPwD Act 2016 does not list ADHD as a specified disability, so there is no reservation or accommodation entitlement.

### Recent advances
- **Established:** DSM-5 adult criteria; long-term registry evidence.
- **Emerging:**
  - Objective measures (e.g., QbTest; EEG theta/beta ratio, which has limited value).
  - Digital phenotyping.
  - Telehealth diagnosis controversies (US, after 2020).
  - Viloxazine ER and centanafadine (newer non-stimulants).
- **Debate:** recognition of **"late-onset ADHD"** as a distinct entity; whether DSM should require childhood corroboration.
