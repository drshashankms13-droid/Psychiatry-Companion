## Exam answer

### Why it matters [1, 3]
- **Physical illness** (cardiovascular disease, chronic pain, diabetes, Parkinson's disease) carries a high risk of depression, and comorbidity rises with age [1].
- **Higher morbidity and mortality:**
  - frailty makes **self-neglect** dangerous (dehydration, hypothermia), as is **immobility** (venous stasis);
  - **suicide is relatively more common** in older people;
  - **effective treatment reduces mortality** [1].
- **Link with dementia:**
  - depression can **masquerade as dementia** or worsen it;
  - late-life depression may be **prodromal to dementia**: most people with "pseudodementia" that reversed with treatment had established dementia **5 years later** [3].
- **Vascular link:** frontal white matter hyperintensities are associated with depression [3].

### Assessment [2, 3]
- **Screening and rating** [2] (geriatric depression often has **atypical** presentations):

| Scale | Rater | Items | Cut-off | Note |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **GDS** | Self | 30 or 15 (yes/no) | **≥7 (15-item)** | Reliable; free |
| CES-D | Self | 20 | ≥16 | No appetite, suicidality or psychomotor items |
| BASDEC | Clinician | 19 cards | ≥7 | Unaffected by hearing difficulties |
| **CSDD** | Clinician | 19 | **≥8** | **For depression with cognitive impairment** |

  - **HAM-D** is used for severity but may **underestimate atypical symptoms** [2].
- **Cognition:** distinguish depression from dementia, which can coexist. A family history of affective disorder may point to depressive pseudodementia. **Follow up**, as dementia may emerge [3].
- **Physical comorbidity and medication review**, prescribed and over the counter [1].
- **Risk:** suicide, self-neglect [1].

### Pharmacological management [1]
**Efficacy:**
- **response about 51%**, similar to younger adults;
- poorer outcome with **physical illness, baseline anxiety and executive dysfunction**;
- **biological age** may matter more than chronological age;
- **non-responders can be identified at about 4 weeks**.

**Choice of drug:**
- **No ideal antidepressant**; choose by comorbidity and co-medication.
- **SSRIs = TCAs in efficacy**, but **TCAs have more dropouts** (Cochrane).
- **NICE sequence:**
  1. an **SSRI** first (**sertraline** is commonly used first in older people);
  2. a **different SSRI** or a better-tolerated newer drug (**mirtazapine**);
  3. a different class (**venlafaxine, lofepramine**).
- Network meta-analysis: **quetiapine, duloxetine, agomelatine, imipramine, vortioxetine** had the highest efficacy (inconsistent data).
- **Treatment-resistant late-life depression:** **augmentation with aripiprazole or bupropion** beat switching to bupropion (a trial printed as "OTIMUM" in Maudsley; ⚠️ name as printed, not corrected).
- **Other options:**
  - **agomelatine**: effective, well tolerated, no hyponatraemia, but needs **LFT monitoring**;
  - **vortioxetine, duloxetine**: effective, reasonably tolerated (SSRI-like caveats);
  - **trazodone**: sedation and postural hypotension limit dose; useful in depression **with dementia**;
  - **methylphenidate**: when **rapid onset** is needed;
  - **ketamine/esketamine**: rapid effect in those over 65 without cognitive worsening;
  - **omega-3 fatty acids** and **memantine**: possibly helpful in mild to moderate depression.

**Adverse effects specific to old age (Table 6.8):**
- **TCAs: last resort**: cardiac conduction, anticholinergic effects (confusion, cognitive impairment), postural hypotension, overdose toxicity, **fractures**. **Lofepramine** is safer in overdose.
- **SSRIs:**
  - **GI bleeding** (very old; NSAIDs, steroids, warfarin: **add gastroprotection**); possibly **haemorrhagic stroke**, a risk that may persist after stopping;
  - **hyponatraemia** (highest with SSRIs);
  - **falls** (highest with SSRIs), worsened by SSRI-related **osteopenia**;
  - **QT**: citalopram and escitalopram have the greatest effect;
  - **interactions**: fluvoxamine, fluoxetine and paroxetine inhibit CYP enzymes. **Sertraline is safer; citalopram, escitalopram and vortioxetine are safest.** Paroxetine is best avoided (anticholinergic).
- **"Other" antidepressants** (venlafaxine, mirtazapine) vs SSRIs: more stroke/TIA, fracture, seizures, self-harm and all-cause mortality in an observational database study (confounded).
- **Mirtazapine, trazodone:** sedation and **hangover**. **Venlafaxine:** more toxic in overdose than SSRIs; raises BP at higher doses.
- **Highly anticholinergic drugs** worsen cognition and increase **dementia risk**.

**Continuation and deprescribing:**
- **Over 60% relapse within 2 years** if antidepressants are withdrawn after 2 years of treatment (partly related to how they are stopped).
- **Continue effective treatment**, especially after severe or recurrent depression.
- **Withdraw ineffective treatment** (adverse effects, polypharmacy).

### Special situations [1]
- **Depression in dementia:** **cognitive or physical therapies** are probably better; antidepressants are of **doubtful benefit**. The same applies to MCI.
- **Physical comorbidity and polypharmacy:** check interactions; gastroprotection; monitor sodium.

### Non-drug management [1]
- Cognitive and physical therapies (in dementia) [1].
- *Psychotherapies, ECT and social interventions are not described in the sources (see gaps).*

**Conclusion.** Late-life depression is common with physical illness, carries excess mortality and suicide risk, and overlaps with dementia; pseudodementia often heralds dementia. Assessment uses age-appropriate scales (GDS; CSDD in cognitive impairment), a medication review and cognitive follow-up. Treatment:
- **SSRIs first (sertraline)**, then mirtazapine, then venlafaxine or lofepramine;
- augmentation with aripiprazole or bupropion for resistance;
- avoid TCAs and highly anticholinergic drugs;
- monitor for hyponatraemia, bleeding, falls and QT prolongation;
- assess response at about 4 weeks;
- continue effective treatment long term (over 60% relapse on withdrawal).

In depression with dementia, non-drug therapies are preferred.
