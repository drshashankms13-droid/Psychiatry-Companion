## Exam answer

**Note on the question.** "Sleep architecture disorders" is not a diagnostic category in ICSD, ICD or DSM. Answer it as **normal sleep architecture**, then **how it is altered** by psychiatric disorders, sleep disorders, ageing and drugs. Say this in the opening line.

**Definition.** **Sleep architecture** is the structural organisation of sleep: the sequence, proportion, timing and cyclic pattern of the sleep stages (N1, N2, N3 and REM) across the night. It is shown on a **hypnogram** derived from polysomnography (PSG) [1].

### Normal architecture (young adult)

| Parameter | Normal value |
| --- | --- |
| Sleep-onset latency | 10–20 min |
| NREM–REM cycles | 4–6 cycles of about 90 (70–120) min |
| **N1** | 2–5% (transition: theta, vertex waves, slow eye movements) [1] |
| **N2** | 45–55% (sleep spindles 12–14 Hz, K-complexes) [1] |
| **N3 (slow-wave sleep)** | 15–25% (≥20% of epoch is delta ≤2 Hz, ≥75 μV); **concentrated in the first third of the night** [1] |
| **REM** | 20–25%; first REM about **90 min** after sleep onset; REM periods lengthen towards morning. Sawtooth waves, rapid eye movements, **muscle atonia** [1] |
| Wake after sleep onset | <5% |
| Sleep efficiency | ≥85% |

### Regulation (mechanism)
- **Two-process model:** homeostatic process S (adenosine) and circadian process C (SCN–melatonin) [1].
- **NREM:** the **VLPO** (GABA/galanin) inhibits the arousal system (flip-flop switch stabilised by **orexin**) [1].
- **REM:** a REM-on/REM-off flip-flop between the sublaterodorsal nucleus and vlPAG/LPT.
  - **Cholinergic** PPT/LDT neurons promote REM.
  - **Noradrenergic** LC and **serotonergic** DR neurons inhibit REM.
  - Atonia arises through medullary inhibitory interneurons [1].
- **This is why drugs change architecture:** anything that raises 5-HT or NA suppresses REM; anticholinergic drugs suppress REM; GABA-ergic drugs reduce N3 and increase spindles [1].

### Changes across the lifespan
- **Newborns:** about 50% of sleep is active (REM) sleep; sleep onset is through REM.
- **Childhood and adolescence:** abundant N3, falling through adolescence.
- **Ageing:** ↓ N3 (from about 20% to under 10% by age 60–70), ↓ sleep efficiency, ↑ awakenings and N1, earlier circadian phase; REM is relatively preserved [3].

### Architecture in psychiatric disorders

| Disorder | Characteristic PSG changes |
| --- | --- |
| **Depression** | **Shortened REM latency** (often <60 min), **↑ REM density** (especially first REM period), ↑ first-cycle REM duration, **↓ N3** (slow-wave sleep shifted from the first to the second cycle), ↓ sleep efficiency, **early morning awakening** [2] |
| **Mania** | ↓ total sleep time; short REM latency; reduced need for sleep |
| **Schizophrenia** | ↑ sleep latency; ↓ sleep efficiency; **↓ slow-wave sleep**; **↓ sleep-spindle density** (linked to cognitive deficits); variable REM latency [1] |
| **Generalised anxiety / panic** | ↑ sleep latency, ↑ awakenings; REM largely normal; panic attacks arise from N2–N3 transitions |
| **PTSD** | Fragmented REM, ↑ REM density, nightmares, ↑ arousals; NREM parasomnias [1] |
| **Alcohol** | Acute: ↓ latency, ↑ N3, ↓ REM in the first half, then REM rebound and fragmentation. **Withdrawal:** marked REM rebound, insomnia. Chronic: ↓ N3, persisting for months into abstinence [1, 2] |
| **Dementia (AD)** | ↓ N3 and REM, fragmented sleep, circadian disorganisation ("sundowning") [1, 2] |
| **Narcolepsy** | **Sleep-onset REM periods (SOREMPs)**, fragmented night sleep [1] |
| **Insomnia** | ↑ latency and WASO, ↓ efficiency; **paradoxical insomnia** shows near-normal architecture [1] |

### Drug effects on architecture

| Drug | Effect |
| --- | --- |
| SSRIs / SNRIs, TCAs, MAOIs | **REM suppression**, ↑ REM latency; REM rebound on withdrawal; may cause or worsen RBD and periodic limb movements [1] |
| Benzodiazepines, Z-drugs | ↓ sleep latency, **↓ N3**, ↑ N2 and spindles; mild REM suppression [1] |
| Trazodone, mirtazapine (5-HT2/H1 blockade) | ↑ N3, ↑ continuity |
| Antipsychotics (sedating) | ↑ total sleep and N3 (5-HT2A / H1 blockade) [1] |
| Lithium | ↑ N3, ↓ REM (check source) |
| Stimulants, caffeine | ↑ latency, ↓ total sleep time, ↓ REM and N3 [1] |
| Opioids | ↓ REM and N3, central apnoea [1] |
| Sodium oxybate | ↑ N3 |

### Clinical use of architecture
- **Diagnosis:** narcolepsy (SOREMPs), RBD (REM without atonia), parasomnias (arousal from N3), OSA (events cluster in REM and supine position) [1].
- **Biomarker research:** short REM latency as a trait marker of depression risk; the antidepressant effect of **sleep deprivation**; spindles as a cognitive marker in schizophrenia [1].

**Conclusion.** Normal sleep architecture is a predictable sequence of four to six NREM–REM cycles: N3 in the early night and REM in the later night. Psychiatric illnesses and psychotropic drugs alter it in characteristic ways. The best-replicated pattern is the **depression profile** (short REM latency, increased REM density, reduced slow-wave sleep). These changes are not diagnostic on their own, but they explain symptoms, guide drug choice and are central to diagnosing narcolepsy and RBD.

## In depth

### Background
- **1953:** Aserinsky and Kleitman discovered REM sleep.
- **1957:** Dement and Kleitman described the cyclic organisation of sleep.
- **1968:** Rechtschaffen and Kales staging manual (stages 1–4 and REM).
- **2007:** the **AASM** merged stages 3 and 4 into **N3** [1].
- **1970s–80s:** **Kupfer** described shortened REM latency in depression; Gillin and Wehr studied the antidepressant effect of sleep deprivation; Borbély proposed the two-process model (1982) [1].
- **Benca et al. 1992** meta-analysis (177 studies) of sleep across psychiatric disorders.

### Epidemiology
- About 90% of people with depression have sleep complaints: insomnia in about 70%, hypersomnia in about 15% [1].
- Sleep disturbance is nearly universal in psychiatric disorders, and psychiatric symptoms are common in sleep disorders (Benca 1992; Breslau 1996) [1].

### Classification and criteria
- **Scoring:** the **AASM Manual for the Scoring of Sleep and Associated Events** defines stages by 30-second epochs on EEG (frontal, central and occipital), EOG and chin EMG [1, 4].
- **No nosological category** "sleep architecture disorder" exists in ICSD-3-TR, ICD-11 or DSM-5-TR. Architecture abnormalities are findings, not diagnoses [1].

### Mechanism in detail
- **Physiology by stage** [1]:
  - **NREM:** ↑ parasympathetic tone; ↓ heart rate, BP and ventilation.
  - **REM:** autonomic instability with sympathetic surges; ↑ cerebral blood flow; airway muscle tone markedly reduced (OSA worsens); thermoregulation suspended. This contributes to early-morning MI, arrhythmias and stroke.
- **Hormones** [1]:
  - **Growth hormone** peaks in early-night N3.
  - **Prolactin** rises 30–90 min after sleep onset.
  - **Cortisol** is lowest around sleep onset and peaks on waking.
  - **Melatonin** peaks at 02:00–04:00.
  - **TSH** peaks in the evening.
  - **Testosterone** peaks around 08:00.
- **Depression models:**
  - **Cholinergic–aminergic imbalance** (Janowsky): cholinergic hypersensitivity shortens REM latency; the cholinergic agonist RS-86 induces REM earlier in depressed people.
  - **Process S deficiency** (Borbély): low slow-wave activity and reduced sleep pressure; sleep deprivation raises S and lifts mood transiently.
  - **Circadian phase advance** (Wehr): REM and temperature rhythms advanced relative to sleep.
  - **HPA hyperactivity:** CRH suppresses N3 and promotes awakenings.
- **Schizophrenia:** thalamic reticular nucleus dysfunction reduces spindles, which correlates with impaired memory consolidation [1].

### Clinical features in detail: the hypnogram
- **Normal:** the first cycle has the most N3 and a short REM. Later cycles have little N3 and long REM. Brief awakenings occur near REM ends.
- **Depressed:** REM appears within about 40–60 min; dense eye movements; N3 reduced in the first cycle; awakenings in the early morning.
- **Narcolepsy:** REM at sleep onset; frequent stage shifts.
- **RBD:** REM sleep without atonia, with phasic EMG bursts and dream enactment [1].
- **Sleepwalking:** arousal out of N3 in the first third of the night [1].

### Assessment in detail
- **PSG channels** [1]:
  - EEG: frontal for delta, central for spindles and K-complexes, occipital for alpha.
  - Bilateral EOG; chin and limb EMG.
  - Airflow (nasal pressure, thermistor); thoracoabdominal effort; SpO₂ (± end-tidal CO₂).
  - ECG; body position; video and audio.
- **Derived indices:** total sleep time, sleep-onset latency, REM latency, wake after sleep onset, sleep efficiency, stage percentages, arousal index, AHI/RDI, periodic limb movement index.
- **Home sleep tests and actigraphy** do not stage sleep reliably [1].

### Differential: causes of reduced slow-wave sleep or short REM latency
| Finding | Causes |
| --- | --- |
| Short REM latency | Depression, narcolepsy, REM-suppressant withdrawal, sleep deprivation, OSA, shift work, schizophrenia (some) |
| ↓ N3 | Ageing, depression, schizophrenia, alcohol use disorder, benzodiazepines, chronic pain, fibromyalgia (alpha-delta sleep) |
| ↑ REM | REM rebound (withdrawal of antidepressants or alcohol; after deprivation) |
| REM without atonia | RBD, SSRIs/SNRIs, narcolepsy, synucleinopathies |

### Management details: using architecture knowledge clinically
- Choose a **sedating antidepressant** (mirtazapine, trazodone) when insomnia with low slow-wave sleep is prominent.
- **Avoid abrupt stopping of REM-suppressants:** REM rebound causes vivid dreams and nightmares, and rebound cataplexy in narcolepsy.
- **Screen for RBD** in older people started on SSRIs, as it may be a prodromal synucleinopathy.
- **Chronotherapy for depression:** total or partial sleep deprivation (wake therapy), sleep-phase advance and bright light give rapid but transient response. Combined with lithium or light, they sustain benefit.
- **Elderly:** explain that light, fragmented sleep is partly normal ageing, to limit unnecessary hypnotics.

### Evidence table
| Study | Design | Result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Kupfer & Foster 1972 | PSG case series | Short REM latency in depression |
| Benca et al. 1992 (Arch Gen Psychiatry) | Meta-analysis, 177 studies | Disturbed continuity across disorders; short REM latency and ↓ SWS most marked in affective disorders, but not specific [2] |
| Ohayon et al. 2004 (Sleep) | Meta-analysis, normative data | Age-related ↓ SWS and efficiency; REM relatively stable [3] |
| Ferrarelli et al. 2010 | PSG/hd-EEG | ↓ spindles in schizophrenia, correlated with symptoms and cognition [1] |
| Wilson & Argyropoulos 2005 (Drugs) | Review | REM suppression by most antidepressants |

### Critical appraisal
- **Specificity:** short REM latency occurs in several disorders, so it is **not a diagnostic test for depression**. Sensitivity and specificity are inadequate.
- **State vs trait:** some changes persist in remission and in high-risk relatives (trait), while others normalise (state).
- **Antidepressant paradox:** most antidepressants suppress REM, but bupropion, mirtazapine and agomelatine do not, so REM suppression is not necessary for an antidepressant effect.
- **Laboratory effect:** first-night effects and the artificial setting limit how well a single PSG generalises.

### Indian context
- PSG is mostly available in tertiary centres. Research on sleep architecture in Indian psychiatric samples comes from NIMHANS, AIIMS and PGIMER.
- The IPS sleep CPG lists PSG indications rather than architecture-based diagnosis of psychiatric disorders [1].

### Recent advances
- **Established:** AASM scoring (version 3); hd-EEG spindle research [1].
- **Emerging:**
  - Automated AI-based sleep staging.
  - Spindle enhancement for cognition in schizophrenia (eszopiclone trials).
  - Slow-wave enhancement by closed-loop acoustic stimulation.
  - Sleep EEG biomarkers of antidepressant response (e.g., ketamine increases slow-wave activity).
- **Wake therapy** (chronotherapy) is being revisited as a rapid antidepressant strategy.
