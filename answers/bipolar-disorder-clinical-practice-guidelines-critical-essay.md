## Exam answer

### Introduction

Bipolar disorder is a recurrent mood disorder characterised by episodes of mania or hypomania and depression. Clinical practice guidelines synthesise evidence to support diagnosis, acute treatment, relapse prevention and monitoring. Important guidelines include CANMAT/ISBD, NICE, British Association for Psychopharmacology and Indian Psychiatric Society recommendations.

### General principles

-   Confirm diagnosis through longitudinal history and collateral
    information.
-   Assess episode polarity, severity, psychosis, suicide risk,
    substance use and comorbidities.
-   Exclude medical and substance-induced causes.
-   Consider previous treatment response, adherence, patient preference
    and reproductive potential.
-   Develop an individualised acute and maintenance plan.
-   Combine pharmacotherapy with psychoeducation and appropriate
    psychotherapy.

### Acute mania

First-line options in major guidelines include lithium, valproate and selected second-generation antipsychotics such as quetiapine, olanzapine, risperidone, aripiprazole and others depending on guideline and patient factors. Combination treatment may be needed for severe mania or inadequate response.

Assess agitation, risk, hydration, sleep, physical health and need for admission. Benzodiazepines may be used short-term for severe agitation or insomnia, with caution.

### Bipolar depression

Options supported by guidelines include quetiapine, lurasidone (often with lithium or valproate), lithium, lamotrigine and selected combination regimens. Olanzapine–fluoxetine is an option in some guidelines. ECT may be considered for severe, psychotic, catatonic, suicidal or treatment-resistant depression.

Antidepressant monotherapy is generally avoided in bipolar I disorder because of risk of mood switching and destabilisation. If antidepressants are used, they are usually adjunctive and require monitoring.

### Hypomania and bipolar II disorder

Treatment depends on severity, impairment and predominant polarity. Quetiapine has evidence for bipolar depression; lithium and lamotrigine may be considered for maintenance or depressive-predominant illness. Antidepressants require caution and careful monitoring.

### Maintenance treatment

Lithium is an important maintenance treatment and has evidence for relapse prevention and suicide-risk reduction. Other options include valproate, lamotrigine and selected antipsychotics, depending on polarity and prior response. Treatment should be continued after recovery when recurrence risk is significant.

### Special clinical situations

-   **Rapid cycling:** review thyroid function, substances,
    antidepressant exposure and adherence; optimise mood stabilisation.
-   **Mixed features:** avoid treatments likely to worsen activation;
    use mood stabilisers and antipsychotics according to presentation.
-   **Pregnancy and reproductive potential:** carefully balance relapse
    risk and fetal/neonatal risks. Valproate has major teratogenic and
    neurodevelopmental risks and should generally be avoided in
    pregnancy and in people who could become pregnant unless stringent
    safeguards and specialist justification apply.
-   **Comorbid substance use:** integrate addiction treatment.
-   **Children and older adults:** use age-appropriate assessment,
    dosing and monitoring.

### Psychosocial interventions

Psychoeducation, CBT, family-focused therapy and IPSRT improve adherence, early relapse recognition, routine regulation and family functioning. Sleep regularity, exercise, substance avoidance and social rhythm stabilisation are important.

### Monitoring

-   **Lithium:** serum levels, renal and thyroid function, calcium,
    hydration and toxicity education.
-   **Valproate:** liver function, blood counts, weight/metabolic
    effects and reproductive safety.
-   **Antipsychotics:** weight, glucose, lipids, blood pressure,
    extrapyramidal effects and prolactin where relevant.
-   **Lamotrigine:** slow titration and education about serious rash.
-   Monitor mood symptoms, functioning, adherence, suicide risk and
    adverse effects.

### Critical appraisal

Guidelines support evidence-based care but cannot replace clinical judgement. Evidence differs across episode types and patient groups; comorbidity, culture, cost and availability affect implementation. Many trials exclude complex patients. In India, affordability, laboratory access, specialist availability and continuity of care influence feasibility.

### Conclusion

Guideline-based bipolar treatment requires phase-specific pharmacotherapy, long-term relapse prevention, psychosocial interventions and systematic monitoring. Individualisation and shared decision-making are essential.
