## Exam answer

**Introduction.** SARS-CoV-2 is a β-coronavirus. It is the **seventh coronavirus known to infect humans**. It enters cells through the **spike (S) protein binding ACE2**, primed by **TMPRSS2**. **Neuropilin-1** is a co-receptor [1].

ACE2 is expressed in lung, kidney, gut, **capillary endothelium** and the brain (cortex, striatum, brainstem, choroid plexus, thalamic paraventricular nuclei, middle temporal and posterior cingulate gyri) [1]. Neurological features are therefore common:
- they are reported more often in **older patients** and those with **comorbidities**;
- they are associated with **greater severity, disability and mortality** [1].

A systematic review (Ousseiran 2021; 113 articles, January 2020 to February 2021) classified the manifestations into **CNS, PNS and skeletal muscle** categories [1].

### Mechanisms of neuro-invasion and injury [1]
1. **Direct (neurotropism):**
   - **olfactory route**: nasal epithelium, then olfactory nerve with retrograde axonal and trans-synaptic transport (explains anosmia);
   - **haematogenous**: infection of ACE2-positive endothelium, BBB breach;
   - possible **enteric nervous system** and respiratory nerve routes.
2. **Immune-mediated:**
   - **cytokine storm** (IL-6, TNF-α, CRP), BBB disruption, ROS, neuronal dysfunction;
   - **post-infectious autoimmunity / molecular mimicry**: GBS, ADEM, myasthenia gravis.
3. **Vascular / coagulopathy:** endotheliitis, raised **D-dimer**, complement activation. Causes ischaemic stroke, CVST and haemorrhage.
4. **Hypoxia:** pneumonia, then hypoxic-ischaemic injury, cerebral vasodilatation, oedema.
5. **Systemic and iatrogenic** (general knowledge): sepsis, metabolic derangement, organ failure, sedation, ICU factors, drugs.

**Notably, CSF RT-PCR was usually negative.** This favours **indirect** mechanisms in most cases [1].

### Classification of manifestations [1]
**A. Central nervous system:**
| Manifestation | Key data [1] |
| --- | --- |
| **Headache** | 3.7–43% (holocranial or bifrontal; 68.3% in one series); associated with poorer memory scores |
| **Dizziness** | 0.9–16.8%; sometimes the first symptom |
| **Encephalopathy / altered mental status** | Most common in many cohorts (**81%** in a French series of 58 inpatients); AMS 29–68% in hospital cohorts; linked to longer stay and **higher mortality**; frontal hypometabolism persisting 6 months |
| **Delirium** | 14.8–84.3% in cohorts; **54.9%** in a 2,088-patient multinational ICU study |
| **Cerebrovascular disease** | **Ischaemic stroke** (often **large-vessel occlusion**, young patients, high D-dimer and CRP); **CVST**; **ICH**; **microbleeds** (corpus callosum, juxtacortical); 8.2% of 1,760 Italian patients had neurological complications, 38.7% of them cerebrovascular |
| **PRES** | In critically ill patients with hypertension and AKI; reversible with BP control |
| **Encephalitis / meningoencephalitis** | Case reports; CSF PCR occasionally positive; pleocytosis; MRI temporal and frontal changes |
| **Acute necrotising (haemorrhagic) encephalopathy** | Case reports (cytokine storm) |
| **ADEM / acute haemorrhagic leukoencephalitis; acute myelitis / transverse myelitis** | Post-infectious demyelination |
| **Seizures / status epilepticus** | Uncommon (for example, 1 in 350); secondary to CVST, encephalitis, ICH |
| **Movement disorders** | **Acute parkinsonism** (young patients, MRI changes); **myoclonus** (generalised, diaphragmatic); **acute cerebellar ataxia**; **opsoclonus–myoclonus–ataxia** |
| **Neuropsychiatric** | 22.5% of 40,469 patients (anxiety 4.6%, mood disorders 3.8%, suicidal ideation under 1%); depression 37.9% and sleep impairment 49.5% in an Italian prospective study; a florid post-infarct neuropsychiatric syndrome (paranoia, aggression, disinhibition) |
| **Others** | Akinetic mutism, locked-in syndrome, tremor, MS exacerbation |

**B. Peripheral nervous system:**
| Manifestation | Key data [1] |
| --- | --- |
| **Anosmia / ageusia** | Very common: **68.5% anosmia** in an Indian prospective study of 250 patients, with recovery in 1–2 weeks; may occur in otherwise **asymptomatic** people; associated with **milder disease** |
| **Guillain–Barré syndrome** and variants (AIDP, **AMAN**, **Miller Fisher**) | **14–30 days** after respiratory symptoms; flaccid paresis, facial palsy, ophthalmoplegia; albuminocytological dissociation; responds to **IVIg** |
| **Cranial and peripheral nerve palsies** | Facial palsy; polyneuritis cranialis; recovery with steroids or supportive care |
| **Hearing loss, tinnitus** | Case reports |

**C. Skeletal muscle and neuromuscular junction:**
- **Myalgia and fatigue**; **skeletal muscle injury** with raised **CK** (4.7–19.4%), associated with high CRP and D-dimer and with liver and renal dysfunction [1].
- **Myasthenia gravis** (new ocular or bulbar MG, **anti-MuSK**) weeks after infection; responds to pyridostigmine and immunotherapy [1].
- **Rhabdomyolysis**; **critical-illness myopathy and neuropathy** [general knowledge].

### Timing
- **Early or presenting:** anosmia, ageusia, headache, dizziness, sometimes stroke or encephalopathy **before respiratory symptoms**.
- **During severe illness:** encephalopathy, delirium, stroke, seizures, PRES.
- **Post-infectious (2–6 weeks):** GBS, ADEM, myelitis, MG, OMA syndrome.
- **Long-term:** cognitive impairment ("brain fog"), fatigue, dysautonomia, mood and sleep disorders (**long COVID**). A concern exists about a **parkinsonism wave**, by analogy with post-1918 encephalitis lethargica [1].

### Assessment
- **History:** timing relative to infection, vaccination status, risk factors.
- **Neurological examination.**
- **Delirium screening:** CAM-ICU [general knowledge].
- **Bloods:** D-dimer, CRP, ferritin, IL-6, CK, electrolytes, glucose, renal and liver function.
- **Neuroimaging:** CT/CTA for stroke; **MRI** (SWI for microbleeds; FLAIR; venography for CVST).
- **EEG:** non-specific slowing; non-convulsive status; frontal sharp waves in some reports.
- **CSF:** cell count, protein, **SARS-CoV-2 RT-PCR** (rarely positive), autoimmune panel.
- **Nerve conduction studies / EMG** for GBS or myopathy; repetitive nerve stimulation and antibodies for MG [1].

### Management
- **Treat the infection and its systemic effects** (oxygenation, steroids where indicated, anticoagulation per protocols).
- **Specific treatment:**
  - **stroke:** thrombolysis or thrombectomy per standard protocols;
  - **CVST:** anticoagulation;
  - **GBS:** IVIg or plasmapheresis [1];
  - **encephalitis or ADEM:** steroids or IVIg;
  - **MG:** pyridostigmine plus immunosuppression [1];
  - **PRES:** blood pressure control [1];
  - **seizures:** standard antiseizure medicines (**watch interactions** with antivirals such as nirmatrelvir–ritonavir) [general knowledge].
- **Delirium:** non-pharmacological measures first. Low-dose antipsychotics for distress or danger; check QTc with azithromycin or HCQ (see `covid-19-delirium-management`).
- **Rehabilitation:** physiotherapy, cognitive rehabilitation, olfactory training (for anosmia) [general knowledge].
- **Psychiatric:** screen for depression, anxiety, PTSD and cognitive impairment at follow-up (see `long-covid`, `covid-19-neuropsychiatric-syndromes`).

**Conclusion.** COVID-19 produces a **wide spectrum of neurological manifestations**:
- **CNS:** headache, encephalopathy and delirium, stroke, encephalitis, seizures, movement disorders;
- **PNS:** anosmia and ageusia, GBS, cranial neuropathies;
- **muscle:** myalgia, myositis, MG.

These arise through **direct neurotropism (olfactory and haematogenous)** and, more often, **indirect** mechanisms (cytokine storm, coagulopathy, hypoxia, autoimmunity). Many are associated with severe disease and higher mortality, so early neurological monitoring is important. Some, such as anosmia, can be the **first or only** sign [1].

## In depth

### Background
- The **earlier coronaviruses** (SARS-CoV, MERS-CoV) also caused anosmia, ischaemic stroke, meningoencephalitis, acute necrotising encephalopathy and acute flaccid paralysis [1].
- **Mao et al. (Wuhan, 2020; 214 patients)**: one of the first series. It reported dizziness, headache, taste and smell impairment, CVD and skeletal muscle injury [1].

### Epidemiology
- Neurological manifestations occurred in **about 35% of hospitalised patients** in a Turkish prospective study [1], and in up to about 80% of severe cases in some series [general knowledge].
- The **Indian** data in the review include:
  - a cross-sectional study (Garg; 391 patients: taste, smell, headache);
  - a case series (Kushwaha; 14 patients: meningoencephalitis, stroke, GBS, anosmia);
  - Thakur (250 patients: olfactory and gustatory dysfunction) [1].

### Classification by mechanism
| Mechanism | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| Direct viral | Anosmia; rare PCR-positive encephalitis |
| Para-infectious inflammatory | Encephalopathy, ANE, myoclonus |
| Post-infectious autoimmune | GBS, ADEM, MG, myelitis |
| Vascular | Stroke, CVST, ICH, microbleeds, PRES |
| Hypoxic and metabolic | Encephalopathy, delirium |
| Critical illness | ICU-acquired weakness |

### Mechanism in detail [1]
- Neuro-invasion via **NRP1** (olfactory epithelium and tubercles, para-olfactory gyri) and **TMPRSS2** (enteric neurons and glia; choroid-plexus endothelium).
- **IL-6** and **D-dimer** increase vascular permeability and activate complement and coagulation.
- Chronic neuro-inflammation could contribute to **neurodegeneration** (Parkinson's disease, Alzheimer's disease, MS). This is speculative.

### Clinical features: neuropsychiatric overlap
- Delirium and encephalopathy dominate in the ICU.
- **Post-acute:** "brain fog", depression, anxiety, insomnia, PTSD.
- **Frontal syndromes** with persistent hypometabolism at 6 months [1].

### Assessment: imaging findings [1]
- **MRI:** acute ischaemic infarcts (23.3% of 73 patients in a French series); microhaemorrhages in the corpus callosum and subcortical white matter; deep venous thrombosis; leptomeningeal enhancement; medial temporal changes.
- **EEG:** generalised slowing; non-specific encephalopathy markers.

### Differential diagnosis
- **Septic encephalopathy** from other causes.
- **Drug-induced:** steroids (psychosis); HCQ (neuropsychiatric effects); sedatives.
- **Wernicke encephalopathy** (prolonged ICU stay, vomiting).
- **Hyponatraemia, hypoglycaemia, uraemia.**
- **Vaccine-related:** VITT-associated CVST (adenoviral-vector vaccines); GBS after vaccination [general knowledge].

### Management details
- **Anticoagulation** for hospitalised patients per protocols. Weigh it against haemorrhage risk in microbleeds or ICH.
- **IVIg** for GBS (5 days, 0.4 g/kg/day) [general knowledge for the dose].
- **Olfactory training** (four odours twice daily for 12 weeks) [general knowledge].

### Critical appraisal [1]
- **Heterogeneous** literature, so no meta-analysis was possible.
- **Mostly case reports and retrospective cohorts**, with quality concerns.
- **Causality is uncertain**: background incidence (GBS, stroke) vs true association.
- **Selection bias** towards hospitalised and severe patients.
- **Early pandemic** data (pre-vaccine, pre-Omicron); manifestations may differ with later variants [general knowledge].

### Indian context
- High burden during the **Delta wave (2021)**. **COVID-associated mucormycosis** (rhino-orbito-cerebral) was a distinctive Indian neurological complication, linked to steroids and diabetes [general knowledge].
- The IPS COVID CPG addresses neuropsychiatric care (see `covid-19-neuropsychiatric-syndromes`).

### Recent advances
- **Long COVID** neurocognitive phenotypes; MRI cohorts (**UK Biobank**: grey-matter loss in orbitofrontal and parahippocampal regions after mild infection) [general knowledge].
- **Persistent viral reservoirs** and **autoantibodies** as mechanisms of long COVID.
- **Olfactory training** and platelet-rich plasma for persistent anosmia.
