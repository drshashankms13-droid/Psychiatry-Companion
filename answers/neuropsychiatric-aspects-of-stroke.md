## Exam answer

### Introduction [1]
- **Outcome:**
  - about **a quarter die within 1 month**;
  - at 6 months about half have motor loss, **a quarter are disoriented**, about 15% have significant aphasia, and over 20% are at least moderately dependent;
  - at 5 years, one-third are moderately or severely disabled.
- **Psychiatric and cognitive sequelae** are often **the main barrier to independence**: in long-stay patients "paralysis by itself rather seldom accounts for their incapacity" (Adams & Hurwitz).
- **Psychiatric morbidity is shaped by:**
  - **lesion** (site, size, laterality);
  - **reaction** to sudden disability;
  - **premorbid personality and vulnerability**;
  - **social setting**.

### Classification of neuropsychiatric sequelae [1, 2]

| Domain | Key facts |
| --- | --- |
| **Acute confusion** | Global confusion and disorientation early, slow to clear in extensive damage; worsened by anoxia (cardiac failure, chest infection) and communication barriers. The longer clouding lasts, the worse the residual deficits |
| **Focal cognitive syndromes** | **Aphasia** (a large handicap, often outlasting motor recovery; expressive aphasia with good comprehension adjusts better). **Apraxia** (especially of gait) obstructs rehabilitation. **Neglect and anosognosia** (right hemisphere 43% vs left 20% at onset; 17% vs 5% at 3 months; denial of hemiplegia predicts poor independence). **Amnesic syndrome** from posterior cerebral infarction may be missed |
| **Post-stroke dementia** | Prevalence **6–32%**. Stroke **doubles** the risk of new dementia, including AD. **Risks:** low education, **pre-stroke cognitive decline**, severe stroke, **left hemisphere**, silent infarcts, atrophy, leucoaraiosis. **Strategic infarcts:** left **angular gyrus** (mimics AD), bilateral basal forebrain, **bilateral (paramedian) thalamic** (hypersomnolent, apathetic, vertical gaze palsy, "subcortical" dementia). Increases mortality and recurrence |
| **Thalamic syndromes** (Carrera & Bogousslavsky) | **Anterior:** apathy, amnesia, perseveration. **Paramedian:** amnesia, loss of self-activation, disinhibition. **Inferolateral:** executive dysfunction. **Posterior:** neglect, aphasia |
| **Personality change** | **Directly organic** change often reflects widespread vascular disease and heralds **progressive dementia**: a "**reduction of margins**", rigid routine, irritability, catastrophic reactions, hypochondriasis, paranoia in isolated people. **Carer ratings:** at 1 year over half slowed, worried, miserable or complaining of pain; over a third withdrawn, irritable, fearful. Worse with depression, ADL impairment, carer distress, total anterior circulation strokes |
| **Depression** | **About one-third** [1]; 30–40% [2]. Predicted by severity and social isolation; laterality disputed. **Suicidal ideation about 10%; suicide risk doubled; higher mortality.** See the post-stroke depression answer |
| **Emotionalism** (pathological crying/laughing) | **21% at 6 months, 11% at 12 months**; left anterior lesions; responds to TCAs and SSRIs [1]; citalopram, sertraline, nortriptyline, dextromethorphan–quinidine [2] |
| **Anxiety** | **GAD in about 25%**, usually with depression. **Agoraphobia and social withdrawal** (fear of looking conspicuous; fear that exertion will cause recurrence) |
| **Apathy / abulia** | Mild apathy is common (usually with depression, sometimes alone). **Extreme abulia** with **left frontal** lesions improves in hospital and relapses at home, mimicking depression (Förstl & Sahakian) |
| **Mania** | Uncommon; **right hemisphere** (right temporal, right thalamocapsular); within days to weeks. **Bilateral thalamic infarcts** caused cyclical mood disorder via frontal diaschisis (McGilchrist) |
| **Psychosis** | Non-affective psychosis about **1–2%**. **Acute delusions in 4%** (Kumral): within days, **right posterior temporoparietal**, with atrophy, **Capgras** and misidentification, mean 13 days. **Late psychosis** with **right temporo-parieto-occipital** lesions plus **post-stroke seizures** (Levine & Finklestein); constructional apraxia; subcortical atrophy adds risk |
| **Hallucinosis** | **Peduncular hallucinosis** (midbrain/pons): vivid, insight retained, resolves in days to weeks. Auditory hallucinations after **right temporal** cortical strokes |
| **Epilepsy** | Early seizures 3–6% (fourfold with haemorrhage); late epilepsy 2–4%. Stroke is the **commonest cause of late-onset epilepsy**. Linked to worse outcome, **psychosis** and cognitive impairment |
| **Carer burden** | **About 40% of carers depressed**; persistent responsibility, worry and social restriction |

### Assessment [1]
1. **Timing:** separate early confusion from enduring deficits; reassess as clouding clears.
2. **Cognition:** global vs focal. Beware **pseudo-dementia** from aphasia, constructional problems, agitation, depression, apathy or agnosia. Screen and use neuropsychology.
3. **Mood:** depression vs apathy, emotionalism, somatic stroke symptoms, flat expression and abulia (Box 8.5); special care in **aphasia**; suicide risk.
4. **Psychosis:** look for misidentification, seizures and right-hemisphere lesions.
5. **Personality and behaviour:** carer interview.
6. **Carer** mental health and burden.

### Management [1, 2]
**General:**
- organised **stroke-unit care** and **multidisciplinary rehabilitation** (Box 8.6);
- secondary prevention;
- early assessment;
- family as team members;
- community reintegration.

**Specific:**

| Problem | Management |
| --- | --- |
| **Depression** | SSRIs (citalopram/escitalopram if anticoagulated; PPI with antiplatelets; caution after haemorrhage), mirtazapine, nortriptyline [2]. A monitored trial with a stopping protocol [1]. Care management; exercise. Prophylaxis not routine |
| **Emotionalism** | SSRI trial [1]; TCAs; dextromethorphan–quinidine [2]; explanation to family |
| **Anxiety / agoraphobia** | Recognise (usually co-occurs with depression; treat that). Specific anxiety treatments after stroke are not described in the sources |
| **Psychosis / delirium** | Early psychosis may **resolve spontaneously**. Antipsychotics carry **stroke risk in the elderly**: discuss risks with patient and family; use cautiously (haloperidol or quetiapine were used in Kumral's series) [1] |
| **Mania** | Treat as secondary mania (no stroke-specific drug data in the source) |
| **Cognitive deficits** | Cognitive rehabilitation (EFNS guidance): compensatory memory strategies, **errorless learning**, simple repeated instructions, practical demonstration (see the cognitive rehabilitation answer) |
| **Aphasia** | Communication strategies; SLT (unproven but helps morale); swallowing assessment |
| **Neglect** | Approach from the intact side; arrange belongings; mostly spontaneous recovery |
| **Motivation and apathy** | Team encouragement; **motivational interviewing** improves mood |
| **Personality change** | Structured environment, clear guidelines, flexibility; watch for evolving dementia |
| **Carers** | Information; problem-solving training (reduces carer depression); social worker; discharge planning. Interventions to reduce burden have limited evidence |
| **Seizures** | Recognise as a risk for psychosis and cognitive decline |

**Conclusion.** Stroke produces a broad spectrum of neuropsychiatric morbidity, which often matters more than paralysis for outcome:
- acute confusion;
- focal syndromes (aphasia, apraxia, neglect, anosognosia);
- post-stroke dementia (6–32%; strategic thalamic or angular gyrus infarcts);
- personality change;
- depression (about one-third, with suicide risk) and emotionalism (21% at 6 months);
- anxiety (25%), apathy or abulia;
- right-hemisphere mania and psychosis (often with Capgras or seizures), and hallucinosis.

Management is integrated stroke rehabilitation with targeted treatment: SSRIs with bleeding precautions, SSRIs for emotionalism, cautious short-term antipsychotics, cognitive rehabilitation, and carer support.

## In depth

### Background
- Classic focal neuropsychology came from **stroke survivors**: Broca (1861), Wernicke (1874), Liepmann's apraxia (1900), Babinski's anosognosia (1914) [1].
- **Adams and Hurwitz (1963, 1974):** in long-stay stroke patients, **mental impairment, not paralysis**, explained most incapacity [1].
- **Goldstein's "catastrophic reaction"** and the "**reduction of margins**" describe how brain-damaged people cope by restricting themselves to routine [1].
- **Lhermitte (1922)** described peduncular hallucinosis [1].
- Modern frameworks:
  - **post-stroke dementia** (Leys 2005), with the stroke as index event [1];
  - **vascular cognitive impairment (VCI)** and **VASCOG** / **VICCCS** criteria [general knowledge].

### Epidemiology [1; general knowledge for India]
**Stroke types [1]:**
- **Ischaemic about 85%:**
  - large-artery atherosclerosis about 50%;
  - **lacunar about 25%**;
  - cardioembolic about 20%;
  - other causes 5%.
- **Haemorrhagic about 15%:**
  - primary intracerebral haemorrhage, hypertensive (deep) or amyloid (lobar);
  - **SAH about 5% of strokes**, incidence 6–7 per 100,000; half of patients are under 55; about half die.

**Outcome [1]:**
- **one-quarter die within 1 month**; 1-year case fatality 34–40% for ICH and SAH;
- at 6 months, a quarter are disoriented and 15% aphasic;
- at 5 years, one-third disabled.

**Neuropsychiatric frequencies [1]:**
- post-stroke dementia **6–32%**;
- depression about one-third;
- GAD about 25%;
- emotionalism 21% at 6 months;
- non-affective psychosis 1–2%;
- acute delusions 4%;
- neglect 43% (right) vs 20% (left) at onset;
- carer depression about 40%.

**Additional figures [general knowledge]:**
- post-stroke **delirium** 10–48% (pooled about 25%);
- **post-stroke fatigue** 25–75%;
- **PTSD** about 20–25% at 1 year (meta-analysis, Edmondson 2013);
- **obstructive sleep apnoea** in 50–70%;
- **apathy** about one-third (Caeiro 2013 meta-analysis).

**India [general knowledge]:**
- stroke incidence about 105–152 per 100,000 per year;
- **higher proportion of haemorrhagic stroke** and younger age;
- **cerebral venous thrombosis** relatively common (postpartum, anaemia);
- tuberculous meningitis vasculitis and neurocysticercosis are local causes of stroke.

### Classification of stroke relevant to neuropsychiatry [1]
**TOAST (aetiology):** large-artery atherosclerosis; cardioembolism; small-artery occlusion (lacunar); other determined aetiology; undetermined.

**Oxford/Bamford (OCSP) clinical subtypes:**
| Subtype | Features | Neuropsychiatric relevance |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **TACI** (total anterior circulation) | Hemiplegia, hemianopia, **aphasia or neglect** | **Worst prognosis** (60% dead and 4% independent at 1 year); most personality change [1] |
| **PACI** (partial anterior) | Two of three, or isolated higher cortical dysfunction | Early recurrence; focal syndromes |
| **POCI** (posterior circulation) | Brainstem, cerebellar, occipital | Amnesia (PCA, thalamus), visual agnosia, **peduncular hallucinosis**, Anton syndrome |
| **LACI** (lacunar) | Pure motor or sensory, ataxic hemiparesis | Multiple lacunes, leucoaraiosis, **subcortical vascular cognitive impairment**, apathy, "vascular depression" |

**Neuropsychiatric sequelae by time course** [1; general knowledge]:
- **Hyperacute and acute (days):** delirium; acute confusion; anosognosia; acute delusions; mania; catastrophic reactions; peduncular hallucinosis.
- **Subacute (weeks to months):** depression; emotionalism (onset at 4–6 weeks); anxiety; apathy; fatigue.
- **Chronic (months to years):** post-stroke dementia; personality change; late psychosis (often after seizures); PTSD; agoraphobia; chronic fatigue; carer burden.

### Mechanism in detail
**Lateralisation [1; general knowledge for the left/right summary]:**
| Left hemisphere | Right hemisphere |
| --- | --- |
| Aphasia; ideomotor apraxia | Neglect, **anosognosia**, aprosodia |
| **Catastrophic reactions** | **Indifference reaction** (denial, inappropriate jocularity) |
| Depression (early, anterior; disputed) [1] | **Mania** (right temporal, thalamocapsular) [1] |
| Post-stroke dementia more likely [1] | **Delusions**: Capgras, reduplicative paramnesia, somatoparaphrenia (posterior temporoparietal) [1] |
| Emotionalism (anterior left; longer-lasting) [1] | **Late psychosis with seizures** (temporo-parieto-occipital) [1] |
| **Abulia** (left frontal) [1] | Constructional apraxia [1] |

**Network and remote effects:**
- **Diaschisis:** remote functional depression. In McGilchrist's case, bilateral thalamic infarcts produced frontal hypoperfusion and **cyclical mood disorder** [1].
- **Frontal–subcortical circuits:** lesions anywhere in the loops cause circuit-specific syndromes [general knowledge; 1 for thalamic territories]:
  - caudate, globus pallidus, thalamus, frontal cortex;
  - dorsolateral circuit: executive dysfunction;
  - orbitofrontal circuit: disinhibition;
  - anterior cingulate circuit: apathy and akinetic mutism.
- **Thalamic infarcts** are "unique insights into brain–behaviour relationships" because of four distinct arterial territories [1].
- **Misidentification** requires a right posterior lesion **plus** frontal dysfunction or atrophy (the "two-hit" model). The atrophy found in Kumral's patients fits this [1; general knowledge for the model].
- **Peduncular hallucinosis:** midbrain or pontine lesions disrupt the ascending reticular and REM-regulating systems, producing dream-like intrusions [1; general knowledge for mechanism].

**Post-stroke dementia mechanisms [1]:**
- **strategic infarct** (angular gyrus, basal forebrain, thalamus);
- **multiple infarcts**;
- **small-vessel disease** (lipohyalinosis, leucoaraiosis from endothelial dysfunction and blood–brain barrier breakdown);
- **unmasked or accelerated Alzheimer pathology** (stroke doubles AD risk);
- **pre-stroke cognitive decline**.

### Clinical features in detail: syndromes not covered above
**Post-stroke delirium** [general knowledge]:
- **Risk factors:** older age, pre-stroke cognitive impairment, **haemorrhagic stroke**, large or right hemisphere lesions, infection, metabolic disturbance, polypharmacy.
- **Consequences:** longer stay, mortality, institutionalisation, dementia.
- **Screen** with **4AT** or CAM-ICU.
- **Manage:** treat causes; non-drug measures; brief low-dose antipsychotic only for distress or danger.

**Akinetic mutism and abulia** [1; general knowledge]:
- bilateral medial frontal or anterior cingulate lesions (ACA territory, ACoA aneurysm rupture);
- paramedian thalamic or mesencephalic lesions;
- alert-appearing but without spontaneous speech or movement.

**Post-stroke fatigue** [general knowledge]:
- disproportionate, not relieved by rest;
- associated with depression but distinct;
- graded activity; treat sleep apnoea and depression; **modafinil** (small RCT; MIDAS trial).

**Post-stroke PTSD** [general knowledge]:
- intrusive memories of the stroke; avoidance of medical settings;
- **non-adherence to secondary prevention** and higher recurrence;
- trauma-focused CBT.

**Anosognosia and somatoparaphrenia [1]:** denial of hemiplegia and disowning of limbs carry a poor prognosis for independence and are associated with prolonged incontinence.

**SAH-specific neuropsychiatry [1]:**
- **Acute:**
  - **delirium in about 16%** (more with intraventricular blood, hydrocephalus, age);
  - presentation as **delirium without headache**;
  - **akinetic mutism**, misidentification, amnesic–confabulatory states;
  - mislabelled as drunkenness or hysteria [1].
- **ACoA syndrome:**
  - **basal forebrain** damage gives **Korsakoff-like amnesia with confabulation**, disorientation and poor insight;
  - only about 1 in 10 ACoA cases (3 of 30 in Bottger 1998);
  - causes: vasospasm, hydrocephalus, perforator injury at surgery, direct haemorrhage;
  - **donepezil** improved memory in an open study [1].
- **Late:**
  - **anxiety in 27%** (Walton);
  - persistent headache 23%;
  - personality change ("**organic moodiness**": moderate–severe in 19%, mild in 22%; Storey);
  - frontal disinhibition with ACoA; risk-taking on gambling tasks;
  - **personality "improvement" in about 10%** (less worry) [1];
  - cognitive impairment in **25%** even with good outcome (Samra 2007) [1].
- **Pituitary deficiency** after SAH may contribute to chronic symptoms [1].
- **Late hydrocephalus** can cause mental deterioration months or years later [1].

### Assessment: tools
| Domain | Tool [general knowledge unless cited] |
| --- | --- |
| Delirium | 4AT, CAM-ICU |
| Cognition | **MoCA** (better than MMSE for vascular executive deficits); OCS (Oxford Cognitive Screen, aphasia- and neglect-friendly); ACE-III |
| Neglect | Line bisection, star cancellation, Catherine Bergego Scale |
| Mood | PHQ-9, HADS, SADQ-10 (aphasia) |
| Emotionalism | Kim's criteria; Center for Neurologic Study-Lability Scale (CNS-LS) |
| Apathy | Apathy Evaluation Scale; Lille Apathy Rating Scale |
| Anxiety | GAD-7, HADS-A |
| Fatigue | Fatigue Assessment Scale |
| Function | Barthel Index, modified Rankin Scale |
| Carer | Zarit Burden Interview; HADS for carers (associated with patient personality change) [1] |
| Imaging | MRI (DWI, FLAIR for small-vessel disease, SWI for microbleeds); lesion mapping for laterality questions |

### Differential diagnosis
- **New neuropsychiatric change after stroke:** consider **recurrent stroke**, **non-convulsive seizures**, **hydrocephalus** (especially after SAH or ICH), subdural haematoma, infection, hyponatraemia, **drug effects** (levetiracetam: irritability, depression; benzodiazepines; baclofen; beta-blockers) [1; general knowledge for drugs].
- **Pre-existing disorders unmasked:** dementia (pre-stroke decline), recurrent depression, alcohol misuse (withdrawal after admission).
- **Functional overlay** and **conversion** symptoms can coexist with genuine stroke [general knowledge].
- **Stroke mimics in psychiatry:** conversion disorder; migraine aura; hypoglycaemia; Todd's paresis [general knowledge].

### Management details
**Antipsychotics after stroke [1, 2]:**
- antipsychotics are linked to **increased stroke risk and mortality in the elderly**. Use the **lowest dose for the shortest time** and document the discussion with family [1];
- early psychosis often **resolves spontaneously** [1];
- prefer **quetiapine** or low-dose haloperidol for delirium-related agitation [1];
- **avoid** in Lewy-body-type sensitivity.

**Cognitive impairment** [general knowledge]:
- **cholinesterase inhibitors and memantine** have modest effects in vascular dementia (Cochrane; not licensed in the UK for VaD);
- treat **vascular risk factors** (BP, statins, antiplatelets, AF anticoagulation);
- **cognitive rehabilitation**: compensatory strategies, errorless learning [1].

**Mania** [general knowledge]:
- valproate or antipsychotics;
- **lithium** can be used but beware renal function and interactions;
- look for seizures (EEG).

**Late psychosis with seizures:** optimise antiseizure treatment; use an antipsychotic with lower seizure threshold effects (avoid clozapine and chlorpromazine) [1; general knowledge].

**Emotionalism** [1, 2]:
- **SSRIs** (citalopram, fluoxetine, sertraline): rapid effect within days;
- nortriptyline;
- **dextromethorphan–quinidine** (licensed for PBA in the US and EU) [2];
- educate the family that crying does not necessarily mean sadness.

**Anxiety and agoraphobia** [1; general knowledge]:
- graded exposure; CBT;
- SSRIs; avoid long-term benzodiazepines (falls, cognition);
- address **fear of recurrence** through education about secondary prevention.

**Driving and capacity** [general knowledge]:
- neglect, hemianopia and executive deficits affect driving;
- assess **decision-making capacity** for discharge destination, finances and treatment.

**Rehabilitation principles (Box 8.6) [1]:**
- prevent complications;
- early, standardised assessment;
- goal-based evidence-based interventions;
- a coordinated team;
- family as team members;
- education;
- community reintegration;
- ongoing management of risk factors.
- **Outpatient therapy:** 7 per 100 spared a poor outcome [1].

### Critical appraisal
- **Selection bias:** severe cases, **aphasic patients** and those with dementia are excluded from many studies. Prevalence is therefore underestimated [1].
- **Laterality findings** are inconsistent and depend on timing and setting [1].
- **Small case series** underpin mania, psychosis and misidentification syndromes [1].
- **Carer interventions** have not shown benefits for patients [1].
- **Speech therapy and neglect rehabilitation** evidence is weak [1].
- Lishman (2009) predates large trials and meta-analyses on fatigue, PTSD, delirium and apathy. Those sections are drawn from later literature [general knowledge].

### Indian context [general knowledge]
- **Rising burden**: about 1.8 million strokes a year. A large urban–rural gap in stroke units, thrombolysis and rehabilitation.
- **Neuropsychiatric sequelae are under-recognised** in neurology wards. Liaison psychiatry is present mainly in tertiary hospitals.
- **Family-based care** places a large burden on carers (mostly women).
- **Traditional beliefs** (stroke as fate or punishment) shape coping.
- **Rehabilitation:** community-based rehabilitation programmes; Tele-MANAS for mood follow-up.
- **RPwD Act 2016:** locomotor disability, speech and language disability, and chronic neurological conditions are certifiable.

### Recent advances [general knowledge]
- **Thrombectomy-era survivors** with larger salvaged penumbra: different neuropsychiatric profiles are emerging.
- **Network lesion mapping** shows that lesions causing depression, delusions or mania map to common functional networks (Fox, Darby; for example, the right orbitofrontal network for mania).
- **Post-stroke cognitive impairment (PSCI)** guidelines (the AHA 2023 statement).
- **Digital cognitive rehabilitation** and VR for neglect.
- **SSRIs for motor recovery:** not supported (FOCUS, AFFINITY, EFFECTS).
