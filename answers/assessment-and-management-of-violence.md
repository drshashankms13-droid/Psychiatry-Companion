## Exam answer

**Introduction [1].**
- Acute behavioural disturbance occurs in **psychiatric illness, physical illness, substance misuse and personality disorder**.
- Psychotic patients may be aggressive **because of persecutory delusions or hallucinations** (auditory, visual, tactile).

### 1. Assessment
**Exclude a medical cause first** [1]. In an emergency, ask whether there could be a medical cause. **Optimise the regular prescription.**

**Clinical causes and associations:**
- **Psychiatric:** psychosis (persecutory delusions, hallucinations); mania; intoxication and withdrawal [1].
- **Organic [2]:**
  - **limbic and amygdala** lesions;
  - **temporal lobe epilepsy**: explosive aggression as a trait in some patients. Violence as a direct consequence of seizures is rare;
  - birth trauma, **head injury**, encephalitis;
  - **frontal (prefrontal) dysfunction**: dorsolateral lesions linked to impulsive aggression, medial lesions to unempathic aggression;
  - in 5 of 14 repetitively violent patients with organic syndromes, **anterior temporal lesions near the amygdala** were found (Tonkonogy).
- **Habitually aggressive offenders** (explosive personality) [2]:
  - mostly male, from disturbed families, with outbursts since adolescence;
  - **abnormal EEGs in 65%** of habitually aggressive violent offenders (57% after excluding brain damage), often temporal and immature-appearing, improving with age;
  - neuropsychological studies associate violence with **executive deficits**.
- **Episodic dyscontrol / intermittent explosive disorder** [2]: violence as the only overt sign of disturbed cerebral physiology, once epilepsy, brain damage and psychosis are excluded. Its nosological status is **controversial**; Lucas regards it as invalid.
- **Intervening variables** such as affective disorder or paranoid psychosis are common, especially in serious violence [2].

**Contextual factors** [4]: distress over isolation, death of others, and staff relationships. **Promptly predict imminent violence**: look for agitation and irritability.

### 2. Prevention and practical measures [1]
- **Plan in advance** for individual patients, to prevent disturbed behaviour and reduce risk.
- **Nursing interventions:** **de-escalation**, time out, seclusion; more nursing staff; transfer to a **PICU**.
- **The three aims of RT:**
  1. reduce the patient's suffering;
  2. reduce the risk of harm to others;
  3. **do no harm**: safe regimens and physical monitoring.
- RT is a **treatment of last resort**, after psychological and behavioural approaches fail. Patients often experience it as **punitive**.

### 3. Rapid tranquillisation, stepwise (Maudsley summary) [1]
**Step 1:** de-escalation, time out, placement.

**Step 2: offer oral treatment.**
- **Already on a regular antipsychotic:** lorazepam 1–2 mg or promethazine 25–50 mg. Buccal midazolam 10 mg (unlicensed) may avoid IM treatment.
- **Not on an antipsychotic:** olanzapine 10 mg, risperidone 1–2 mg, quetiapine 50–100 mg, or **haloperidol 5 mg with promethazine 25 mg** (ECG required in the UK/EU).
- **Repeat after 45–60 minutes** if needed. Go to step 3 if two doses fail, or sooner if risk is significant.
- **Other oral or sublingual options:** sublingual asenapine; inhaled loxapine (restricted: bronchospasm); sublingual dexmedetomidine.

**Step 3: IM treatment.**
- **Single-drug IM:** **midazolam** (7.5–15 mg; 5 mg also effective) and **droperidol** are the **fastest-acting**.
- **IM olanzapine 10 mg:** as effective as haloperidol, with fewer EPSEs.
- **Haloperidol plus promethazine** is more effective and better tolerated than haloperidol alone (TREC 3: 6% dystonia). It beat lorazepam 4 mg (TREC 2).
- **Haloperidol alone should be avoided**: poorly tolerated, with **5% acute dystonia**. Some suggest abandoning it altogether.
- **Second line:** benzodiazepine plus antipsychotic combinations.
- **Third line:** IV benzodiazepines, then **ketamine 2–5 mg/kg IM**, only where **intubation** facilities exist (30% eventually intubated; laryngospasm in more than 1%).

**Zuclopenthixol acetate (Acuphase):**
- **not** a rapid tranquilliser: sedation at 2–4 hours, antipsychotic effect only after 8 hours;
- use only after repeated injections are needed, and when **at least 60 minutes have passed since an IM dose**;
- **never** in neuroleptic-naïve patients or physically resisting patients (risk of oil embolus).

**Avoid** antipsychotic polypharmacy (QT risk) and high cumulative doses. "High-dose sedation" is **no more effective** and causes more hypotension and desaturation.

### 4. Physical monitoring after parenteral RT [1]
- **Temperature, pulse, BP, respiratory rate every 15 minutes for 1 hour**, then hourly until ambulatory.
- Keep the patient **continuously in sight for at least 1 hour**; use pulse oximetry if asleep.
- **ECG for all who receive haloperidol** (QTc can rise by up to 15 ms after 10 mg IM). Hypokalaemia and agitation add arrhythmia risk.
- **Remedial measures:**
  - **acute dystonia**: procyclidine 5–10 mg IM or IV;
  - **respiratory rate under 10 or SpO₂ under 90%**: oxygen, raise the legs, never face-down; **flumazenil** for benzodiazepine-induced depression (200 mcg IV, then 100 mcg every 60 s, maximum 1 mg in 24 hours);
  - pulse irregular or under 50: urgent medical care;
  - BP fall: lie flat, tilt head-down;
  - **fever: check CK for NMS**.

### 5. Special groups
- **Children, adolescents and the elderly:** lower doses [1].
- **Pregnancy** [3]:
  - de-escalation, then oral lorazepam 1–2 mg, promethazine 25–50 mg, olanzapine 5–10 mg or chlorpromazine 50–100 mg;
  - then IM lorazepam 2 mg, promethazine, haloperidol 2.5–5 mg (with promethazine) or olanzapine 10 mg;
  - monitor fetal well-being; **right hip elevated**.
- **Alcohol intoxication** [1]: combined haloperidol 5 mg plus lorazepam 2 mg was more effective than either alone.
- **Medically ill (COVID)** [4]: psychotropics for agitation and violence, **with attention to oxygen saturation**.

### 6. Longer-term management of persistent aggression [2]
- **Treat the underlying disorder** (psychosis, epilepsy, mood disorder).
- Habitually aggressive persons often **outgrow** aggression by middle age, but resist intervention.
- **Psychosurgery** (amygdalotomy, Ramamurthi's Indian series of 481 bilateral amygdalotomies; temporal lobectomy) is historical and controversial: an **absolute last resort**.

**Conclusion.** Managing violence means:
- assessing medical, psychiatric and organic causes;
- preventing through planning and de-escalation;
- stepped RT: oral first, then IM midazolam, droperidol, olanzapine or haloperidol plus promethazine;
- avoiding haloperidol alone, polypharmacy and Acuphase for RT;
- rigorous physical monitoring and remedial protocols.

## In depth

### Key RT trial findings [1]
| Trial or comparison | Result |
|---|---|
| TREC 1 | IM midazolam 7.5–15 mg sedated faster than haloperidol 5–10 mg plus promethazine 50 mg |
| TREC 2 | Haloperidol plus promethazine beat lorazepam 4 mg |
| TREC 3 | Haloperidol plus promethazine beat haloperidol alone |
| TREC 4 | Olanzapine 10 mg as effective as haloperidol plus promethazine short term, but shorter-lasting |
| Emergency room (n = 737) | IM midazolam 5 mg faster than olanzapine, ziprasidone, haloperidol |
| Droperidol QT | QTc over 500 ms in under 2%; no torsades |
