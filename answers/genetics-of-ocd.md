## Exam answer

### Introduction

OCD is a heterogeneous neuropsychiatric disorder with a substantial genetic contribution. Family, twin and molecular genetic studies support heritability, particularly in early-onset OCD, but no single gene explains the disorder.

### Family and twin studies

First-degree relatives of individuals with OCD have an increased risk. Twin studies generally show greater concordance in monozygotic than dizygotic twins, supporting genetic contribution. Heritability estimates vary by study and age of onset.

### Candidate genes and pathways

Research has examined:
- **Serotonergic:** SLC6A4 and serotonin receptor genes.
- **Dopaminergic:** DRD2, DRD3, DRD4 and COMT.
- **Glutamatergic:** SLC1A1 and other glutamate-related genes.
- **Neurodevelopmental/synaptic:** BDNF and genes involved in synaptic organisation.

Early candidate-gene findings have often been inconsistent and should not be treated as established causal associations.

### Genome-wide studies

Genome-wide association studies and Psychiatric Genomics Consortium work support a polygenic architecture involving many variants of small effect. Larger samples are needed to identify robust loci and clarify biological pathways.

### Childhood-onset OCD

Earlier onset may show stronger familial loading and greater genetic contribution. PANDAS/PANS are proposed acute-onset syndromic constructs involving infection-associated symptoms in selected children; they are not explanations for most OCD and require careful differential assessment.

### Epigenetics and gene–environment interaction

Stress, development and environmental exposures may influence gene expression through epigenetic mechanisms. These findings remain largely investigational.

### Clinical implications and limitations

There is currently no genetic test that diagnoses OCD or reliably selects treatment. Genetic counselling is not routinely indicated solely because of OCD. Family history may inform clinical understanding but does not determine an individual's outcome.

### Conclusion

OCD is a polygenic, neurodevelopmentally influenced disorder with familial aggregation. Genetic research is improving understanding of its biological heterogeneity but has limited current direct clinical application.
