## Exam answer

**Definition.** **Synthetic cannabinoids (SCs)**, more precisely **synthetic cannabinoid receptor agonists (SCRAs)**, are laboratory-made psychoactive substances designed to mimic Δ9-THC by acting on the endocannabinoid system. They are the largest and fastest-changing group of **new psychoactive substances (NPS)**. They are sprayed onto plant material and sold as "herbal incense" under brand names such as **Spice, K2, Black Mamba, "fake weed" and Joker** [1].

Examples:
- **JWH-018, JWH-073, JWH-200** (naphthoylindoles).
- **CP-47,497** (cyclohexylphenol).
- **HU-210** (classical cannabinoid).
- **AB-FUBINACA, AB-CHMINACA, AB-PINACA** (indazole carboxamides).
- **XLR-11, UR-144** (tetramethylcyclopropylindoles).
- **5F-PB-22** (quinolinyl ester) [1].

Hundreds of new compounds keep appearing to evade drug laws [1].

### Mechanism
SCs are **full agonists** at **CB1R** (mainly CNS) and **CB2R** (immune cells). THC is only a **partial** agonist. Many SCs have far higher binding affinity: HU-210 has a CB1 Ki of about 0.06 nM, against about 9 nM for JWH-018 [1].
- **Presynaptic CB1R** → Gi/o → ↓ adenylyl cyclase, ↓ PKA, ↓ voltage-gated Ca²⁺, ↑ K⁺ currents → suppressed neurotransmitter release (GABA and glutamate).
- **Postsynaptic** → MAPK/ERK1/2 activation → gene expression changes [1].
- **Pro-psychotic effects:** ↑ dopamine release in nucleus accumbens and ↑ firing of VTA (A10) DA neurons; CB1–ERK-mediated **5-HT2A upregulation** and 5-HT2A–D2 interaction in PFC; altered NMDA function [1].
- **Off-target actions:** GPR55, GPR18, GPR119, PPARs and TRPV1 contribute to a wider, less predictable toxicity profile [1].
- **No cannabidiol (CBD).** Natural cannabis contains CBD, which may protect against psychosis; SC products lack it [1].
- **Toxicity mechanisms:** oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, inflammation and apoptosis (caspase-3) in neurons, myocardium, renal tubules and liver [1].

### Clinical features: SC vs cannabis

| Feature | Cannabis (THC) | Synthetic cannabinoids |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Receptor action | Partial CB1 agonist; contains CBD | **Full**, high-potency CB1/CB2 agonists; no CBD |
| Product consistency | Relatively predictable | Unpredictable mixtures and doses; "hot spots" on plant material |
| Intoxication | Euphoria, relaxation, tachycardia, red eyes | **Severe agitation, anxiety, aggression, delirium**, catatonia-like "zombie" state |
| Psychosis | Dose-dependent risk | **More frequent, more severe, more prolonged**; may occur in people without vulnerability [1] |
| Seizures | Rare | Common; status epilepticus reported [1] |
| Cardiovascular | Tachycardia | Tachycardia, **hypertension**, arrhythmias (AF, VF, AV block), **myocardial infarction** in young people, cardiac arrest, stroke [1] |
| Renal / hepatic | Rare | **Acute kidney injury** (ATN, interstitial nephritis), toxic hepatitis [1] |
| Other | — | Vomiting, hyperemesis, respiratory depression, hypokalaemia, rhabdomyolysis, alveolar haemorrhage, visual disturbance [1] |
| Urine immunoassay | Positive for THC | **Usually negative** (needs LC-MS/MS) |
| Withdrawal | Mild–moderate | Often more severe: irritability, insomnia, craving, sweating, tachycardia; seizures reported |

**Chronic use** leads to dependence and cognitive impairment (attention, learning, memory, flexibility, emotional processing) [1].

### Assessment
- **History:** product name and source, route, frequency, co-use; collateral history. **Suspect SCs** when a cannabis-like history is matched by a negative urine THC screen and severe agitation [1].
- **Examination:** vitals (BP, heart rate, temperature), mental state, neurological examination.
- **Investigations:**
  - ECG and **troponin**, CK, urea/creatinine, electrolytes (K⁺), LFT, glucose, ABG.
  - Urine LC-MS/MS where available.
  - CT/MRI if seizures or focal signs.

### Management
**Acute intoxication** (supportive; no antidote):
- Calm environment.
- **Benzodiazepines** (e.g., lorazepam 1–2 mg, repeated) for agitation and seizures.
- Antipsychotic for psychosis or severe agitation (e.g., haloperidol or olanzapine, after ECG/QTc).
- IV fluids for AKI and rhabdomyolysis.
- Treat hypertension, arrhythmia and MI per cardiology.
- **N-acetylcysteine** has been used in SC hepatotoxicity [1].
- Monitor for at least 6–24 hours.

**Then:**
- Treat persistent psychosis as a substance-induced psychotic disorder; review the diagnosis if it persists beyond about 4 weeks.
- **Withdrawal:** symptomatic benzodiazepines; quetiapine has been reported.
- **Longer term:** MET and CBT-based relapse prevention; treat co-morbidity; harm-reduction education. There is no specific pharmacotherapy.

### Key evidence
- Most evidence is from **case series, poison-centre data and outbreaks**, not RCTs [1].
- **New York "zombie" outbreak** (2016, Adams et al., NEJM): 33 people were affected by **AMB-FUBINACA**, which is many times more potent than JWH-018.
- In vitro and animal work shows CB1-mediated neuronal apoptosis, mitochondrial dysfunction and ROS in neural, cardiac and renal cells [1].

**Conclusion.** Synthetic cannabinoids are **not "legal cannabis"**. They are high-potency full agonists of unpredictable composition, lack protective CBD, and cause severe psychiatric (agitation, psychosis), neurological (seizures), cardiovascular, renal and hepatic toxicity, with deaths reported. Standard drug screens miss them. Management is supportive, so prevention, awareness among adolescents, and rapid legal scheduling of new compounds are the key public-health responses.

## In depth

### Background
- **1960s–1990s:** compounds such as HU-210 (Hebrew University), CP-55,940 (Pfizer) and the JWH series (**John W. Huffman**, Clemson University) were synthesised as research tools to map the endocannabinoid system. The CB1 and CB2 receptors were cloned in 1990 and 1993.
- **Mid-2000s:** "Spice" products appeared in Europe as herbal incense. JWH-018 was identified in them in 2008. Successive generations followed each legal ban (naphthoylindoles → cyclopropylindoles → indazole carboxamides → fluorinated analogues), each typically more potent and more toxic [1].
- The **UNODC** monitors hundreds of SCRAs within the NPS early warning system. The US DEA has placed many in Schedule I [1].

### Epidemiology
- Use is concentrated among **adolescents and young adults** [1], prisoners (undetectable on routine tests), homeless people, and those subject to drug testing (military, workplaces, probation).
- Overdose deaths have increased in recent years [1].
- In India, SC use has been reported in metropolitan party settings and in emergency case reports, but systematic prevalence data are lacking. The Magnitude of Substance Use in India survey (2019) did not measure SCs separately.

### Classification and criteria
- **ICD-11** has a separate grouping: **disorders due to use of synthetic cannabinoids**. It includes a single episode of harmful use, harmful pattern of use, dependence, intoxication, withdrawal, delirium, psychotic disorder, and mood and anxiety disorders induced by synthetic cannabinoids.
- **DSM-5-TR** has no separate category. Problems are coded under cannabis-related or other (unknown) substance-related disorders. Check the current DSM-5-TR text for the preferred coding.
- **Chemical classes:** classical (HU-210), non-classical (CP-47,497), aminoalkylindoles (JWH-018), indazole carboxamides (AB-PINACA), quinolinyl esters (5F-PB-22), cyclopropylindoles (XLR-11) [1].

### Mechanism in detail

| Organ | Mechanism | Result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Brain: neurons | CB1 → ↓ CaMKIV/CREB phosphorylation (JWH-081, hippocampus); caspase-3 apoptosis; ↓ mitochondrial membrane potential; ROS, IL-6, TNF-α | Cognitive impairment, neurotoxicity [1] |
| Brain: glia | GPR18–CB2 → microglial activation; GPR55-Rho → neuroinflammation, blood–brain barrier damage; intense CB1 activation overrides PPAR neuroprotection | Neuroinflammation [1] |
| Seizures | Strong CB1 binding (mechanism unclear); loss of GABAergic inhibition | Seizures, status epilepticus [1] |
| Heart | CB1 → p38/JNK MAPK → apoptosis; mitochondrial hyperpolarisation and ATP accumulation → apoptosis; sympathetic activation | Hypertension, arrhythmia, MI [1] |
| Kidney | CB1/CB2 on tubular cells → mitochondrial dysfunction, ER stress; toxic metabolites | AKI (ATN, AIN) [1] |
| Liver | Oxidative and ER stress; inflammation; PPARα-driven ketogenesis | Toxic hepatitis, metabolic acidosis [1] |
| Lung / immune | CB1-mediated inflammation; immune suppression | Alveolar damage or haemorrhage, respiratory failure [1] |

**Dose effect:** at high doses the protective non-cannabinoid pathways (GPR55, PPAR) are overwhelmed and harmful CB1 signalling dominates [1].

### Clinical features in detail
- **Onset:** within minutes when smoked; duration 2–6 hours, sometimes longer with active metabolites.
- **Psychiatric:** anxiety, panic, paranoia, hallucinations, catatonic or "zombie-like" stupor, aggression, suicidal behaviour. Psychosis can be prolonged and may unmask schizophrenia [1].
- **Neurological:** drowsiness, dizziness, confusion, delirium, seizures, impaired motor performance [1].
- **Cardiac presentations** in adolescents: three teenagers had ECG changes and raised troponin consistent with MI one day after smoking K2 (Mir et al.) [1].
- **Renal presentation:** severe nausea, vomiting, flank pain, raised creatinine [1].

### Assessment in detail
- Standard immunoassays are designed for THC-COOH and **do not detect most SCs**. Confirmation needs **LC-MS/MS** of urine or blood, which is rarely available, and assays must be updated for new compounds.
- Consider SCs in any young person with unexplained severe agitation, psychosis, seizures, MI, AKI or toxic hepatitis.

### Differential diagnosis

| Condition | Distinguishing point |
| --- | --- |
| Cannabis intoxication or psychosis | Positive THC screen; usually milder |
| Synthetic cathinone ("bath salts") intoxication | Sympathomimetic picture, hyperthermia; may co-exist |
| Stimulant (amphetamine, cocaine) toxicity | Positive screen; mydriasis; hyperthermia |
| Anticholinergic delirium | Dry skin, urinary retention, mydriasis |
| Serotonin syndrome / NMS | Clonus, hyperthermia / rigidity, drug history |
| Primary psychotic or manic disorder | Persists after detoxification; prodrome |
| Encephalitis, epilepsy | Fever, CSF/EEG findings |

### Management details
- **Agitation:** benzodiazepines first. Physical restraint can worsen rhabdomyolysis and hyperthermia.
- **Antipsychotics:** acceptable for psychosis, with ECG monitoring (arrhythmia risk) and caution about lowering seizure threshold.
- **Cardiac and renal:** standard ACS pathway; hydrate and monitor creatinine and CK; nephrology input for AKI.
- **Hepatic:** N-acetylcysteine may restore oxidative capacity [1].
- **Persistent psychosis:** continue an antipsychotic. Follow up for evolution into a primary psychotic disorder.
- **Psychosocial:** motivational interviewing, CBT, relapse prevention; family work; vocational support.
- **Special populations:**
  - Adolescents: school education.
  - Prisoners: SC use is a major problem in custodial settings.
  - Pregnancy: fetal risks are unknown, so advise avoidance.

### Critical appraisal
- Evidence is mostly **case reports, case series and preclinical studies**, so incidence and causality are uncertain [1].
- Products contain **mixtures and adulterants** (sometimes opioids or cathinones), so attributing effects to specific compounds is difficult.
- **Unknown long-term effects** on cognition and psychosis risk; no prospective cohorts exist.
- **Regulatory "whack-a-mole":** banning individual compounds drives the creation of new, more potent analogues. Generic or analogue legislation is the more effective response.
- Some preclinical work suggests therapeutic potential (e.g., lowering ocular pressure in glaucoma), but **serious systemic and ocular side effects** rule out use [1].

### Indian context
- **Law:** the NDPS Act, 1985 lists specific narcotic drugs and psychotropic substances. Several SCRAs have been brought under control by notification, but new analogues may fall outside current schedules. Check the latest NDPS schedule notifications.
- **Detection:** most Indian laboratories cannot detect SCs, so cases are probably under-recognised. Emergency physicians should suspect SCs in young people with toxicity and negative screens.
- **Services:** de-addiction centres (NDDTC AIIMS, NIMHANS, PGIMER); National Action Plan for Drug Demand Reduction; Nasha Mukt Bharat Abhiyaan; Tele-MANAS.
- **Public health:** awareness in colleges and party settings; online sale through social media and the dark web.

### Recent advances
- **Established:** ICD-11 recognition of SC-specific disorders; LC-MS/MS screening panels; UNODC early-warning advisories.
- **Emerging:**
  - Newer "generations" (e.g., MDMB-4en-PINACA, ADB-BUTINACA) with very high potency.
  - Research into CB1 negative allosteric modulators as potential treatments for intoxication.
  - **Investigational:** CB1 antagonists for toxicity (rimonabant was withdrawn because of psychiatric adverse effects, so caution is needed).
