## Exam answer

**Introduction.** Healthcare workers (HCWs) went "an extra mile" as frontline workers throughout the pandemic [1]. They worked:
- in **PPE** that made care physically difficult;
- with **fear of infection** and of infecting their families;
- through **public stigma**;
- through **surges in patient load** in the first and second waves.

The result was significant **burnout, depression, anxiety, sleep disturbance and substance use** [1].

### Magnitude [general knowledge; verify figures]
- **Lai et al. 2020 (*JAMA Netw Open*, Wuhan, n = 1,257):**
  - depression 50%;
  - anxiety 45%;
  - insomnia 34%;
  - distress 72%.

  Higher in **nurses, women, frontline workers and those in Wuhan**.
- **Pappa et al. 2020 meta-analysis:** anxiety about 23%, depression about 23%, insomnia about 39%.
- **Later meta-analyses:** PTSD symptoms in about 20–30%; burnout in about 40–50%.
- **Indian studies:** high rates of anxiety, depression, insomnia and stress, especially among nurses and resident doctors. Violence against HCWs and evictions of HCWs by landlords were reported [general knowledge].

### Stressors
| Domain | Stressors |
| --- | --- |
| **Infection-related** | Fear of infection and death; **fear of infecting family** (self-isolation from family); PPE shortages; colleagues falling ill or dying |
| **Work-related** | Long shifts; **PPE discomfort** (heat, dehydration, no toilet breaks); redeployment outside specialty; staff shortages; high mortality; **triage and rationing** (oxygen, ICU beds in the second wave) |
| **Moral and ethical** | **Moral injury**: inability to give the care they believe is right; patients dying alone; breaking bad news by phone or video |
| **Social** | **Stigma** (seen as carriers; eviction); separation from family; childcare; also "hero" pressure |
| **Organisational** | Unclear communication; lack of rest areas; inadequate support; salary cuts or delays |
| **Personal** | Pre-existing mental illness; female sex; younger age; less experience; **nurses**; lack of social support |

### Psychological consequences
- **Acute:** anxiety, fear, irritability, insomnia, exhaustion, acute stress reactions.
- **Disorders:**
  - **depression**;
  - **generalised anxiety**;
  - **insomnia**;
  - **PTSD** (especially ICU and emergency staff);
  - **substance use** (alcohol, sedatives) [1];
  - suicidal ideation.
- **Burnout (Maslach):** emotional exhaustion, depersonalisation (cynicism), reduced personal accomplishment.
- **Moral injury and compassion fatigue.**
- **Occupational:** absenteeism, intention to leave, medical errors.
- **Positive outcomes:** post-traumatic growth, team cohesion, professional pride.

### Assessment
- **Routine screening programmes** for HCWs [1]:
  - PHQ-9, GAD-7, ISI;
  - **Maslach Burnout Inventory**;
  - IES-R;
  - AUDIT;
  - C-SSRS when indicated.
- Mental health professionals should **evaluate burnout, stress and psychiatric morbidity in HCWs on COVID duty** [1].

### Interventions
**Organisational (most important):**
- **adequate PPE**, training and clear protocols;
- **reasonable rosters**: shift limits, rotation between high- and low-stress areas, mandatory rest;
- clear, honest communication from leadership;
- safe accommodation (protecting families);
- food and transport; childcare;
- recognition and fair pay;
- protection from violence (Epidemic Diseases (Amendment) Act 2020 made violence against HCWs a cognisable, non-bailable offence) [general knowledge];
- insurance (PM Garib Kalyan package: ₹50 lakh cover for HCWs) [general knowledge].

**Team-level:**
- **peer-support programmes** (e.g., TRiM-style trauma risk management; buddy systems);
- regular team huddles and **Schwartz rounds** or Balint-type groups;
- ethical consultation for triage decisions (sharing moral burden).

**Individual:**
- **psychological first aid**;
- stress management, relaxation, mindfulness;
- sleep hygiene;
- **helplines** and confidential counselling for HCWs (many Indian institutions set up dedicated lines);
- tele-counselling.
- **Treatment of disorders** according to existing guidelines [1]:
  - CBT, trauma-focused therapies;
  - SSRIs;
  - alcohol and sedative use management.
- **Avoid routine single-session debriefing** (psychological debriefing may increase PTSD risk; NICE) [general knowledge].
- **Reduce barriers** to help-seeking: confidentiality, fear of career consequences, self-stigma among doctors.

**If HCWs themselves get COVID-19:** manage as for other patients [1].

**Conclusion.** The pandemic produced substantial **depression, anxiety, insomnia, PTSD, burnout and moral injury** among HCWs, especially nurses, women, junior staff and frontline workers. The evidence favours **organisational measures** (PPE, rosters, communication, rest, safety) plus **peer support, screening and accessible confidential care**. Protecting HCWs' mental health is essential for workforce sustainability and patient safety in current and future pandemics.

## In depth

### Background
- **SARS 2003 (Toronto, Hong Kong):** HCWs had high distress, PTSD symptoms and long-term burnout, with stigma and quarantine as key factors [general knowledge].
- **COVID-19** amplified these effects at global scale and over a longer duration.

### Epidemiology
- Higher risk among:
  - nurses vs doctors;
  - women;
  - younger staff;
  - frontline vs second-line workers;
  - those with infected colleagues;
  - those with pre-existing illness [general knowledge].
- **Second wave in India (April–May 2021):** oxygen shortages and mass deaths, with a high moral-injury burden [general knowledge].

### Classification
- **Burnout:** ICD-11 **QD85**, an "occupational phenomenon", not a medical condition.
- **Moral injury:** not a diagnosis; a psychological wound from violating one's moral beliefs (Litz 2009).
- **Compassion fatigue / secondary traumatic stress.**

### Mechanism
- **Job demands–resources model:** high demands with low resources lead to burnout.
- **Effort–reward imbalance.**
- **Conservation of resources theory.**
- Chronic stress causes HPA dysregulation, sleep loss, emotional exhaustion and depression.

### Clinical features
- Exhaustion, detachment, irritability, cynicism.
- Intrusive memories of deaths; guilt (survivor guilt; "could have done more").
- Avoidance; alcohol use.
- Hypervigilance about symptoms (health anxiety).

### Assessment
- Self-report scales; occupational health screening.
- **Confidential pathways** separate from line management.

### Differential diagnosis
- Burnout vs depression (overlap; depression is pervasive beyond work).
- Normal stress vs adjustment disorder.
- Long COVID fatigue in HCWs who were infected.

### Management: evidence
- **Systematic reviews** show organisational and peer interventions have the best support. Individual mindfulness and resilience programmes give modest benefit (Pollock 2020 Cochrane: limited evidence) [general knowledge].
- **Psychological first aid** is recommended (WHO, IASC guidance) [general knowledge].

### Evidence table
| Study | Finding |
| --- | --- |
| IPS CPG (Grover 2022) | HCW burnout, depression, anxiety, sleep and substance problems; need for awareness, screening and management [1] |
| Lai 2020, *JAMA Netw Open* (general knowledge) | Wuhan HCWs: depression 50%, anxiety 45%, insomnia 34%, distress 72% |
| Pappa 2020, *Brain Behav Immun* (general knowledge) | Pooled anxiety 23%, depression 23%, insomnia 39% |
| Pollock 2020, Cochrane (general knowledge) | Limited evidence on interventions for frontline workers' mental health |

### Critical appraisal
- Most studies are **cross-sectional online surveys** with self-report scales, so prevalence is likely **overestimated** by probable-case cut-offs.
- Little longitudinal or intervention research from India.
- Heterogeneous definitions of burnout.

### Indian context
- **Epidemic Diseases (Amendment) Act 2020:** protection from violence [general knowledge].
- **Insurance scheme for HCWs** (PMGKP) [general knowledge].
- **Institutional helplines** (NIMHANS, AIIMS); the IPS task force.
- **Resident doctors' working hours:** an ongoing problem (NMC guidelines; reports of resident suicides) [general knowledge].

### Recent advances
- **Physician wellness programmes** and chief wellness officers.
- Moral injury frameworks in training.
- **Digital self-help** for HCWs.
