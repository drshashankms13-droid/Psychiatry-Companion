## Exam answer

**Introduction.** Pregnancy does **not protect against psychotic relapse**. Psychosis in pregnancy **predicts postpartum psychosis**. Some groups carry a postpartum psychosis risk of **up to 50%**:
- women with a previous postpartum episode;
- women with bipolar I or schizoaffective disorder;
- women with a family history of postpartum psychosis.

Untreated psychosis harms the mother through poor self-care, missed obstetric care and suicide. It harms the baby through neglect and, rarely, infanticide. **Perinatal suicides are associated with lack of active psychotropic treatment** [1]. The question is therefore rarely "antipsychotic or not". It is **which drug, what dose, and what monitoring**.

### Background facts to state [1]
- **Baseline risks in any pregnancy:**
  - major malformation **2–3% (about 1 in 40)**;
  - early miscarriage 10–20%.
- **Severe mental illness itself** raises the risk of malformations, stillbirth, neonatal death and obstetric near-misses.
- **The evidence is observational:**
  - registry studies;
  - teratology information services;
  - case reports.

  Confounding by **maternal illness, smoking, obesity, alcohol and illicit drugs, and co-prescription** is the main problem when reading this evidence.

### Teratogenicity
- **Neither FGAs nor SGAs are major teratogens** (meta-analysis; Wang 2021) [1].
- **Huybrechts 2016 (JAMA Psychiatry):** over 1 million US pregnancies (733 FGA- and 9,258 SGA-exposed). No meaningful increase in major or cardiac malformations. **Risperidone** showed a small absolute increase, treated as a **safety signal**.
- **Huybrechts 2023 (JAMA Psychiatry):** about 6.5 million US and Nordic pregnancies confirmed **no major teratogenicity**. Signals to watch:
  - **olanzapine:** oral clefts;
  - **all SGAs:** gastroschisis and brain anomalies;
  - **chlorprothixene:** cardiac defects.
- **Haloperidol and limb defects:** based on a handful of cases, not replicated.
- **Cariprazine:** malformations in animal studies, so **avoid** [1].
- **Lurasidone:** data do not suggest major teratogenicity.

### Obstetric and neonatal risks

| Outcome | Notes |
| --- | --- |
| **Gestational diabetes** | Highest with **clozapine, olanzapine, quetiapine**. **Aripiprazole may not raise the risk.** Maternal illness and lifestyle contribute |
| Birth weight | **Olanzapine:** macrosomia, large head circumference, **LGA**. **FGAs:** preterm birth and **low birth weight** |
| Caesarean section, preterm birth, postpartum haemorrhage | Higher with SGAs vs no exposure |
| **Neonatal adaptation and withdrawal** | 15–20% of exposed neonates show somnolence, jitteriness, agitation, crying or increased suckling. This is a **class effect**, worse with late-pregnancy exposure, usually mild and transient |
| **EPS / neonatal dyskinesia** | Mainly FGAs (EMA warning 2011) |
| Persistent pulmonary hypertension (PPHN), neurological disorders | Small absolute risk, mainly with late exposure |
| **Clozapine-specific** | Neonatal seizures; theoretical fetal agranulocytosis; floppy infant |
| Jaundice | Phenothiazines |

### Long-term neurodevelopment
- Early delays at 2–6 months (Peng 2013) **disappeared by 12 months**.
- **Danish cohort of 667,517 children:** no effect on school test scores.
- **ADHD and ASD risk tracks maternal illness, not antipsychotic exposure** (Wang 2021; Hálfdánarson 2022; Straub 2022). There is an aripiprazole signal [1].

### Management principles (Maudsley Box 7.2; NICE CG192) [1]
1. **Pre-conception:**
   - Discuss pregnancy plans with every woman of childbearing potential (half of pregnancies are unplanned).
   - Get specialist **perinatal psychiatry advice**.
   - **Hyperprolactinaemia** (risperidone, amisulpride, FGAs) may prevent conception. Consider switching to a prolactin-sparing drug (aripiprazole, quetiapine) if pregnancy is planned.
2. **If stable on an antipsychotic and likely to relapse: continue it.** Avoid switching, because a switch exposes the fetus to two drugs and risks relapse.
3. **If starting treatment:**
   - choose the drug that **worked best for her before**;
   - use the **lowest effective dose** and **monotherapy**;
   - stop ineffective drugs.
4. **Clozapine:** usually **continue**. Monitor plasma levels, especially if smoking changes. Pharmacovigilance does not show it to be less safe than other antipsychotics.
5. **Depots:** NICE advises avoiding them in pregnancy unless she is already well on a depot and has a history of non-adherence.
6. **Monitoring:**
   - weight and diet;
   - **OGTT for gestational diabetes**;
   - **anomaly scan** (COPE: 13 or 18–20 weeks);
   - growth scans;
   - dose adjustment in the third trimester as blood volume expands about 30%. **CYP1A2 activity falls by up to 70%**, so clozapine and olanzapine levels rise.
7. **Delivery:** in a unit with **neonatal intensive care**. Inform the obstetric and neonatal teams. Monitor the neonate for adaptation syndrome.
8. **Address risk factors:** smoking cessation (NRT; quitting raises clozapine levels), alcohol, drugs, folic acid.
9. **Plan for the postpartum period and breastfeeding:**
   - **Clozapine:** avoid breastfeeding.
   - **Olanzapine and quetiapine:** preferred if starting a drug postpartum.
10. **Shared decision-making and documentation.** Involve the partner and family.

**Conclusion.** No antipsychotic is proven safe, but **none is a major teratogen**. The **risks are mainly metabolic** (gestational diabetes, birth weight) and **transient neonatal adaptation**. These are usually smaller than the risks of untreated psychosis. **Continue the effective drug at the lowest dose and monitor the pregnancy and the neonate closely.**

## In depth

### Background
- Women with schizophrenia have fewer pregnancies than the general population. Rates are rising with SGAs, which lack the hyperprolactinaemia of FGAs and so restore fertility.
- **Unplanned pregnancy is common:** about half of all pregnancies, and probably more in SMI.
- **Pharmacokinetic changes:**
  - blood volume and total body water rise by about 30% in the third trimester;
  - **CYP2D6 activity rises by about 50%** (aripiprazole and risperidone levels may fall);
  - **CYP1A2 activity falls by up to 70%** (olanzapine and clozapine levels may rise) [1].
- **Placental passage:** quetiapine passes relatively little; olanzapine passes the most (Newport 2007, general knowledge).

### Epidemiology
- **Postpartum psychosis:** 0.1–0.25% of births (1–2 per 1,000).
  - About **1 in 5 women with bipolar disorder** relapse with postpartum psychosis.
  - The risk is up to 50% in women with a prior postpartum episode, bipolar I or schizoaffective disorder, and genetic loading [1].
- **Antipsychotic exposure in pregnancy:** about 0.3–1% of pregnancies in Western registries, mostly quetiapine (general knowledge).

### Classification of risk (why FDA letter categories were abandoned)
- The FDA's A/B/C/D/X categories were replaced in 2015 by the **Pregnancy and Lactation Labelling Rule (PLLR)**. It uses narrative risk summaries because letter grades gave a false sense of precision (general knowledge).
- The Maudsley approach weighs individual drug data against the risk of the illness, not categories.

### Mechanism: how drugs and illness each contribute to risk
- **Drug effects:**
  - D2 blockade causes neonatal EPS and dyskinesia;
  - H1 and α1 blockade cause sedation and hypotonia;
  - metabolic effects (insulin resistance) cause **gestational diabetes and macrosomia**.
- **Illness and lifestyle (confounders):**
  - obesity (neural tube defects, GDM);
  - smoking (the greatest share of excess poor outcomes, per Vigod 2020);
  - alcohol and substance use;
  - poor antenatal care;
  - stress;
  - SSRI co-exposure (septal defects).

### Clinical features: the neonatal adaptation syndrome
- Presents in the first days of life:
  - crying, agitation, jitteriness;
  - increased or poor suckling;
  - somnolence;
  - tremor, hypertonia or hypotonia, EPS-like movements.
- Usually **self-limiting**.
- Some centres use **mixed breast and bottle feeding** to soften withdrawal [1].

### Assessment before prescribing
- Psychiatric history:
  - relapse pattern;
  - time to relapse after previous discontinuations;
  - previous postpartum episodes;
  - which drug worked.
- Family history of postpartum psychosis.
- Substance use, smoking, BMI, glucose/HbA1c, prolactin, ECG (QTc).
- Gestational age at exposure:
  - **organogenesis weeks 3–8**;
  - by the time a woman presents, organogenesis exposure has usually already happened, so stopping now does not undo it.
- Social support, partner involvement, capacity and informed choice.

### Differential: choosing among drugs

| Drug | Practical position [1] |
| --- | --- |
| **Quetiapine** | Low placental passage. Common in registries. Gestational diabetes risk. Compatible with breastfeeding (RID about 0.1%) |
| **Olanzapine** | Effective. Gestational diabetes, macrosomia; oral cleft signal. Low RID (1–1.6%) |
| **Aripiprazole** | Possibly no GDM excess; prolactin-sparing. **May reduce milk supply**. ADHD/ASD signal (unconfirmed) |
| **Risperidone/paliperidone** | Small malformation signal; hyperprolactinaemia |
| **Haloperidol** | Long experience; EPS in neonate; limb defect signal not replicated |
| **Clozapine** | Continue if needed; level monitoring; neonatal seizures; **avoid breastfeeding** |
| **Lurasidone** | Reassuring registry data |
| **Cariprazine** | Avoid (animal teratogenicity) |
| **Depots** | Avoid unless already established and needed for adherence |

### Management: a worked plan for a woman with schizophrenia on olanzapine 15 mg at 8 weeks
1. Do not stop. Continue olanzapine 15 mg. Advise that the main risks are GDM and LGA, not malformation.
2. Start folic acid 5 mg if obese or on other high-risk drugs; otherwise 400 µg (local guidelines).
3. Check baseline weight, HbA1c, lipids.
4. Offer an early OGTT (around 16–18 weeks) and again at 24–28 weeks.
5. Anomaly scan at 18–20 weeks; growth scans.
6. Smoking cessation with NRT. Check whether she smokes, because quitting raises olanzapine levels through loss of CYP1A2 induction.
7. Joint obstetric–psychiatric antenatal clinic.
8. Plan delivery in a centre with a neonatal unit.
9. Postpartum relapse plan:
   - early warning signs;
   - sleep protection;
   - family support;
   - consider admission to a mother–baby unit.
10. Breastfeeding is compatible.

| Study | Design / n | Key finding |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Huybrechts 2016, *JAMA Psychiatry* | US Medicaid, >1.3 million pregnancies | No meaningful increase in malformations (FGA, SGA); risperidone signal |
| Huybrechts 2023, *JAMA Psychiatry* | US + Nordic, about 6.5 million | Antipsychotics not major teratogens; olanzapine clefts and SGA gastroschisis/brain anomaly signals |
| Habermann 2013 | Prospective, 561 SGA, 284 FGA | FGAs: preterm birth, LBW. SGAs: higher birth weight, septal defects (possible SSRI or screening confounding); 15% neonatal withdrawal |
| Wang 2021, *JAMA Intern Med* | Hong Kong cohort | ADHD, ASD, preterm birth and SGA explained by maternal illness, not antipsychotics |

| Liu 2022, *JAMA Intern Med* | 667,517 Danish children | No effect on school test scores |
| Heinonen 2022 | Swedish register | Neonatal morbidity mild and transient; GDM highest with olanzapine, clozapine, quetiapine |

### Critical appraisal
- **Confounding by indication** is the central problem. Studies that compare antipsychotic-exposed women with healthy women overstate drug risk.
  - Better designs include **disease-matched comparison** (women with the same illness who stopped), **sibling designs** and **propensity scores**.
- **Small numbers per drug:** signals such as olanzapine clefts may be chance findings from multiple comparisons.
- **Outcome ascertainment:** SGA-exposed women may get more scans (screening bias for septal defects).
- **Neurodevelopmental outcomes** rely on registry diagnoses. Subtle cognitive effects are not excluded.
- **Generalisability to India:** data come almost entirely from Western high-income countries. Indian women differ in BMI distribution, GDM prevalence (South Asians are at high risk), access to scans and delivery settings.

### Indian context
- **Mental Healthcare Act 2017, Section 21(2):** a woman admitted for mental illness has the right not to be separated from her child under 3 years, unless there is a risk to the child. This supports mother–baby admission [general knowledge].
- **NIMHANS** runs a perinatal psychiatry service and a **mother–baby unit**, among the few in India [Likely; check date].
- **IPS Clinical Practice Guidelines (Bharadwaj, Chandra et al., 2022)** [6]:
  - **SGA monotherapy** is preferred for schizophrenia and bipolar disorder in pregnancy;
  - **FGAs or LAIs are not initiated** in pregnancy (less safety data), but are continued if she is well on them and a switch risks relapse;
  - after LAI exposure, stopping does not quickly end fetal exposure;
  - monitor weight and do an **OGTT**;
  - anomaly scans at **10–12 and 16–18 weeks**, with fetal echo for cardiac-risk drugs;
  - **folic acid 5 mg** for all women planning pregnancy;
  - deliver where **NICU** care is available;
  - **clozapine is contraindicated in breastfeeding**.
- **Relapse risk** for severe mental illness is **60–80% without prophylaxis vs 20–30% with it** [6].
- Explain risk using **absolute numbers, visual aids** and a common denominator, and document the discussion (Box 2) [6].
- **Practical issues:**
  - high GDM prevalence in South Asians makes OGTT monitoring especially important;
  - home deliveries;
  - stigma, and pressure from families to stop "medicines harmful to the baby".

### Recent advances
- **Huge registry consortia** (US Medicaid with Nordic registries) now allow drug-specific signals.
- **National Pregnancy Registry for Psychiatric Medications** (Massachusetts General Hospital): lurasidone and quetiapine reassuring (Cohen 2023) [1].
- **ACOG Clinical Practice Guideline No. 5 (2023):** do not withhold or stop medication solely because of pregnancy or lactation.
- **COPE (Australia) 2023:** structured scan recommendations [1].
