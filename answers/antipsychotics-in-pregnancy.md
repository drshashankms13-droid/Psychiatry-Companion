## Exam answer

### Why it matters [1]
- Background risks in any pregnancy:
  - spontaneous abortion **10–20%** of confirmed early pregnancies;
  - major malformation **2–3% (about 1 in 40)**.
- **Psychiatric illness itself** independently raises the risk of malformations, stillbirth and neonatal death, and of obstetric near misses in severe mental illness (SMI).
- **Pregnancy does not protect against psychotic relapse.** Psychosis in pregnancy **predicts postpartum psychosis**.
- Untreated psychosis harms the mother (poor self-care, missed obstetric care) and the child, from **neglect to infanticide**.
- **Perinatal suicides** are associated with lack of active treatment, especially no psychotropic medication. ACOG warns against stopping psychiatric medication **because of pregnancy alone**.
- Safety data come mostly from **database studies with confounding** (maternal illness, smoking, obesity, substances); RCTs would be unethical.

### First-generation antipsychotics (FGAs) [1]
- **Unlikely to be major teratogens.**
  - Early data came from low-dose phenothiazines for **hyperemesis gravidarum**, which itself raises malformation risk.
  - Large US (over 1 million women) and US–Nordic (about 6.5 million) studies found **no meaningful increase** in major or cardiac malformations.
  - **Chlorprothixene** showed a cardiac-malformation **signal** only.
- **Haloperidol and limb defects:** a possible association from a few cases, not replicated; if real, the risk is extremely low.
- **Other risks:**
  - gestational diabetes; preterm birth and **low birth weight** (more than with SGAs in one prospective study);
  - **neonatal somnolence and jitteriness** in 20% after last-week exposure;
  - postpartum bleeding after vaginal delivery;
  - neonatal dyskinesia; neonatal jaundice (phenothiazines);
  - neonatal withdrawal and **persistent pulmonary hypertension of the newborn (PPHN)**: low absolute risk, mostly mild and transient.

### Second-generation antipsychotics (SGAs) [1]
- **Unlikely to be major teratogens** (US cohort of 9258 exposed; US–Nordic cohort of 21,751 exposed).
- **Safety signals only, needing study:**
  - **risperidone**: small absolute increase in malformations;
  - **olanzapine**: oral clefts;
  - all SGAs: gastroschisis and brain anomalies;
  - an NBDPS association with heart defects and gastroschisis may reflect confounders (obesity, substances, smoking).
- **Metabolic risks:**
  - **gestational diabetes**, highest with **clozapine, olanzapine and quetiapine**; **aripiprazole** possibly not associated;
  - increased birth weight, **large for gestational age**, caesarean section (more with SGAs than FGAs);
  - preterm birth.
- **Drug-specific points:**
  - **Olanzapine:** low birth weight, NICU admission, large head circumference, macrosomia.
  - **Clozapine:**
    - possibly more **neonatal seizures**; theoretical risk of **fetal or neonatal agranulocytosis**;
    - pharmacovigilance shows it is **no less safe** than other antipsychotics. NICE lists it as usable in pregnancy;
    - **usually continue**, and monitor **plasma levels** (especially with changes in smoking).
  - **Quetiapine:** relatively **low placental passage**.
  - **Lurasidone:** data do not suggest a major teratogen.
  - **Cariprazine:** malformations in animals; the manufacturer advises against use, so **probably avoid**.
- **Neonatal withdrawal** in 15% of exposed neonates in one prospective study (561 women); PPHN at low absolute risk.

### Longer-term neurodevelopment [1]
- **Unclear, but reassuring overall:**
  - delays at 2 and 6 months were **not present at 12 months**;
  - no effect on school IQ or test scores (cohort of 667,517);
  - **ADHD and autism risk relates to maternal illness, not antipsychotic exposure**;
  - a 2022 cohort found no causal link, with an **aripiprazole signal** needing study.

### Recommendations (Box 7.1, Box 7.2) [1]
**Before pregnancy:**
- Discuss pregnancy with every woman of child-bearing potential; **half of pregnancies are unplanned**.
- Seek **pre-conception perinatal psychiatry advice** for SMI.
- **Hyperprolactinaemia may prevent conception**; switch drugs if pregnancy is planned.
- If she is well and at **low risk**, consider stopping. If she is at **high risk**, do not stop; consider a low-risk drug, bearing in mind that **switching itself risks relapse**.

**If pregnant on an antipsychotic:**
- **Do not stop abruptly.** If she is stable and likely to relapse, **continue the same drug**; switching is generally not advised.

**Starting treatment:**
- Avoid drugs in the **first trimester** where possible; consider non-drug options.
- Use the antipsychotic that **previously worked best**.
- **Lowest effective dose**, **fewest drugs** (at the same time and in sequence); stop ineffective drugs.

**Monitoring:**
- diet and **weight gain**; screen for **gestational diabetes** (NICE: offer an oral glucose tolerance test);
- **fetal ultrasound** (COPE: 13 or 18–20 weeks after first-trimester exposure);
- **adjust doses**: blood volume rises about 30% by the third trimester. **CYP2D6 activity rises about 50%; CYP1A2 falls up to 70%** (relevant to clozapine and olanzapine). Plasma levels may help;
- support stopping **smoking** (NRT preferred). Stopping smoking **raises clozapine levels**.

**Depots:** NICE advises avoiding depots in pregnancy or when planning pregnancy or breastfeeding, **unless** she responds well to a depot and has a history of non-adherence with oral medication.

**Delivery and neonate:**
- inform the obstetric and neonatal teams;
- deliver where there is **paediatric intensive care** access;
- watch for **neonatal discontinuation symptoms** (crying, agitation, increased suckling: a class effect). Some centres use mixed breast and bottle feeding.

**Breastfeeding** [1]:
- usually continue the pregnancy drug;
- **clozapine: continue it but advise against breastfeeding**;
- if starting postpartum, **olanzapine or quetiapine** may be considered.

**Document all decisions** and involve the parents.

**Conclusion.** Neither FGAs nor SGAs are major teratogens; the main concerns are metabolic (gestational diabetes, large babies, especially with clozapine, olanzapine and quetiapine), preterm birth, and transient neonatal withdrawal or adaptation problems. Untreated psychosis carries greater risks: relapse, postpartum psychosis, suicide and infanticide. So:
- a stable woman should usually continue her effective antipsychotic at the lowest dose, without switching;
- use one drug where possible;
- screen for gestational diabetes and fetal anomalies;
- adjust for pharmacokinetic changes;
- avoid cariprazine;
- plan delivery and neonatal care in liaison with obstetrics.
