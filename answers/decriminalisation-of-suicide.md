## Exam answer

**Introduction.** "Decriminalisation of suicide" means removing criminal penalties for **attempted suicide**. Completed suicide was never punishable, because there is no one left to prosecute.

In India, attempted suicide was an offence under **Section 309 of the Indian Penal Code (IPC), 1860**: simple imprisonment up to one year, or a fine, or both [general knowledge].

**Section 115 of the Mental Healthcare Act (MHCA) 2017** brought about de facto decriminalisation. The MHCA was passed on 7 April 2017 and came into force on 29 May 2018 [1].

### Section 115, MHCA 2017 [1, 2]
- **s115(1):** "Notwithstanding anything contained in section 309 of the Indian Penal Code, any person who attempts to commit suicide shall be **presumed, unless proved otherwise, to have severe stress** and shall not be tried and punished under the said Code."
- **s115(2):** the appropriate Government has a **duty to provide care, treatment and rehabilitation** to a person who has severe stress and attempted suicide, **to reduce the risk of recurrence**.

**Key features:**
1. It is a **rebuttable presumption** ("unless proved otherwise") of **"severe stress"**.
   - An earlier draft (2013) used the words "mental illness". These were replaced by **"severe stress"** in 2016.
   - The aims were to avoid labelling every attempter as mentally ill, and to keep a workable relationship with the **abetment** provisions [1].
2. It **does not repeal IPC 309.** It overrides it with a *non obstante* clause. The NSPS (2022) itself notes that "IPC 309 still exists and it is unclear whether attempted suicide needs to be reported to the police" [3].
3. It **converts a criminal-justice issue into a health-care duty**: the State must provide care.
4. **Abetment remains a crime** [1]:
   - **IPC 306:** abetment of suicide (up to 10 years);
   - **IPC 107–109:** abetment generally;
   - **IPC 108:** abetment of an abetment;
   - **IPC 116:** abetment of an offence that is not committed (up to a quarter of the maximum term).
   - In *Satvir Singh v. State of Punjab* (2001), the husband and in-laws were convicted when the woman survived her attempt [1].
5. **Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita (BNS) 2023**, in force from 1 July 2024, replaced the IPC.
   - It **contains no general offence of attempted suicide**.
   - **Section 226** criminalises attempting suicide "with intent to compel or restrain any public servant" from discharging duty, for example a protest self-immolation.
   - Abetment of suicide continues (**s108 BNS**).

### Historical and legal evolution [1, general knowledge]
| Year | Event |
| --- | --- |
| 1860 | IPC s309 (from English common law, where suicide was a felony) |
| 1912 | *Emperor v. Dwaarka Poonja* (Bombay): jumping into a well **to evade arrest**, without intent to die, is not s309 [1] |
| 1961 | *Ram Sunder Dubey v. State* (Allahabad): a **hunger strike** fell short of attempted suicide [1] |
| 1971 | **Law Commission 42nd Report** recommended repeal; the 1978 IPC (Amendment) Bill passed the Rajya Sabha but lapsed [general knowledge] |
| 1985 | *State v. Sanjay Kumar Bhatia* (Delhi HC): s309 "an anachronism unworthy of a human society like ours" [1] |
| 1987 | *Maruti Shripati Dubal v. State of Maharashtra* (Bombay HC, Justice Sawant): s309 struck down under **Article 21**. People who attempt suicide because of mental disorder need treatment, not prison [1] |
| 1994 | *P. Rathinam v. Union of India* (SC, 2 judges): s309 unconstitutional [general knowledge] |
| 1996 | *Gian Kaur v. State of Punjab* (SC Constitution Bench): **overruled Rathinam**. The "right to life" does not include a "right to die", so s309 is valid [general knowledge] |
| 2008 | **Law Commission 210th Report**: recommended effacing s309 [general knowledge] |
| 2011 | *Aruna Shanbaug v. Union of India*: passive euthanasia allowed under safeguards; active euthanasia rejected [1] |
| 2017–18 | **MHCA s115** [1] |
| 2018 | *Common Cause v. Union of India*: right to die with dignity; living wills [general knowledge] |
| 2022 | **NSPS** (MoHFW) [3] |
| 2024 | **BNS** in force, with no general attempt offence [general knowledge] |

### Arguments for decriminalisation
1. **Human rights and humaneness:** punishing someone already in despair "inflicts further suffering" [1]. It is the opposite of compassion.
2. **Public health:** criminalisation **deters help-seeking**. Families and hospitals conceal attempts, and attempts are **misclassified** as accidents. This distorts **surveillance** (NCRB data).
3. **Medical reality:**
   - about **48%** of attempters had a diagnosable psychiatric disorder (adjustment disorder 23.5%, depression 14.5%);
   - about **80% of attempts were impulsive**;
   - most were of **low lethality (68%)** and **low intent (58%)** in an Indian series [1].
   These call for treatment.
4. **Deterrence does not work:** there is no evidence that criminal sanctions reduce suicide.
5. **International trend:** decriminalised in England and Wales (1961), most of Europe and Sri Lanka (1998). WHO advocates decriminalisation [general knowledge; 3].
6. **SDG 3.4.2 and NSPS goals** require accurate data and accessible care [3].

### Arguments raised against, and responses
| Concern | Response |
| --- | --- |
| Protests and hunger strikes used to coerce the State | BNS s226 retains a narrow offence for coercive attempts [general knowledge] |
| The State's interest in preserving life | Served better by care (s115(2)) than punishment |
| Hard to prosecute abetment without the attempt being an offence | Abetment is retained as a specific offence (IPC 306 and 116 / BNS 108) [1] |
| Euthanasia "slippery slope" | Separate legal framework (*Aruna Shanbaug*; *Common Cause*) [1] |

### Clinical implications for the psychiatrist [1]
**Seven steps in a general hospital:**
1. Triage in the emergency room.
2. Medical or surgical stabilisation.
3. **Medico-legal case (MLC) registration**, and admission as needed. *Abetment, foul play and the cause of injury still need police inquiry.*
4. **Mandatory psychiatric referral:** diagnosis, **stress** (Perceived Stress Scale) and **suicidal intent** (Beck SIS).
5. **Inform the patient about s115.**
6. Inquiry by the health team and police about IPC 108/109/116 (abetment).
7. Discharge planning and follow-up.

**Use of the MHCA:**
- **s94:** emergency treatment up to **72 hours** in a general hospital, or until assessment at a mental health establishment. Also transfer to a licensed MHE.
- **s89/s90:** supported admission; the Mental Health Review Board is informed.
- Suggested: a registry and a **psychological autopsy** for in-hospital suicides [1].

**Advance directives:** an advance directive refusing treatment after a suicide attempt does **not** bar **medical** treatment. An advance directive covers mental-illness treatment only [1].

### Challenges in implementation
- Police and hospital confusion: MLC formalities persist [3].
- **Resources:** medical costs of attempts were estimated at about **Rs 14,545 crore** (2016). The total health allocation for 2018–19 was about Rs 52,000 crore [1].
- Lack of awareness among the public, police and doctors.
- **Stigma** persists despite the law.
- Unclear interpretation: who rebuts the presumption, and how?

**Conclusion.** Section 115 MHCA, and now the BNS, moved India from **punishing to caring for** people who attempt suicide. The presumption of severe stress places a positive **duty of care, treatment and rehabilitation** on the State. Abetment remains punishable. Turning the law into fewer deaths needs police sensitisation, routine psychiatric assessment of every attempter, follow-up services and better surveillance. These are the aims of the **National Suicide Prevention Strategy (2022)** [1, 3].

## In depth

### Background: why the law mattered
- **Scale [1, 3]:**
  - India recorded **230,314 suicides in 2016** (GBD estimate) [1];
  - NCRB recorded **over 1 lakh deaths a year**, with a rate rising from **10.2 to 11.3 per 100,000** (2010s–2020) [3];
  - India accounts for **36.6% of global female** and **24.3% of male** suicide deaths [3].
- **Attempts:** about **25 attempts per suicide**, which would mean about **5.75 million attempts in 2016** [1].
  - About 17% are "severe", leading to incapacity [1].
- **Criminalisation led to under-reporting:**
  - private hospitals refused to treat attempters without police involvement;
  - attempts were recorded as "accidental poisoning";
  - poor families faced harassment.

### Epidemiology of attempts in India [1]
- Methods: pesticides 50%, drug overdose 35%, hanging 10%, others 5% (Kumar et al.).
- Precipitants: marital conflict, family stress, low socioeconomic status, finances, **alcoholism in the spouse**.
- **Recurrence about 10%**, which supports follow-up (WHO SUPRE-MISS) [1].

### Classification: what "attempt" means legally and clinically
- **Clinical:** suicide attempt = a non-fatal self-injurious act **with intent to die**. **Non-suicidal self-injury** has no intent to die. Both can occur in the same person [1].
- **Legal (historical s309):** required intent plus an act towards death.
  - Accidental injury does not qualify (*Dwaarka Poonja*).
  - A hunger strike falls short unless death is imminent (*Ram Sunder*).
- **MHCA s115:** presumes **"severe stress"**. This is a **non-diagnostic** construct and **not** synonymous with "mental illness" as defined in s2(s).

### Mechanism of change: how decriminalisation is expected to reduce suicide
1. **Help-seeking** rises when the fear of police goes.
2. **Data quality** improves, which allows targeted prevention.
3. **Statutory duty of care (s115(2))** forces service development: crisis services, follow-up, **brief contact interventions** (WHO BIC trial).
4. **Stigma reduction:** suicidality is reframed as distress, not crime.

### Clinical features relevant to the psychiatrist's assessment
- **Intent:** Beck Suicide Intent Scale (circumstances, planning, precautions against discovery, note, communication, expected lethality).
- **Lethality**, **method** and **access to means**.
- **Psychiatric disorder:** depression, substance use, psychosis, personality disorder.
- **Stressors:** domestic violence, debt, academic pressure.
- **Abetment indicators** (dowry harassment, cruelty under IPC 498A / BNS 85–86). These must be documented carefully because police inquiry may follow.

| Domain | Record |

| Medico-legal | MLC number, police intimation as per hospital policy, history in the patient's words |

### Differential: decriminalisation vs related legal concepts
| Concept | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Decriminalisation of attempt | No punishment for the person who survives |
| Abetment of suicide | Still a crime (IPC 306 / BNS 108) |
| Passive euthanasia | Withdrawal of life support under safeguards (*Aruna Shanbaug* 2011; *Common Cause* 2018) |
| Active euthanasia / physician-assisted suicide | **Illegal** in India |

### Management details: the service response the law demands
- **Emergency:**
  - pesticide poisoning protocols;
  - mandatory psychiatric liaison for every attempter;
  - risk reassessment hourly, then 2-hourly, decreasing with risk [1].
- **Discharge [1]:**
  - psychiatric assessment;
  - treatment of comorbidity;
  - contingency supports for stressors;
  - psychoeducation on alcohol and securing lethal substances;
  - a support person at the destination;
  - a 24-hour contact number;
  - a scheduled follow-up.
- **Rehabilitation [1]:**
  - medical, surgical and brief psychological interventions (including cosmetic treatment of burns);
  - occupational, family and social reintegration.
- **Public health (NSPS) [3]:**
  - pesticide regulation;
  - responsible media reporting;
  - gatekeeper training;
  - school mental health curricula;
  - surveillance.

### Critical appraisal
- **"Severe stress" is undefined** in the Act, which creates legal ambiguity about how it is "proved otherwise".
- **Not a true repeal:** s309 remained until the BNS. Lower courts and police were inconsistent.
- **Resource gap:** a duty of care without funding risks tokenism [1].
- **Evidence gap:** there are no Indian data yet showing that s115 changed help-seeking or reporting.
- **BNS s226** preserves criminalisation for coercive attempts. This may still deter some distressed protesters (for example, farmers).

### Indian context
- **NSPS 2022:**
  - first national strategy;
  - "REDS" priority areas;
  - targets: surveillance within 3 years, DMHP psychiatric OPDs with suicide-prevention services within 5 years, curriculum within 8 years [3].
- **Tele-MANAS (14416)** for crisis support [general knowledge].
- **Kiran helpline (2020)** [general knowledge].
- **Pesticide bans** have been driven by state-level action, for example Kerala and Punjab; they need consolidation [general knowledge].

### Recent advances
- **BNS 2023** (in force 2024): general offence removed [general knowledge; verify].
- WHO **LIVE LIFE** implementation guide (2021) adopted in the NSPS [3].
- Debates on **psychological autopsy registries** and **suicide surveillance** through IHIP [3].
