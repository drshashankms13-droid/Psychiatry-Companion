## Exam answer

**Terminology first.** In Indian and US usage, "learning disability" means **specific learning disability (SLD)**. In UK usage it means **intellectual disability**. This answer addresses SLD, as the RPwD Act uses the term; state this assumption in the first line of the exam answer.

**Definition.** **Specific learning disability** is a **neurodevelopmental disorder**. It involves persistent, **unexpected** difficulty in acquiring academic skills (reading, written expression, spelling, arithmetic) that is:
- substantially below expectation for age and general intelligence;
- present from the early school years;
- **not** explained by intellectual disability, uncorrected visual or hearing impairment, neurological disorder, psychosocial adversity, lack of proficiency in the language of instruction, or inadequate schooling [1, 2].

**Subtypes:**
- **Dyslexia** (reading; the commonest).
- **Dysgraphia** (written expression).
- **Dyscalculia** (mathematics) [1].

**Nomenclature:**
- ICD-10: specific developmental disorders of scholastic skills (SDDSS).
- **ICD-11:** **developmental learning disorder** (with impairment in reading, written expression or mathematics).
- **DSM-5-TR:** **specific learning disorder** (with specifiers) [2].

### Mechanism
Genetic (heritable) and neurobiological disruption of the **left parieto-temporal and occipito-temporal reading network** → core deficit in **phonological processing** (splitting words into phonemes and linking sounds to symbols) → slow, inaccurate decoding and spelling. Compensatory right-hemisphere activation occurs with effective intervention [1].

Two further points:
- **Transparent Indian scripts** are easier to learn than **opaque English**, but dyslexia still occurs in Indian languages [1].
- SLD is a **persistent deficit, not a developmental lag** [1].

### Presentations: why every child seen by a psychiatrist should be screened

| Direct | Indirect (secondary consequences) |
| --- | --- |
| Scholastic backwardness, especially in languages | Poor motivation, avoids homework |
| "Forgetful, knows orally but cannot write" | Somatic complaints (headache), dissociative symptoms |
| Poor handwriting, slow reading and writing, poor spelling | Externalising: ODD, CD, lying, forging, truancy, stealing |
| Falling grades in higher classes as demands increase | Internalising: anxiety, exam phobia, depression, low self-esteem |
| — | School refusal |

Sources: [1].

A typical cascade: dyslexia → poor grades → punishment at home and school → lying and forging → deviant peers → conduct disorder [1].

### Assessment: steps

**1. Clinical history** [1]:
- Who referred, and why.
- Complaints: onset, evolution, severity.
- **Schooling history:** progress over time; changes of school, board or medium; school reports and notebooks.
- Developmental history: language delay; fine-motor, sequencing and left–right difficulties.
- Family history.
- Daily schedule, including tuition hours.
- **Parenting practices:** criticism, comparison, punishment, academic pressure.
- Strengths, talents and interests.
- Previous interventions.

**2. Interview format:** see the child and parents together, then the child alone, then the parents. Give the child a writing or drawing task while you talk to the parents [1].

**3. Quick office assessment:**
- **General intelligence:** fund of information, reasoning, vocabulary, concepts of time and rules.
- **Scholastic skills:** reading aloud, **writing to dictation**, spelling, mental arithmetic.
- **Look for:** reversals (b/d), inversions (u/n), mirror writing, omissions, substitutions, guessing, poor punctuation and spacing [1].

**4. Rule out alternative explanations:**
- Vision and hearing tests.
- Neurological examination.
- Intellectual disability or **borderline intellectual functioning ("slow learner")**.
- Inadequate schooling or absence.
- Language of instruction.
- Emotional disorders [1, 2].

**5. Psychometric assessment** (needed for milder cases and for certification) [1, 2]:
- **IQ test:** MISIC, WISC, or Binet-Kamat, with Indian norms.
- **NIMHANS Index for SLD** (Kapur et al.): for ages 5–12; covers attention, visual and auditory processing, reading, writing, spelling and arithmetic.
- **Educational assessment:** grade-level achievement; curriculum-based or criterion-referenced tests.

**6. Comorbidity** (present in 43% at intake in a NIMHANS study) [1]:
- **ADHD:** 20%. Diagnose only if symptoms are pervasive, not just at study time.
- Internalising disorders: 13%.
- ODD/CD, anxiety, depression, school phobia.
- Developmental coordination disorder, speech and language disorders.

**7. Formulation:** Rutter's multiaxial framework [1]:
- I: clinical syndrome.
- II: specific developmental delay.
- III: intellectual level.
- IV: medical conditions.
- V: abnormal psychosocial situation.

**8. Certification under the RPwD Act 2016** (after a teacher screen at class 3 or age 8) [2]:
- Paediatrician: vision, hearing and neurological examination.
- Psychiatrist: rule out comorbidities and mimics (emotional and behavioural disorders) [6].
- Psychologist: **IQ ≥85** (2024 guidelines: MISIC, **WISC-IV**, **NIEPID Indian Test of Intelligence**, BKT or VSMS) [6].
- **NIMHANS battery** for SLD.
- **NIMHANS battery or the Grade Level Assessment Device (GLAD)**; SLD is diagnosed when the child functions **3 SD below the current class**, with normal vision and hearing, no mimicking emotional or behavioural disorder, and adequate opportunity to learn [6].
- Certification from age 8; repeat in Class X and Class XII if needed; lifelong after 18. The board includes a psychiatrist or child psychiatrist [6].
- This allows board exemptions and concessions.

### Management linked to assessment
- **Parent counselling:** the child is not lazy; avoid ridicule and punishment; build strengths [1].
- **Child counselling:** "it is not your fault" [1].
- **Remediation** by a special educator: structured, sequential, cumulative, thorough, multisensory, phonologically based, with over-learning [1].
- **School accommodations:** extra time, a scribe, calculator, exemption from a second or third language, NIOS [1, 2].
- **Treat comorbidity,** e.g., methylphenidate for ADHD.

**Conclusion.** Assessing SLD means establishing an **unexpected, specific and persistent** academic deficit and excluding intellectual, sensory, neurological, educational and linguistic explanations. Assessment combines history, office tests, standardised IQ and achievement testing, and a comorbidity screen. Clinical judgement must stay central: rigid, test-bound certification under the RPwD Act can wrongly deny children support, especially those in vernacular-medium schools or above 12 years of age.

## In depth

### Background
- **1896:** Morgan reported "congenital word blindness" (BMJ) [1].
- **1960s:** Samuel Kirk (University of Illinois) introduced the term "learning disability", promoted by parent advocacy groups [1].
- **1975:** the **Isle of Wight studies** (Rutter & Yule) separated **specific reading retardation** (unexpected, given IQ) from general reading backwardness [1].
- **Neurobiology:** the Shaywitz studies (Connecticut Longitudinal Study; fMRI) established the persistence and neurobiology of dyslexia [1].
- **India:** the NIMHANS Index for SLD (Kapur et al., 1992). **SLD became a notified disability in the RPwD Act 2016**, with certification guidelines in 2018 [1, 2].

### Epidemiology
- **Prevalence:** 5–17.5% of school-age children (Shaywitz 1998). Reading disorder is the commonest type [1].
- **India:** scholastic backwardness in 9.4% (Srinath et al. 2005, Bengaluru) [1]. Indian school-based SLD estimates commonly fall between 3% and 15%, depending on the method.
- **Persistence:** about **74%** of children who are reading-disabled in grade 3 still read below grade level in grade 9 [1].
- **Outcome predictors:** severity, IQ, age at start of remediation, emotional comorbidity, socio-economic status and parental education, family and school support [1].

### Classification and criteria

| System | Name | Key requirements |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ICD-10 (SDDSS) | F81.0 reading, F81.1 spelling, F81.2 arithmetic, F81.3 mixed | Clinically significant impairment (expected in fewer than 3% of schoolchildren); not explained by intellectual level (individually administered achievement and IQ tests); developmental (present from early schooling); not due to external factors or uncorrected sensory impairment [2] |
| ICD-11 | Developmental learning disorder (6A03) with impairment in reading / written expression / mathematics / other | Markedly below expected for age and intellectual level; significant impairment; excludes intellectual disability, sensory, neurological, lack of opportunity, language of instruction, psychosocial adversity. **Psychiatric disorders are not an exclusion** [2] |
| DSM-5-TR | Specific learning disorder (reading, written expression, mathematics specifiers; mild/moderate/severe) | (A) difficulty for at least **6 months despite targeted intervention**; (B) skills substantially below age, usually **≥1.5 SD below the mean (below the 7th percentile)**, or a more lenient 1.0–2.5 SD with converging evidence; (C) begins in school years; (D) not better explained by other conditions [2] |
| RPwD Act 2016 | Specific learning disabilities | "A heterogeneous group of conditions wherein there is a deficit in processing language, spoken or written…", including dyslexia, dysgraphia, dyscalculia, dyspraxia and developmental aphasia [2] |

**Key conceptual shift:** DSM-5 dropped the strict **IQ–achievement discrepancy** in favour of **response to intervention** plus low achievement. ICD keeps a discrepancy-based framing [2].

### Mechanism in detail
- **Genetics:** heritability is about 40–70%. Candidate loci include DYX1C1, KIAA0319 and DCDC2.
- **Neuroimaging:** under-activation of the left temporo-parietal and left occipito-temporal ("visual word form area") regions; compensatory frontal and right-hemisphere activity, which normalises partly after effective phonological intervention [1].
- **Cognitive theories:** phonological deficit (dominant); rapid automatised naming (double-deficit); magnocellular or visual; cerebellar or automatisation; working memory.
- **Dyscalculia:** intraparietal sulcus and "number sense" deficits.

### Clinical features in detail: common error types
- **Reading:** slow and hesitant; omissions, substitutions, guessing; reversals ("on" for "no"); misreading ("put" for "but") [1].
- **Writing:** slow; ignores capitals and full stops; poor handwriting and use of space; poorly formed letters [1].
- **Spelling:** letter-order errors, **reversals** (b/d), **inversions** (u/n), **mirror writing**, omissions ("wet" for "went"). These are best elicited by dictation [1].
- **Arithmetic:** errors in mental and written sums, place value, sequencing and tables [1].
- **Associated features:** fine-motor and left–right difficulties, mixed handedness, poor sequencing [1].

### Assessment in detail

| Component | Tool / method | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Screening | Teacher checklist (class 3 / age 8, RPwD guideline); Dyslexia Early Screening Test | Teacher screen also assesses parental motivation, a practice with moral connotations for first-generation learners [2] |
| Vision, hearing, neurological | Paediatrician; audiometry, ophthalmology | Mandatory first step [2] |
| Intelligence | **MISIC** (1969, never revised), **WISC-III** (no Indian norms) as notified; also WISC-IV (Indian norms), Binet-Kamat, Stanford-Binet, progressive matrices | IQ ≥85 required by the gazette. Verbal scales penalise language deficits; UK norms underestimate Indian children [2] |
| Achievement | **NIMHANS Index for SLD** (ages 5–12; English and maths); curriculum-based or criterion-referenced tests (allowed under DSM-5) | Not valid for vernacular languages or after age 13 [2] |
| Processing | Phonological awareness, rapid naming, working memory, visual-motor (Bender-Gestalt), attention tests | Profile for remediation |
| Comorbidity | Conners/Vanderbilt (ADHD), CBCL, SDQ, anxiety and depression scales | ADHD in about 20% [1] |
| Function | School, peer and family impact | DSM requires interference |
| Severity | Mild / moderate / severe (DSM-5-TR); RPwD severity indicators | Needed for certification [2] |

**Timing:** start early, and treat at-risk children before a formal diagnosis. Signs of risk include delayed language, slow vocabulary growth, difficulty rhyming, poor memory for the alphabet, and poor concentration. Weigh early help against the effects of labelling [1].

### Differential diagnosis

| Condition | Distinguishing point |
| --- | --- |
| Intellectual disability | Global deficits, IQ below 70, adaptive impairment |
| Borderline intellectual functioning ("slow learner") | IQ 71–84; uniform low achievement, not unexpected [2] |
| ADHD alone | Attention problems across settings; academic skills adequate when attending |
| Sensory impairment | Vision or hearing tests |
| Inadequate schooling, absenteeism, change of medium | Educational history [1, 2] |
| Emotional disorder (depression, anxiety) | Recent decline with mood symptoms; remits with treatment |
| Language disorder | Oral language also impaired |
| Neurological disorder (epilepsy, absence seizures, acquired brain injury) | History, EEG, examination |
| Autism spectrum disorder | Social communication deficits |

### Management details
- **Remediation principles:** sympathetic and at the child's pace; **structured, sequential, cumulative, thorough**; **over-learning**; **multisensory** (seeing, hearing and touch, e.g., Orton-Gillingham approaches); **phonics** (sound–symbol correspondence, e.g., /ar/ → /tar/) then orthographic processing; behavioural rewards and contracting; flash cards, games and puzzles [1].
- **Parental mal-adaptations to address:** comparison, excessive tuition, studying at odd hours, punishment, public criticism, blame, and "quick fixes" such as bribing for promotion. Parents often go through denial, shock, bargaining and blame; support them through these phases with repeated explanation and parent groups [1].
- **School:** teacher understanding and small achievable tasks with praise [1].
- **Educational decisions:** consider age, severity, the gap between class and ability, medium and syllabus, and the availability and cost of services [1].
  - Options: stay in a regular school with remedial input; move to an SLD-aware school; enrol in **NIOS** (flexible pace and subjects) [1].
  - **Board concessions:** CBSE has explicit provisions; ICSE decides case by case; Karnataka exempts second and third languages at SSLC; other states have their own orders. Concessions include extra time, a scribe and a calculator [1, 2].
- **Comorbid ADHD:** stimulant or atomoxetine improves attention, not reading itself.
- **Adolescents and adults:** accommodations and technology (text-to-speech, spell-checkers); career guidance.

### Evidence table

| Study | Design | Result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Rutter & Yule 1975 (Isle of Wight) | Epidemiological | Specific reading retardation distinct from general backwardness [1] |
| Shaywitz et al. 1990, 1999 (Connecticut Longitudinal Study) | Prospective cohort | Dyslexia equally common in boys and girls in population samples; persistent into adolescence (74%) [1] |
| Shaywitz & Shaywitz 2005 | Review / fMRI | Left posterior network disruption; compensation with intervention [1] |
| Kumari 2002 (NIMHANS) | Follow-up of children with SLD | 43% comorbidity at intake; ADHD 20%; internalising 13%; outcome predictors [1] |
| Panicker et al. 2006 | Comparison of MISIC and WISC-III (n=300) | Indian children score lower on UK norms, so they risk being misclassified as below average [2] |
| Panicker et al. 2015 | Validation | NIMHANS Index valid for ages 5–12 [2] |

### IPS Clinical Practice Guideline (Shah, Sagar, Somaiya, Nagpal 2019): key points [7]
- **Prevalence:** **2–10%** (Sahoo 2015).
- **Comorbidity:**
  - behavioural and emotional disorders about 30%;
  - **ADHD 10–60%**; inattention 20–40%;
  - conduct disorder 6%;
  - depression about 33%;
  - anxiety 20–30%;
  - language disorders common (30–40% of SLD have a reading disorder).
- **DSM-5 severity:**
  - **mild:** compensates with accommodations;
  - **moderate:** unlikely to become proficient without intensive teaching;
  - **severe:** needs intensive individualised teaching for most school years.
- **IQ testing:** MISIC, Binet–Kamat, WISC-IV.
  - In SLD, **performance IQ is often higher than verbal IQ**.
  - The **ACID profile** (low Arithmetic, Coding, Information, Digit span) may be seen.
- **Achievement testing:**
  - **NIMHANS Index for SLD:** Level I (5–7 years: pre-academic skills) and Level II (8–12 years);
  - WRAT; TOWL;
  - **GLAD** (NIEPID; from 6 years, grades I–IV).
- **Exclusions before diagnosis include:**
  - ID; sensory impairment; neurological disorders;
  - psychiatric disorders;
  - **discrepancy between mother tongue and medium of schooling**;
  - inadequate schooling; **first-generation learners** with poor support.
- **Management tiers:**
  1. **Accommodations:** grippers, tactile paper, spell-checkers, audio books, extra time, not reading aloud.
  2. **Modifications:** dropping a language, lower-level mathematics.
  3. **Remedial education:** usually **2–3 times a week for 3–4 years**; focus shifts with grade from basic skills to critical thinking.
- **Multidisciplinary team:** psychologist, special educator, occupational therapist, speech therapist, paediatrician. The psychiatrist is often the first contact who suspects and screens for SLD and treats comorbidity.
- **Response to Intervention (RTI)** model: start help before failure. This could be integrated with Sarva Shiksha Abhiyan.
- **Rights:** SLD is a **benchmark disability under RPwD Act 2016**, with duties on government and local authorities.

### Critical appraisal
- **Construct debate:** SLD is "one of the least understood and most debated disabling conditions" (Lyon 1996). There is **no natural cut-point**, because academic skills are continuously distributed [2].
- **Discrepancy vs response-to-intervention:** IQ discrepancy has poor validity, and verbal IQ is depressed by the language deficit itself [2].
- **RPwD certification problems** (Kishore et al. 2021, written about the 2018 guidelines) [2]. The **2024 revision** [6] addressed some of these: it now allows WISC-IV and the NIEPID test, adds GLAD as an alternative achievement tool, makes the psychiatrist's role explicit, and says the tools apply at all ages until new scales are validated. The fixed IQ ≥85 threshold and the lack of vernacular tools remain:
  - Mandated tests (MISIC, unrevised since 1969; WISC-III, no Indian norms) are outdated.
  - The fixed **IQ ≥85** threshold excludes children with lower IQ caused by the test's verbal load.
  - The NIMHANS Index is limited to ages 5–12, English and maths; there are **no vernacular-language tools**.
  - Reassessment at 18 is required, but child IQ tests no longer apply.
  - Clinical judgement has no place in the process.
  - Boards differ in their requirements (CBSE, ICSE, NIOS and state boards).
  - Assessing "parental motivation" is morally loaded.
- **Over-identification risk:** borderline intellectual functioning may be misdiagnosed as SLD to obtain concessions [2].
- **Proposed solutions** [2]:
  - Free choice of any standardised IQ test with Indian norms (WISC-IV, Binet-Kamat, progressive matrices).
  - Curriculum-based criterion-referenced tests.
  - New tools for 13–16-year-olds (an ICSSR-funded NIMHANS tool), covering cognitive domains (planning, attention, simultaneous and successive processing) and academic domains drawn from multiple boards.
  - Screening forms for teachers and professionals, with severity indicators.

### Indian context
- **RPwD Act 2016:**
  - SLD is a **specified disability**.
  - Children with **benchmark disability** have the right to free education (Section 31).
  - Section 16 places duties on educational institutions: reasonable accommodation, monitoring, and suitable modifications in exams.
  - Reservation: 1% of posts is shared with autism, intellectual disability and mental illness [2].
- **Certification flow (2024 guidelines):** school screening committee (headed by the principal) → paediatrician (vision, hearing, neurological) → psychiatrist (comorbidity and mimics) → psychologist (IQ ≥85) → NIMHANS battery or GLAD → Medical Board certificate [6]. Psychiatrists were first added to the board through the 2020 amendment [2].
- **NEP 2020** emphasises early identification and support for children with SLD.
- **Public awareness:** the film *Taare Zameen Par* (2007) raised awareness. A psychiatric commentary is in the *Indian Journal of Psychiatry* (Rao & Krishna 2008) [1].
- **Resources:** Maharashtra Dyslexia Association; Madras Dyslexia Association; NIMHANS Child and Adolescent Psychiatry and Clinical Psychology departments [1].

### Recent advances
- **Established:** DSM-5 shift to a single SLD category with specifiers and response to intervention; ICD-11 "developmental learning disorder"; RPwD certification guidelines (2018, amended 2020) [2].
- **Emerging:**
  - Vernacular and adolescent assessment tools under development (NIMHANS/ICSSR) [2].
  - Digital screening apps.
  - Eye-tracking and machine-learning screening (investigational).
  - Early-literacy prevention programmes in schools.
