## Exam answer

**Introduction [1].**
- BPD is **common in psychiatric settings**: about **2%** of the community, over **10%** of community mental-health patients, **20%** of inpatients.
- **Comorbidity is high:** unipolar and bipolar mood disorders, anxiety disorders, eating disorders, **PTSD** and **substance use disorders**.
- **More than 75% attempt suicide**, and **2–5% die by suicide**.

### Principles of management
1. **Psychological treatment is the core.** Therapies such as **DBT** "have garnered much better evidence". A 2020 Cochrane review noted **75 RCTs** of psychological treatments; a 2023 network meta-analysis included 43 studies [1].
2. **Medication has a limited, adjunctive role.** **No medicine is licensed** for BPD or any aspect of it [1].
3. **Treat comorbid conditions first and fully**, using usual guidance "irrespective of any coexisting BPD diagnosis", because they affect treatment of BPD [1].
4. **Manage suicide risk** continuously [1, 2].
5. **Trauma-informed approach**, given frequent trauma histories and comorbid PTSD [1, 4].

### Assessment
- **Diagnosis and comorbidity:** mood, anxiety, eating, PTSD and substance use [1].
- **Suicide and self-harm risk assessment [2]:**
  - **clinical inquiry is the most important instrument**. "Standardised risk scales are **not predictive** of risk";
  - assess **aggression, impulsiveness, hopelessness**, anxiety;
  - past suicidal behaviour (precipitants, intent, medical severity);
  - past treatment and hospitalisations;
  - family history of suicide and of family conflict, domestic violence and sexual abuse;
  - psychosocial stressors;
  - coping skills and protective factors.
  - **Ask directly and graduated:** "Have you ever felt that life was not worth living?" through to plans and preparations. Asking does not plant the idea; it may bring relief.
  - **Scales as aids:** Beck Suicide Intent Scale, Beck Hopelessness Scale, SAD PERSONS, C-SSRS, Reasons for Living.
- **Trauma history:** ask in private, normalise the inquiry, and do not force full disclosure. Ask what to record [4].

### Psychological treatment
- **First-line, evidence-based core** [1].
- **DBT** has the best-known evidence, and may also have **epigenetic effects** on stress- and emotion-regulation genes [5].
- **Combination:** systematic reviews conclude that evidence does not support **medicines alone**, but **with psychotherapy** "significant improvements in mood and behaviour can be anticipated" [1].

### Pharmacological treatment [1]
**NICE (2009):**
- Drug treatment **should not be used routinely** for BPD or its individual symptoms (repeated self-harm, emotional instability, risk-taking, transient psychotic symptoms).
- Drugs may be used for **comorbid conditions**.
- **Short-term sedation in a crisis, no longer than 1 week**, agreed with the patient.
- The NICE guideline is under review following ICD-11's single, severity-based personality-disorder category.

**Evidence:** a **2022 Cochrane review** found medication has **little or no effect** on symptom severity, self-harm, suicide-related outcomes or functioning, but may slightly reduce **interpersonal problems** (very low or low certainty).

**Current practice:** over **90%** of patients with BPD in England had been prescribed psychotropics, often in **polypharmacy**, frequently **without a documented comorbid diagnosis**.

| Class | Evidence (Maudsley) |
|---|---|
| **SGAs** | No overall improvement in BPD or functioning. **Aripiprazole for anger**; **quetiapine for aggression** (the most widely used; modest gains, not for impulsivity). **Olanzapine** has the best-supported effect on some symptoms, depression and anxiety, but **not self-harm or aggression**; metabolic risk makes it a **last resort** |
| **Clozapine** | For **severe treatment-refractory BPD**, high suicide risk and frequent admissions: improves psychotic symptoms, aggression, impulsivity, self-harm, functioning; less hospital use |
| **Antidepressants** | **Weak** evidence, modest benefit at best. Fluoxetine may improve impulsivity, self-harm, anger and mood instability, but one RCT showed **more suicide attempts** when fluoxetine was added to DBT or supportive therapy. Duloxetine and venlafaxine: small studies. Bupropion: fewer readmissions (database study) |
| **Mood stabilisers** | **Valproate and topiramate** may improve anger; valproate aggression and irritability. **Never valproate in people of child-bearing age.** Gabapentin: anxiety, affective instability. **Lamotrigine: no effect** in a large RCT. Carbamazepine ineffective. Lithium is licensed for aggression and self-harm but use has declined (toxicity) |
| **Stimulants** | Fewer hospitalisations and deaths (database study), probably reflecting comorbid **ADHD** |
| **Others** | Memantine (one RCT); naltrexone for dissociation (very limited); ketamine (mood-related); **omega-3 fatty acids** (meta-analysis: impulsivity, affective dysregulation; an add-on option) |

### Crisis management [1, 2]
- Symptoms **fluctuate**, so drugs may be needed **intermittently**. Each prescription should be a **rigorously evaluated short-term trial** [1].
- **Choice of sedative** [1]:
  - avoid **benzodiazepines**, especially short-acting ones, which cause **disinhibition** in BPD;
  - sedative antipsychotics cause EPS and weight gain;
  - **TCAs are toxic in overdose**;
  - **promethazine 25–50 mg** is usually well tolerated short term, within a coordinated care plan (no trial evidence).
- **Hospitalisation** depends on the patient's state, **family support** and access to outpatient care [2].
- **Acute crisis care:** reduce anxiety, insomnia and depression; **collaborate with family** (with attention to confidentiality); take anti-suicidal measures [2].

### After a suicide attempt (MHCA 2017) [3]
- **s115:** a person who attempts suicide is presumed to have **severe stress** and is **not to be tried or punished**.
- The Government must provide **care, treatment and rehabilitation**.
- **Clinical pathway:** triage, stabilise, medico-legal registration, **mandatory psychiatric referral**, discharge with follow-up.

### Trauma-informed care [4]
- **SAMHSA principles:** safety; trustworthiness and transparency; peer support; collaboration and mutuality; empowerment; attention to cultural, historical and gender issues. **Establishing safety is key.**
- **Resist re-traumatisation.**
- If a patient **dissociates**, stop the procedure, reorient them, stay with them, and use grounding such as square breathing.

**Conclusion.** BPD management rests on:
1. **psychological therapy** (DBT and others), which has far stronger evidence than drugs;
2. **treating comorbid** mood, anxiety, PTSD, eating and substance disorders;
3. **careful, repeated suicide-risk assessment** by clinical inquiry;
4. **trauma-informed care**;
5. **minimal, targeted, time-limited** medication: short sedation in a crisis, clozapine for severe refractory cases, and avoiding benzodiazepines and polypharmacy.

## In depth

### Why prescribing for BPD is hazardous [1]
- **Polypharmacy** is common and the evidence is weak.
- Overdose toxicity matters given the high suicide-attempt rate (TCAs).
- **Benzodiazepine disinhibition.**
- **Metabolic harm** (olanzapine), **teratogenicity** (valproate), long-term **lithium** toxicity.
- Possible **increase in suicide attempts** with fluoxetine added to psychotherapy.

### Lifetime suicide rates by disorder (Harris and Barraclough; seminar) [2]

|---|---|
