## Exam answer

**Definition.** **Insomnia disorder** (ICSD-3-TR, ICD-11, DSM-5-TR) has four elements [1]:
- A complaint of **difficulty initiating sleep, maintaining sleep, or early morning awakening**.
- **At least one daytime consequence:** fatigue, poor concentration, irritability, mood disturbance, headache, loss of interest. In children: irritability, agitation, hyperactivity.
- **Adequate opportunity and circumstances for sleep.**
- Not solely explained by another sleep, medical or mental disorder or substance.

**Duration:**
- **Chronic insomnia:** at least 3 nights per week for at least 3 months.
- **Short-term insomnia:** less than 3 months [1].

The "secondary insomnia" concept is replaced by **comorbid insomnia**. Insomnia can become independent of its trigger, so it often needs treatment in its own right [1].

### Mechanism: Spielman's 3P model (1987) [1]

| Predisposing | Precipitating | Perpetuating |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Anxious, hyperaroused temperament; past trauma; family history; 5-HTTLPR long-arm polymorphism | Life stress, illness, bereavement, shift or social changes, medical conditions | **Conditioned arousal** to the bed; worry about sleep; **extending time in bed**, napping, irregular schedule; caffeine or alcohol as coping |

Perlis's **neurocognitive model** adds the next layer. Cortical and physiological **hyperarousal** (HPA axis, ↑ orexin, histamine and cortisol) → increased sensory processing at sleep onset → reduced "mesograde amnesia" of sleep, so time awake feels longer → attentional bias to sleep threats → dysfunctional beliefs and safety behaviours → chronic insomnia [1].

### Assessment
1. **Sleep history:** sleep-onset latency, number and length of awakenings, early waking, total sleep time, weekday vs weekend, naps, sleep quality, daytime impact [1].
2. **24-hour pattern and the "holiday test":** if the patient sleeps normally when allowed to choose their own times, consider **delayed** (difficulty falling asleep) or **advanced** (early waking) **sleep–wake phase disorder** [1].
3. **Screen for other sleep disorders:**
   - snoring, witnessed apnoeas or gasping (**OSA**: STOP-BANG, Berlin);
   - leg discomfort with an urge to move (**RLS**);
   - parasomnias, seizures;
   - shift work [1].
4. **Psychiatric:** depression (insomnia in about **70%**), anxiety, PTSD (nightmares), mania (reduced need for sleep), psychosis, dementia, substance use [1].
5. **Medical:** pain, COPD or asthma, heart failure (orthopnoea, PND), nocturia (BPH, diabetes), hyperthyroidism, GERD, Parkinson's disease [1].
6. **Drugs:** caffeine after noon, nicotine, alcohol (fragments sleep), SSRIs/SNRIs, steroids, β-agonists, stimulants; hypnotic dependence [1].
7. **Sleep environment** (light, noise, temperature, bed partner, children or pets) and **sleep hygiene** [1].
8. **Tools** [1]:
   - **Sleep diary** for 2 weeks: latency, efficiency, behaviours (C = caffeine, M = medication, A = alcohol, E = exercise).
   - **Insomnia Severity Index** (0–28; ≥15 moderate–severe); PSQI (>5 poor sleep); Athens Insomnia Scale.
   - **DBAS-16** (dysfunctional beliefs); **FIRST** (stress vulnerability).
   - ESS to exclude excessive sleepiness.
9. **Objective tests:** **actigraphy** for patterns. **Polysomnography is not routine.** It is indicated when OSA, PLMD or a parasomnia is suspected, when treatment fails, or to identify **paradoxical insomnia** (sleep-state misperception) [1].

### Management
**Step 1. Treat comorbid conditions and remove precipitants:** drugs, caffeine, alcohol, pain, OSA, depression [1].

**Step 2. CBT-I: first-line** for chronic insomnia, including comorbid insomnia [1, 2]:

| Component | Core instructions |
| --- | --- |
| **Stimulus control** | Bed only when sleepy; if not asleep in about 10–20 minutes, get up and return only when sleepy; bed for sleep (and sex) only; fixed wake time |
| **Sleep restriction** | Time in bed = average actual sleep time (minimum about 5 hours); no naps; fixed wake time; extend by 15–30 minutes when sleep efficiency is ≥85%; reduce if <85% after about 10 days |
| **Relaxation** | Progressive muscle relaxation, guided imagery, breathing |
| **Cognitive therapy** | Challenge beliefs (e.g., "I must get 8 hours", "I can't function", "sleep is uncontrollable"); reduce clock-watching and catastrophising |
| **Sleep hygiene** | Necessary but not sufficient on its own |

Delivered over 4–8 sessions, in groups, or **digitally (dCBT-I, e.g., Sleepio/SleepioRx)** [1, 2].

**Step 3. Pharmacotherapy** when CBT-I fails, is unavailable, or for short-term insomnia (BAP evidence grades) [1, 2]:
- **Z-drugs** (zolpidem 5–10 mg, zopiclone 3.75–7.5 mg) and **short-acting benzodiazepines** (grade A): use the **lowest dose for the shortest time** (2–4 weeks; intermittent dosing). Risks: tolerance, dependence, falls, cognitive impairment, complex sleep behaviours.
- **Prolonged-release melatonin 2 mg** (licensed for those **≥55 years**, grade B).
- **Low-dose doxepin** (3–6 mg; sleep maintenance).
- **Dual orexin receptor antagonists** (suvorexant, lemborexant, daridorexant): little rebound or dependence; check Indian availability.
- **Sedating antidepressants** (trazodone, mirtazapine, low-dose amitriptyline): when depression co-exists.
- **Avoid** antipsychotics such as quetiapine (grade D; metabolic risk) and OTC antihistamines (anticholinergic, hangover) for insomnia alone.

**Step 4. Special groups:**
- **Elderly:** CBT-I, melatonin; avoid benzodiazepines (falls, cognition).
- **Pregnancy:** CBT-I first.
- **Substance use:** avoid benzodiazepines.
- **Long-term hypnotic users:** gradual taper combined with CBT-I.

### Key evidence
- **CBT-I** is as effective as hypnotics in the short term and **more durable** long term (Morin 1999 RCT; Trauer 2015 meta-analysis). It is recommended first-line by the ACP (2016), the BAP and European guidelines [2].
- **Digital CBT-I** is effective and scalable (Espie 2019; SleepioRx is FDA-cleared) [1].
- **Hypnotics:** effective short-term; harms rise with age and duration (Glass 2005: number needed to harm about 6 for adverse events in older adults) [2].

**Conclusion.** Insomnia is a 24-hour disorder of hyperarousal, kept going by learned behaviours and beliefs. Assessment must confirm adequate opportunity and daytime impairment, and exclude circadian disorders, OSA, RLS and medical, psychiatric and drug causes. **CBT-I is the treatment of first choice**, even in comorbid insomnia. Hypnotics are for short-term or adjunctive use at the lowest effective dose.

## In depth

### Background
- **Sleep regulation: Borbély's two-process model (1982).** Process S is homeostatic adenosine-driven sleep pressure; process C is the circadian rhythm from the suprachiasmatic nucleus. Sleep occurs when high pressure coincides with the circadian sleep phase [1].
- **The sleep switch.** Monoaminergic arousal nuclei (LC, DR, TMN) and orexin neurons are mutually inhibitory with the **GABA/galanin VLPO**, forming a "flip-flop switch". Orexin stabilises wakefulness [1].
- **Treatments:**
  - Spielman developed sleep restriction (1987). Bootzin described stimulus control (1972).
  - Insomnia was reclassified from "symptom" to "disorder" (DSM-5, 2013; ICSD-3, 2014).
  - The 30-minute latency criterion was removed, and non-restorative sleep was dropped from ICSD-3 [1].

### Epidemiology
- About a third of adults report insomnia symptoms. About **6–10%** meet criteria for insomnia disorder.
- More common in women, older adults, shift workers, and people with medical or psychiatric illness.
- About **50%** of people with insomnia disorder have a comorbid mental disorder; only about 20% have "primary" insomnia [1].
- **Insomnia is a risk factor** for incident depression (about 2-fold), anxiety and substance use disorders (Breslau 1996; Baglioni 2011). It predicts poorer treatment response and relapse in mood disorders [1].
- Short or disturbed sleep is linked to cardiovascular disease, metabolic syndrome, diabetes and mortality [1].

### Classification and criteria
| Feature | ICSD-3-TR | DSM-5-TR | ICD-11 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Name | Chronic / short-term / other insomnia disorder | Insomnia disorder (episodic, persistent, recurrent) | 7A00 chronic insomnia; 7A01 short-term insomnia |
| Frequency and duration | ≥3/week, ≥3 months (chronic) | ≥3/week, ≥3 months | ≥3/week, ≥3 months |
| Non-restorative sleep | Removed | Retained | — |
| Comorbidity | "Comorbid" allowed | Specify comorbidity | Allowed |

**Clinical subtypes** (described in ICSD but not formal) [1]:
- psychophysiological (conditioned);
- idiopathic (lifelong, from childhood);
- **paradoxical** (misperception: normal PSG, severe complaint);
- inadequate sleep hygiene;
- behavioural insomnia of childhood (sleep-onset association and limit-setting types);
- insomnia comorbid with mental disorder or medical condition;
- drug- or substance-related.

### Mechanism in detail
- **Hyperarousal evidence:**
  - ↑ beta EEG activity in NREM;
  - ↑ metabolic rate and heart rate;
  - ↑ evening cortisol and ACTH;
  - reduced deactivation of arousal regions on PET (Nofzinger) [1].
- **Neuroplastic changes** in hypothalamic sleep centres, with ↑ wake-promoting neurotransmitters (orexin, histamine, dopamine, cortisol) [1].
- **Behavioural model:** extending time in bed reduces sleep efficiency and weakens homeostatic drive. Napping and irregular schedules dysregulate process C. Bed–wake associations condition arousal [1].
- **Cognitive model (Harvey):** worry → selective attention to sleep threats → distorted perception → safety behaviours [1].

### Clinical features in detail
- **Sleep-onset insomnia** is typical of anxiety and delayed phase.
- **Early morning awakening** suggests depression or an advanced phase in elderly people [1].
- **Daytime:** fatigue more than sleepiness. A high ESS suggests another disorder (OSA, narcolepsy).
- **Paradoxical insomnia:** reports almost no sleep, but PSG is normal and daytime impairment is minimal [1].

### Assessment in detail
**Sleep diary indices:**
- Sleep-onset latency.
- Wake after sleep onset.
- Total sleep time.
- **Sleep efficiency = total sleep time ÷ time in bed × 100.** Normal is ≥85%.

**Scales** [1]:
| Scale | Measures |
| --- | --- |
| ISI (7 items, 0–28) | Severity; 8–14 subthreshold, 15–21 moderate, 22–28 severe; tracks treatment response |
| PSQI (19 items, 7 components) | Sleep quality over the past month; >5 = poor |
| Athens Insomnia Scale | ICD-10-based severity |
| DBAS-16 | Dysfunctional beliefs (targets for cognitive therapy) |
| FIRST | Vulnerability to stress-related insomnia |
| ESS | Sleepiness (to exclude hypersomnia disorders) |

**Investigations** (as indicated): TSH, ferritin (for RLS), glucose, drug screen; PSG or home apnoea test if OSA is suspected.

### Differential diagnosis
| Condition | Clue |
| --- | --- |
| Inadequate sleep opportunity / insufficient sleep syndrome | Short time in bed by choice; sleepiness; normal sleep at weekends |
| Delayed sleep–wake phase disorder | Late onset, normal sleep if allowed to wake late; adolescents |
| Advanced sleep–wake phase disorder | Early sleep and early waking; elderly |
| OSA | Snoring, apnoeas, ESS high, morning headache |
| RLS / PLMD | Evening leg discomfort, urge to move |
| Depression | Early waking, anhedonia, low mood |
| Mania / hypomania | Reduced need for sleep without fatigue |
| Substance use and withdrawal | Alcohol, stimulants, benzodiazepine withdrawal |
| Short sleeper | Short sleep without daytime impairment |

### Management details
- **Sleep restriction cautions:**
  - Initial sleepiness; avoid driving when sleepy.
  - Use caution in bipolar disorder (risk of mania), epilepsy and parasomnias.
  - Use a minimum time in bed of 5–6 hours.
- **Benzodiazepines in practice:**
  - Clonazepam 0.5–2 mg or lorazepam 1–4 mg are often used.
  - They reduce slow-wave sleep and are **not FDA-approved for primary insomnia**.
  - Taper gradually (10–25% every 1–2 weeks) when stopping [1].
- **Z-drugs:**
  - Greater selectivity for the α1 subunit, but similar dependence risk [1].
  - Warn about complex sleep behaviours (sleep driving, eating).
  - Women need a lower zolpidem dose (5 mg).
- **Melatonin:** small effect (onset latency reduced by about 7–12 minutes). Better for circadian disorders [1].
- **Orexin antagonists:** reduce sleep latency and increase total sleep time; minimal withdrawal; contraindicated in narcolepsy [1].
- **Trazodone 25–100 mg:** modest benefit; orthostatic hypotension, priapism [1].
- **Pregnancy and lactation:** CBT-I. Avoid routine hypnotics; if needed, short-term low-dose with specialist advice.
- **Combination:** CBT-I plus short-term hypnotic, then taper. This gives good long-term outcomes (Morin 2009).

| Wilson et al. (BAP consensus) | Guideline | — | Graded recommendations in the table above [1, 2] |

### Critical appraisal
- **Access:** CBT-I is the best-evidenced treatment, but very few trained therapists exist, especially in India. Digital and group formats and nurse-led brief behavioural therapy help.
- **Hypnotic trials are short** (2–12 weeks), while real-world use is long-term. Effect sizes on objective sleep are small, and the subjective benefit is larger.
- **Paradoxical insomnia** shows that subjective and objective measures diverge, so treatment targets should be **daytime function and distress**, not PSG minutes.
- **Comorbid insomnia:** treating insomnia improves depression outcomes (e.g., CBT-I alongside antidepressants), which supports treating it independently.
- **Antipsychotic hypnotic use** (quetiapine) is widespread despite poor evidence and metabolic harm.

### Indian context
- **IPS Clinical Practice Guidelines for sleep disorders** (Gupta et al. 2017) give a stepwise diagnostic algorithm for insomnia and hypersomnia [1].
- **Culture:** late bedtimes are culturally common (late dinners, screens), and afternoon naps are customary. Rising night-shift work in BPO/IT sectors adds to the burden.
- **Self-medication** with OTC sleeping pills and benzodiazepines without prescription is widespread. Schedule H1 restrictions are poorly enforced.
- **Ayurveda/yoga** practices (yoga nidra, pranayama) are popular. Some evidence supports yoga for sleep quality, which can be integrated with CBT-I principles.
- **Availability:** DORAs and PR-melatonin availability and cost vary in India, so check current availability.

### Recent advances
- **Established:** dCBT-I (SleepioRx, FDA-cleared 2024); DORAs (suvorexant, lemborexant, daridorexant) [1].
- **Emerging:**
  - CBT-I for comorbid depression, PTSD and psychosis.
  - Brief behavioural treatment for insomnia (BBTI).
  - Wearable consumer sleep trackers (orthosomnia risk).
- **Investigational:** closed-loop acoustic stimulation to enhance slow waves; insomnia phenotyping by sleep duration (Vgontzas: short-sleep insomnia has higher cardiometabolic risk).
