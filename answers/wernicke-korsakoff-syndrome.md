## Exam answer

### Definition and history [1]
- **Wernicke's encephalopathy (WE)** is the **acute neuropsychiatric reaction to severe thiamine deficiency**. It is defined by acute onset of:
  - **nystagmus, abducens (sixth nerve) and conjugate gaze palsies**;
  - **ataxia of gait**;
  - a **global confusional state**;
  - occurring together or in combination (Victor et al. 1971).
- **History:**
  - **Wernicke (1881):** "polioencephalitis haemorrhagica superior" (two alcoholics, one patient vomiting after sulphuric acid poisoning);
  - **Korsakoff (1887):** "psychosis polyneuritica";
  - Malamud & Skillicorn: the two share an identical lesion distribution;
  - **Victor, Adams & Collins (1971):** 245 cases, *The Wernicke–Korsakoff Syndrome*.
- **WE and Korsakoff's syndrome (KS)** are "different facets of the same pathological process": the acute and chronic stages.
- **Terminology:** "Korsakoff's syndrome" is best reserved for amnesia due to **thiamine depletion**. "Amnesic syndrome" is the general term for other damage to the same regions.

### Aetiology and pathophysiology [1, 2]
- **Alcohol** leads to thiamine deficiency through:
  - replacement of food by alcohol;
  - **impaired gut absorption**;
  - impaired **hepatic storage**;
  - reduced phosphorylation to **thiamine pyrophosphate (TPP)**;
  - increased requirements for alcohol metabolism.
  - Partial gastrectomy adds risk.
- **Non-alcoholic causes:**
  - gastric or oesophageal carcinoma; widespread TB; cachexia;
  - pregnancy, **hyperemesis gravidarum**, toxaemia;
  - persistent **vomiting**, diarrhoea;
  - pernicious anaemia;
  - dietary deficiency, **anorexia nervosa**, hunger strike;
  - prolonged **IV feeding**; **renal dialysis**;
  - malnutrition in chronic schizophrenia.
  - Up to **a quarter to a half** of post-mortem cases are non-alcoholic.
- **Biochemistry:** TPP is a coenzyme for **transketolase** (myelin), **pyruvate dehydrogenase** and **α-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase** (glucose metabolism and energy). Severe α-KGDH reduction may trigger neuronal death.
- **Susceptibility:** transketolase variants with low TPP affinity; possible familial transmission.
- **Glucose:** a glucose load in a thiamine-deficient patient can **precipitate WE** [2].
- **Epidemiology:** hospital incidence is highly uneven (Queensland 65 vs New York 8 per million). Post-mortem prevalence is 0.4–2.8%, with no correlation to national alcohol consumption. Males outnumber females about 2:1; all adult ages are affected.
- **Maudsley risk factors in alcohol dependence** [2]:
  - acute withdrawal;
  - malnourishment;
  - decompensated liver disease;
  - ED attendance; hospitalisation for comorbidity;
  - homelessness;
  - memory disturbance; peripheral neuropathy;
  - **previous WE**.

### Clinical features of WE (Victor et al.) [1, 2]
- **Onset:** abrupt, or evolving over days. The commonest presenting features are **confusion or staggering gait**; patients may report diplopia. Prodromal anorexia, nausea and vomiting are common.
- **Classic triad** (confusion, ataxia, ophthalmoplegia):
  - only **16%** of autopsy-proven cases had all three; 28% had two, 37% one, and **19% none** (Harper 1986);
  - hence a **high index of suspicion** is needed [1];
  - the triad is "rarely present", and WE is much commoner than recognised [2].

| Feature | Frequency | Details |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Ocular** | 96% | Nystagmus, **sixth nerve palsy**, conjugate gaze palsy; sluggish pupils; **evanescent** (resolve quickly with thiamine or food) |
| **Ataxia** | 87% | From inability to stand to heel–toe difficulty; intention tremor rare |
| **Peripheral neuropathy** | 82% | Legs; weakness, paraesthesiae, pain |
| **Malnutrition** | 84% | Glossitis, cheilosis, angular stomatitis; liver disease in two-thirds; a quarter bedridden; tachycardia and dyspnoea (overt beriberi rare) |
| **Abstinence syndrome** | 13% | Fits, hallucinosis, DTs |
| **Mental changes** | 90% | **Quiet global confusion**: disorientation, apathy, drowsiness, inattention, **misidentifications**, fatiguability. **Readily rousable**; consciousness rarely profoundly impaired. Some delirium (evanescent DTs). Occasional delusions. **Memory defect** emerges as confusion clears (Korsakoff picture from the outset in 14%). **Confabulation** common early |
| **Other** | — | **Hypothermia, hypotension**, lethargy; rarely stupor or coma |

- **Maudsley presumptive diagnosis:** in any patient undergoing detoxification with **ataxia, hypothermia, hypotension, confusion, ophthalmoplegia/nystagmus, memory disturbance or unconsciousness/coma**. Also weigh malnutrition, weight loss, vomiting, diarrhoea and neuropathy [2].

### Investigations [1]
- **EEG:** diffuse slowing in half (may be normal); generalised asynchronous slow waves, reduced alpha.
- **CSF:** mild protein rise.
- **CT:** symmetrical low attenuation near the **thalamus** (improves with thiamine).
- **MRI:**
  - thalamic lesions;
  - **mamillary body atrophy**;
  - hyperintensities around the **third ventricle and aqueduct**.
- **Cerebral blood flow:** reduced; improves with treatment.

### Pathology [1]
- **Symmetrical lesions** around:
  - the **third ventricle**, **periaqueductal** region and floor of the **fourth ventricle**;
  - **thalamic nuclei** (medial dorsal, anteromedial, pulvinar);
  - the **mamillary bodies**, terminal fornices and brainstem;
  - the **anterior superior cerebellar vermis**.
- Cortex, striatum and hippocampus are rarely affected obviously, though convolutional atrophy was noted in 27%.
- **Microscopy:** all elements are involved, myelin more than neurones; astrocytic and histiocytic proliferation; vascular proliferation; **petechial haemorrhages** (often terminal).
- **Same distribution** in acute WE and chronic KS.
- **Clinicopathological correlations:**
  - ophthalmoplegia: third and sixth nerve nuclei;
  - nystagmus: vestibular nuclei;
  - ataxia: vestibular nuclei and cerebellar vermis;
  - amnesia: medial dorsal thalamus and pulvinar (Victor). Later work: **mamillary bodies, mamillothalamic tract and anterior thalamic nuclei** (Harding 2000).
- **Subclinical WE:** Harper (Perth) found 131 post-mortem cases, **only 26 suspected in life**; two-thirds had chronic changes only. Repeated unrecognised episodes may explain treatment-resistant KS.

### Course after WE [1]
- **Death:** 17% in the acute stage.
- **Sixth nerve palsy** always recovers (hours to weeks). **Horizontal nystagmus** persists in two-thirds.
- **Ataxia:** improves from week 1. **Over half are left permanently unsteady**; a quarter show no improvement.
- **Neuropathy:** slow improvement; absent reflexes may persist.
- **Confusion:** always clears in survivors (begins in 2–3 weeks; complete in 1–2 months).
- **84% develop Korsakoff's syndrome.** Those who escape had brief illnesses. About 10% present with amnesia plus eye and gait signs; 4% develop amnesia without ophthalmoplegia or ataxia.

### Korsakoff's syndrome [1]
- **Core definition:** **anterograde and retrograde amnesia** out of proportion to other cognitive deficits, in **clear consciousness**.
  - **Immediate memory (digit span) is normal**, but supraspan learning fails.
  - **Disorientation in time** is almost universal.
- **Retrograde amnesia:** in alcoholic KS it extends back **20–25 years** with a clear **temporal gradient**. Time sense is disordered: past events are brought up as recent, and periods are telescoped ("temporal tagging").
- **Preserved:** **procedural memory** and priming. IQ is near normal (WAIS similar to controls except digit–symbol). The Wechsler Memory Scale falls **20–30 points below** IQ.
- **Confabulation:**
  - commoner in the **acute (Wernicke) stage**; usually **absent in chronic KS**;
  - **provoked** (momentary) vs **spontaneous** (fantastic), the latter linked to **ventromedial frontal** pathology (Kopelman).
- **Personality:** **apathy, loss of initiative**, bland or fatuous affect, self-neglect, passivity, striking disinterest in alcohol, **lack of insight** (anosognosia).
- **Executive and visuoperceptual deficits:**
  - frontal atrophy (CT interhemispheric widening; SPECT frontal hypoperfusion);
  - PET hypometabolism in the thalamus, ventromedial frontal and retrosplenial regions, and cingulate.
- **Alcohol neurotoxicity:**
  - persistent KS after non-alcoholic thiamine deficiency is equivocal;
  - some patients develop amnesia **insidiously** without a Wernicke episode;
  - so alcohol toxicity plus avitaminosis, or prolonged subclinical deficiency, may both be needed.
  - **Continuity hypothesis:** subclinical memory deficits are common in alcoholics.
- **Overdiagnosis:**
  - Cutting: 17/50 "Korsakoff" patients had gradual onset, were older and more often female, had wider deficits and two-thirds improved; i.e., probably **alcoholic dementia**;
  - Jacobson & Lishman: about two-thirds classic, 10% mainly generalised decline.
- **Differential (other amnesic syndromes):**
  - third ventricle tumours;
  - subarachnoid haemorrhage;
  - **tuberculous meningitis**;
  - herpes encephalitis and bilateral hippocampal lesions (medial temporal atrophy, with thalamus relatively preserved, unlike KS: Colchester).

### Management [1, 2]
**1. Prophylaxis in alcohol misuse** [2]:
- **Low risk** (no neuropsychiatric complications, adequate diet): **oral thiamine 300 mg/day** during withdrawal and continued drinking.
- **NICE:** parenteral then oral thiamine for harmful or dependent drinkers who are malnourished (or at risk) **or** have decompensated liver disease, **and** attend ED or are admitted with acute illness or injury.
- **High risk** (weight loss, poor diet, BMI below 18): IM thiamine **200–300 mg daily for at least 3 days** (community), or IM/IV **200–300 mg daily for 3–5 days** with daily monitoring (hospital).
- Historically, all inpatient detoxification patients received parenteral thiamine. Many countries have no licensed parenteral form.

**2. Established or suspected WE: a medical emergency** [1, 2]:
- treat **from the moment the diagnosis is made**; transfer to a **medical unit for IV thiamine** [2];
- **large doses**: as little as 2–3 mg modifies ophthalmoplegia, but much larger doses are needed to minimise sequelae (hepatic disease impairs utilisation) [1];
- **Pabrinex IV** (because of other vitamin deficiencies). Each injection contains thiamine 250 mg, nicotinamide 160 mg, riboflavin 4 mg, pyridoxine 50 mg and ascorbic acid 500 mg;
- **infuse slowly over 10 minutes** because of **anaphylaxis** risk;
- **at least twice daily for at least 5 days**, then **high-dose oral thiamine** [1];
- **thiamine before glucose** (glucose can precipitate WE) [2];
- **refractory cases:** check **serum magnesium** (hypomagnesaemia impairs response); consider **nicotinic acid deficiency encephalopathy** [1].

**3. Supportive care** [1]:
- treat infection, dehydration, electrolyte imbalance and cardiac failure;
- sedation for disturbed behaviour or DTs;
- continue oral vitamins for weeks;
- in enduring ataxia, neuropathy or memory disturbance, **continue high-potency vitamins as long as improvement occurs**.

**4. Chronic Korsakoff's syndrome** [1]:
- treatment is often **disappointing**: response rates range from none to up to 70%;
- still give **parenteral high-dose thiamine**, then oral thiamine over **many months** while benefit continues;
- recovery: complete in **a quarter**, partial in **half**, none in a quarter (Victor). Improvement may start after weeks to months and continue for up to 2 years;
- neurotransmitter-based treatments have produced no major advance (few trials);
- (memory rehabilitation principles: see the cognitive rehabilitation answer).

**5. Prevention at population level** [1]:
- flour fortification alone is insufficient for problem drinkers;
- **thiamine fortification of alcoholic beverages** has been proposed (cheap);
- wider high-potency prophylaxis in alcoholics.

**Conclusion.** WE is a thiamine-deficiency emergency, classically ophthalmoplegia, ataxia and confusion, but the full triad is present in only 16%, so suspicion must be high (hypothermia, hypotension, memory disturbance). It is mostly but not only alcoholic. Treatment is immediate high-dose parenteral thiamine (Pabrinex) before glucose. Eye signs recover; ataxia and nystagmus often persist; **84% develop Korsakoff's syndrome**. KS is a dense anterograde and graded retrograde amnesia with preserved immediate and procedural memory, apathy and lack of insight, from diencephalic (mamillary–thalamic) lesions plus frontal involvement. It responds poorly, with full recovery in only a quarter. Prophylactic thiamine for at-risk drinkers is the key intervention.

## In depth

### Applying this to the Paper 2 case
*A 55-year-old man is found collapsed on the pavement. He is confused, disoriented to time and place, admits to drinking a bottle of whisky a day, and cannot stand or walk without support.*

- **Working diagnosis:** **Wernicke's encephalopathy** (confusion + ataxia in a heavy drinker). Caine criteria need only 2 of 4: dietary deficiency, eye signs, cerebellar dysfunction, altered mental state or memory. **Eye signs may be absent** (full triad in only 16%) [1, 3].
- **Treat before investigating:** **IV thiamine 500 mg three times a day for 2–3 days** (RCP regimen), then 250 mg daily for 3–5 days if there is a response, **before any glucose** [3]. Pabrinex is preferred because of co-existing vitamin deficiencies [1].
- **Rule out the dangerous differentials of "collapsed and confused":**
  - **hypoglycaemia** (check glucose immediately; give glucose *after* or with thiamine);
  - **head injury, subdural haematoma** (CT; falls are common);
  - alcohol intoxication vs **withdrawal delirium** (time since last drink; tremor, autonomic features);
  - **hepatic encephalopathy** (asterixis, LFT, ammonia);
  - post-ictal state (withdrawal seizures);
  - **hyponatraemia** (beer potomania);
  - sepsis or pneumonia (aspiration);
  - hypothermia;
  - other intoxicants.
- **Examination:** GCS, eye movements (nystagmus, sixth-nerve palsy), gait and truncal ataxia, peripheral neuropathy, stigmata of liver disease, injuries, temperature and BP (hypothermia and hypotension point to WE).
- **Investigations:** glucose, electrolytes (Na, K, **Mg**, phosphate), LFT, INR, ammonia, CBC (MCV), CK, blood alcohol, ECG, CT head, then MRI (medial thalamic, mamillary and periaqueductal T2/FLAIR signal). Take a blood sample for thiamine before giving it, if possible.
- **Alcohol withdrawal:** start a symptom-triggered **benzodiazepine** regimen (CIWA-Ar). Use **lorazepam or oxazepam** if liver disease is present. Correct magnesium, because thiamine needs magnesium as a cofactor.
- **After recovery:**
  - screen for **Korsakoff's syndrome** (MoCA, memory testing);
  - **relapse prevention:** **naltrexone** (if LFT allows), acamprosate, disulfiram (avoid if cognition is impaired), baclofen (in liver disease);
  - psychosocial treatment;
  - continue oral thiamine 100 mg three times a day.
  - In **COMBINE (n = 1,383)**, naltrexone with medical management reduced heavy drinking (HR 0.72); acamprosate added nothing [4].
- **Capacity and safety:** assess decision-making capacity (MHCA 2017). Arrange a social and housing assessment if he lives alone.


### Treatment regimens compared
| Source | Prophylaxis | Established or suspected WE |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Lishman [1] | — | IV Pabrinex at least twice daily for at least 5 days, then high-dose oral thiamine |
| Maudsley [2] | Oral 300 mg/day (low risk); IM/IV 200–300 mg daily for 3–5 days (high risk) | Transfer to a medical unit for IV thiamine; no dose given |
| Royal College of Physicians (Thomson 2002), via Sechi [3] | 250 mg IM once daily for 3–5 days in severe withdrawal, malnutrition or poor diet | **500 mg IV in 100 mL saline over 30 minutes, three times daily for 2–3 days.** If there is a response, 250 mg IV/IM daily for 3–5 days or until improvement stops; if none, stop. Then oral 30 mg twice daily for months |

- Doses of 100–250 mg/day may not restore thiamine status, improve signs or prevent death [3].
- Alcohol-dependent patients **without** clinically apparent WE may need at least 200 mg parenteral to improve neurological signs [3].
- **Indian practice point:** where vials contain only 100 mg, the RCP regimen needs about 15 vials a day. Prescribers often under-dose, so calculate the number of vials explicitly [3].
- **No adequate RCTs:** a Cochrane review found insufficient evidence on dose, frequency, route or duration [3].
- **Safety:** in 989 patients given a 100 mg IV bolus, 1 had a major reaction (pruritus, 0.093%) and 1% had local irritation. A retrospective series of over 300,000 patients found no significant allergic reactions. Dilute in 100 mL and infuse over 30 minutes, with adrenaline available [3].

### Caine criteria (alcohol-dependent patients) [3]
Two of four:
1. Dietary deficiency.
2. Ocular abnormality.
3. Cerebellar dysfunction.
4. Altered mental state **or** mild memory impairment.

These identify WE with high sensitivity and specificity, alone or with KS. The exception is co-existing **hepatic encephalopathy** (sensitivity 50%). Patients with hepatic encephalopathy have a high risk of additional WE, so they should receive parenteral thiamine anyway [3].

### Pathophysiology timeline [3]
- **Stores** last up to about 18 days, so 2–3 weeks of deficiency is enough. The daily requirement is about 1.4 mg (0.5 mg per 1,000 kcal), and the maximum absorbed from a single oral dose is about 4.5 mg.
- **About day 4:** ↓ α-KGDH in astrocytes → cytotoxic oedema.
- **About day 7–10:** ↓ transketolase; endothelial nitric oxide; glutamate excitotoxicity; free radicals; lactate and focal acidosis.
- **About day 14:** DNA fragmentation and apoptosis in thalamic neurons → vasogenic oedema, blood–brain barrier breakdown → irreversible necrosis.
- The early stage is a **reversible biochemical lesion**; this is why speed of treatment matters.
- **Genetics:** transketolase with low TPP affinity (Blass & Gibson 1977); *TKTL1*; **SLC19A2** (high-affinity thiamine transporter) 3′ UTR variants; GABA-A gene cluster on chromosome 5q33. **APOE ε4** is associated with global intellectual decline in WKS.

### Imaging and laboratory performance [3]
- **MRI sensitivity 53%, specificity 93%.** The typical pattern is seen in only 58%. Atypical sites include the cortex and the splenium.
- **DWI and MR spectroscopy** aid early diagnosis.
- **Blood thiamine or erythrocyte transketolase:** limited specificity. HPLC of erythrocyte thiamine esters is more reliable.
- Draw blood before treatment, but **never delay thiamine** for results.
- **Clinical presentation (autopsy series):** mental change 82%, ocular 29%, ataxia 23%. These rates are much lower than in Victor's clinically diagnosed series [1]. The difference reflects how many cases are missed in life.

### Wider clinical settings [3]
| Setting | Point |
| --- | --- |
| **Bariatric and GI surgery** | Usually 4–12 weeks after surgery (range 2 weeks to 20 years), especially young women with vomiting; weight loss over 7 kg/month |
| Hyperemesis gravidarum | Can occur despite routine supplementation; risk of central pontine myelinolysis and death |
| Cancer and chemotherapy | **Malignancy is the commonest cause in children**; ifosfamide; bone-marrow transplant on TPN |
| AIDS, dialysis, thyrotoxicosis, prolonged fever | Increased demand or loss; uraemic guanidinosuccinate inhibits transketolase |
| **Refeeding** or TPN or IV glucose without thiamine | Iatrogenic; relative deficiency |
| Magnesium depletion (diuretics, Crohn's disease) | Magnesium is a cofactor for transketolase and thiamine pyrophosphokinase; causes refractory cases |
| Lithium-induced diarrhoea, migraine vomiting, psychogenic food refusal | Relevant to psychiatric patients |
| Polished-rice diet | Asians tend to develop wet beriberi rather than WE |

**Empirical treatment rule:** give parenteral thiamine to any patient with unexplained coma or stupor, hypothermia or hyperthermia, or intractable hypotension and tachycardia, when a risk factor is present [3].

### Differential diagnosis
| Condition | Distinguishing point |
| --- | --- |
| Alcohol intoxication or DTs | Hard to separate in ED; WE may co-exist, so treat both [3] |
| Hepatic encephalopathy | Asterixis, ammonia; co-existing WE is common [3] |
| Paramedian thalamic (top-of-basilar) infarction | Sudden onset; vascular imaging [3] |

| Hypophosphataemia, methyl bromide, bromvalerylurea | Laboratory and toxicology [3] |

### Critical appraisal
- **Dose regimens rest on consensus,** not RCTs. The three sources disagree, which is why the comparison table above matters [1, 2, 3].
- **Autopsy studies:** 75–80% of WE in alcohol misusers and people with AIDS, and about 58% in children, were missed clinically [3]. Reliance on the triad or MRI produces false reassurance.
- **Non-alcoholic WE is rising** (bariatric surgery, oncology, ICU), and its presentations are often atypical [3].
- **Prevention:** fortifying flour helps, but it did not stop rising KS in Glasgow [3]. Lishman's proposal to fortify alcoholic beverages [1] has not been adopted.

### Indian context
- **Alcohol use disorder:** the Magnitude of Substance Use in India survey (2019) found harmful or dependent alcohol use in about 5% of 10–75-year-olds.
- **Other drivers:** undernutrition and hyperemesis add to risk; wet-beriberi outbreaks have been reported in tea-garden and tribal communities.
- **Prescribing:** low-strength vials make under-dosing common in Indian wards. Write the number of vials explicitly [3].
- **Fortification:** FSSAI standards include thiamine in fortified wheat flour, but coverage is incomplete.

### Recent advances
- **Established:** high-dose parenteral regimens; DWI MRI; Caine criteria [3].
- **Emerging:** HPLC thiamine esters; thiamine-transporter genetics; WE after bariatric surgery and in ICU/refeeding recognised as distinct risk groups [3].
- **Investigational:** benfotiamine; therapies for established KS.
