## Exam answer

### Introduction [1, 2]
- **Neuroleptics:** first-generation (typical) antipsychotics are called "neuroleptics" because they produce **neurolepsis**: psychomotor slowing, emotional quieting and affective indifference [1].
- **Impact:** extrapyramidal symptoms (EPS) are stigmatising, distressing, potentially disabling and a disincentive to taking medication. They are commonly overlooked or misdiagnosed [2].
- **Dose and drug:** EPS are mostly **dose-related**. They are less likely with SGAs, particularly clozapine, olanzapine, quetiapine and aripiprazole, than with FGAs such as haloperidol [2].
- **Community prevalence** of any EPS may exceed **30%** [2].
- **Not all movements are drug-induced:** in never-medicated first-episode patients, 1% had dystonia, 8% parkinsonism and 11% akathisia. In never-treated established psychosis, 9% had spontaneous dyskinesia and 17% parkinsonism [2].

### Mechanism [1]
- **Nigrostriatal D2 blockade:** **striatal D2 occupancy above 78%** raises EPS risk; below 65% gives suboptimal efficacy.
- **Contributing factors:** genetic predisposition; basal-ganglia dopaminergic hypersensitivity; reduced functional reserve; **cholinergic overactivity**.
- **Potency:**
  - **high-potency** drugs (fluphenazine, haloperidol, perphenazine, pimozide, risperidone) carry the highest EPS liability;
  - **low-potency** drugs (chlorpromazine, thioridazine, olanzapine, quetiapine, clozapine, aripiprazole) the lowest.

### Classification (DSM-5 medication-induced movement disorders) [1]
- **Acute:** neuroleptic-induced parkinsonism, acute dystonia, acute akathisia, NMS, postural tremor.
- **Tardive:** tardive dyskinesia, tardive dystonia, tardive akathisia.
- Other medication-induced movement disorders.

### 1. Acute dystonia [1, 2]
- **Features:** involuntary sustained contractions:
  - **oculogyric crisis**;
  - torticollis;
  - buccolingual crisis (trismus, tongue protrusion, dysphagia);
  - **laryngeal dystonia (stridor; life-threatening)**;
  - opisthotonus, tortipelvic crisis, pseudomacroglossia.
- **Experience:** painful and frightening; a common cause of later non-adherence.
- **Onset:** **within hours** of starting or increasing the dose (**minutes** after IM or IV high-potency FGAs). It is the **earliest EPS**, and can also follow reduction of an anti-EPS drug.
- **Prevalence:** about **10%** with FGAs.
- **Risk factors:**
  - **young males**;
  - **antipsychotic-naïve** patients;
  - high-potency drugs;
  - liquid or parenteral formulations;
  - recent cocaine;
  - previous dystonia;
  - family history;
  - mood disorder;
  - hypocalcaemia or hypoparathyroidism.
  - Dystonia is rare in the elderly.
- **Pathophysiology:** D2 changes as CNS drug levels fall; sigma receptors implicated [1].
- **Differential diagnosis:** seizures, Huntington's, Wilson's, TD, Parkinson's disease, neuroacanthocytosis [1].
- **Treatment:**
  - **anticholinergic orally, IM or IV** by severity, e.g., **benztropine 2 mg or diphenhydramine 50 mg** parenterally;
  - IV acts within **5 minutes**, IM in about **20 minutes**;
  - the patient may be unable to swallow;
  - IV diazepam 10 mg is an alternative [1, 2].
- **Prevention:** **prophylactic oral anticholinergic** when a high-potency antipsychotic is used, or use an SGA [1].
- **Refractory cases:** **botulinum toxin** if switching fails [1, 2]; DBS or surgery in refractory dystonia [1].

### 2. Drug-induced parkinsonism [1, 2]
- **Features:**
  - **rigidity** (lead-pipe or cogwheel);
  - **bradykinesia/akinesia** (masked face, reduced arm swing, shuffling gait, slowed speech);
  - **resting tremor**;
  - bradyphrenia, salivation.
- **Pitfalls:** clinically almost **indistinguishable from Parkinson's disease** [1]. It can be mistaken for **depression or negative symptoms** [2].
- **Mechanism:** D2 blockade in the caudate at the nigrostriatal terminals, producing a functional dopamine deficiency [1].
- **Epidemiology:**
  - about **15%** [1] or **20%** [2];
  - more in **elderly women** and those with **pre-existing brain damage** (head injury, stroke);
  - onset **days to weeks** after starting or increasing the dose.
- **Scale:** Simpson–Angus.
- **Treatment:**
  - reduce the dose, or switch to a lower-liability SGA;
  - **anticholinergic** (benztropine, trihexyphenidyl, diphenhydramine). Most patients do not need long-term anticholinergics; **review at least every 3 months**; do not prescribe at night [2];
  - **amantadine** if anticholinergics are not tolerated, or in the elderly [1].

### 3. Akathisia [1, 2]
- **Features:**
  - a **subjective** sense of inner restlessness, with a compulsion to move;
  - **objective** signs: rocking, crossing and uncrossing legs, foot stamping, pacing, alternating sitting and standing.
- **Dangers:** can be mistaken for psychotic agitation. Linked to **suicidal ideation and aggression** (the suicide link is uncertain) [2], and to non-adherence and dysphoria [1].
- **Epidemiology:**
  - about **25%** for acute akathisia with FGAs, lower with SGAs [2]. The slides give incidence 21–75% and prevalence 20–35% [1].
  - **Lowest risk** with olanzapine, quetiapine and clozapine [2]. A 2023 meta-analysis found **minimal risk with sertindole and quetiapine** and **high risk with haloperidol and lurasidone**; among partial agonists, highest with cariprazine and lowest with brexpiprazole [2].
- **Risk factors:**
  - drug: high dose, rapid escalation, high potency, polypharmacy;
  - patient: older age, female sex, mood disorder, negative symptoms, **iron deficiency**, cognitive dysfunction [1].
- **Types** [1]:
  - **acute:** within weeks; lasts under 6 months;
  - **chronic:** 6 months or longer;
  - **tardive:** late onset, may persist after stopping;
  - **withdrawal:** within 6 weeks of stopping or switching;
  - **pseudo-akathisia:** objective signs without the subjective component.
- **Differential diagnosis** [1]:
  - anxiety;
  - **restless legs syndrome**;
  - low iron;
  - hyperthyroidism;
  - neuropathy;
  - alcohol or opiate withdrawal;
  - manic or psychotic agitation.
- **Scale:** **Barnes Akathisia Rating Scale** (objective, subjective, global) [1, 2].
- **Management (Maudsley algorithm)** [2]:
  1. Reduce the dose, move to monotherapy, or slow titration.
  2. Switch to **quetiapine or olanzapine** (or clozapine in TRS).
  3. **Propranolol 30–80 mg/day** (start 10 mg three times a day; note asthma, bradycardia, hypotension).
  4. **Mirtazapine 15 mg, trazodone 50 mg or mianserin 30 mg** (5-HT2A antagonists).
  5. Antimuscarinic (e.g., benzatropine), mainly if other EPS are present; weak evidence.
  6. **Cyproheptadine 16 mg/day.**
  7. **Benzodiazepine** (diazepam up to 15 mg, clonazepam 0.5–3 mg); withdraw after 2–4 weeks.
  8. **Clonidine 0.2–0.8 mg/day**; withdraw very slowly.
  - Evaluate each step for at least 1 month.
  - **2024 meta-analysis:** **mirtazapine, biperiden and vitamin B6** were most efficacious; **B6 had the best efficacy and tolerability balance** [2].
  - The slides list vitamin B6 at 1,200 mg/day (citing RCTs) on one slide and 300 mg/day on another; Maudsley gives no dose [1, 2].

### 4. Neuroleptic malignant syndrome (NMS) [1, 2]
- **Definition:** a rare, idiosyncratic, potentially fatal disorder of **thermoregulation and neuromotor control** [2].
- **Tetrad:**
  - **muscle rigidity** (lead-pipe);
  - **hyperthermia**;
  - **altered consciousness**;
  - **autonomic instability**: tachycardia (88%), labile BP (61–77%), tachypnoea, diaphoresis, incontinence [1].
  - **Presentations are often atypical**, without fever or rigidity [2].
- **DSM criteria (per slides):**
  - **major:** dopamine-antagonist exposure (or dopamine-agonist withdrawal) within 72 hours; rigidity; temperature above 38 °C on two occasions;
  - **minor:** diaphoresis, dysphagia, tremor, incontinence, altered consciousness, mutism, tachycardia, labile BP, leucocytosis, CK above 4× normal [1].
- **Investigations:**
  - **CK often above 1,000 units/L**, leucocytosis, abnormal LFTs [2];
  - myoglobinuria and renal failure; **low serum iron** [1];
  - asymptomatic CK rises are common, so **CK alone is not diagnostic** [2].
- **Epidemiology:**
  - incidence **0.02–0.03%** (other estimates 0.11–0.16%) [2]; slides give 0.02–3% [1];
  - male:female 3:2 [1];
  - **young adult males most at risk; more lethal in older people** [2].
- **Risk factors** [1, 2]:
  - **high-potency FGAs**;
  - **rapid dose increase**;
  - rapid dose reduction;
  - **abrupt anticholinergic withdrawal**;
  - polypharmacy;
  - parenteral or depot use;
  - lithium;
  - **dehydration**, exhaustion, agitation;
  - organic brain disease, alcoholism, PD, hyperthyroidism;
  - **catatonia**;
  - previous NMS.
- **Course:** evolves over 24–72 hours; untreated, it lasts 10–14 days; mean recovery 7–11 days. It is longer with depots and structural brain disease [1].
- **Mortality:**
  - **5.6%** (Maudsley) [2];
  - slides give 5–20%, and up to 20–30% (higher with depots) [1];
  - predictors: respiratory difficulty, severity of hyperthermia, failure to stop the antipsychotic [2].
- **Differential diagnosis:**
  - CNS infection, vasculitis or status epilepticus;
  - heat stroke, thyrotoxicosis, tetanus, porphyria;
  - **catatonia**;
  - **malignant hyperthermia, serotonin syndrome**, intoxication [1].
- **Management** [1, 2]:
  - **Psychiatric unit:** **stop the antipsychotic**; monitor temperature, pulse and BP; benzodiazepines (e.g., IM lorazepam).
  - **Medical unit:** **rehydration, bromocriptine plus dantrolene**, benzodiazepine sedation, ventilation if needed. Levodopa, apomorphine and carbamazepine have also been used. Severe cases may need ICU and dialysis.
  - **ECT** may be effective even after pharmacotherapy fails [2]. The slides suggest it if there is no response within 24–48 hours [1].
  - **Doses in the slides** [1]: bromocriptine 2.5 mg three times a day up to 15 mg three times a day; amantadine 200–400 mg/day; dantrolene "1 mg/kg/day for 8 days then oral for 7 days". Maudsley gives no doses; check the dantrolene dosing before relying on it.
- **Rechallenge** [1, 2]:
  - recurrence is possible (up to 30% in the slides);
  - **stop the antipsychotic for at least 5 days** (the slides say 2 weeks, or 6 weeks for depots) until full resolution;
  - start with a very small dose and titrate slowly, monitoring temperature, pulse and BP;
  - use a **structurally unrelated** or **low-D2-affinity drug (quetiapine, clozapine)**;
  - **avoid depots and high-potency FGAs**;
  - obtain written consent and reduce risk factors; ideally rechallenge in hospital.
- **Other causative drugs:** NMS also occurs with antidepressants, valproate, lithium, metoclopramide and prochlorperazine. SSRIs or cholinesterase inhibitors with antipsychotics may increase the risk [2].

### 5. Antipsychotic-induced postural tremor [1]
- **Features:** fine tremor at 8–12 Hz of the limbs, head, mouth or tongue. Exclude parkinsonism.
- **Course and treatment:** often self-limiting. Otherwise reduce the dose or switch class; benzodiazepines or β-blockers may help.

### 6. Tardive syndromes

**Tardive dyskinesia (TD)** [1, 2]:
- **Features:**
  - orofacial (buccolinguo-masticatory) movements: lip smacking, chewing, **tongue protrusion ("fly-catcher")**, "bon-bon" sign;
  - choreiform "piano-playing" fingers;
  - truncal movements;
  - **respiratory dyskinesia** (grunting);
  - **the tongue is often the first site**.
- **Behaviour of the movements:** worse with stress, absent in sleep. **Masked by antipsychotics** and **unmasked by dose reduction**. Withdrawal dyskinesia that persists is TD [1].
- **DSM criteria (per slides):**
  - movements present for at least 4 weeks;
  - onset during exposure, or within 4 weeks of stopping an oral drug (8 weeks for a depot);
  - **exposure for at least 2 months (1 month if over 60)** [1].
- **Epidemiology:**
  - **annualised incidence 6.5% with FGAs vs 2.6% with SGAs** [2]; about 5% per year of exposure [1, 2];
  - **aripiprazole/partial agonists may have the lowest liability** [2];
  - TD also occurs with metoclopramide and in **never-medicated schizophrenia** [2].
- **Risk factors:**
  - older age, female sex;
  - **affective illness**;
  - **cumulative dose**;
  - early acute EPS;
  - negative symptoms, cognitive impairment;
  - alcohol and substance use;
  - diabetes [1, 2].
- **Pathophysiology:** dopamine-receptor supersensitivity; GABA depletion; cholinergic deficiency; oxidative stress and glutamatergic excitotoxicity [1].
- **Associations:** cognitive impairment, more severe psychopathology and **higher mortality** [2].
- **Monitoring:** **AIMS** at baseline, then every 6 months with FGAs and yearly with SGAs. In high-risk patients, every 3 months (FGAs) or 6 months (SGAs) [1].
- **Prevention is best:**
  - inform patients;
  - use the lowest dose (risk equals duration × dose);
  - reduce doses gradually [1, 2].
- **Management** [2]:
  1. **Stop anticholinergics.** Reduce the antipsychotic dose (TD may worsen initially). Evidence for both steps is limited.
  2. **Switch to clozapine** (best supported) or **quetiapine**; olanzapine and aripiprazole are options.
  3. **Add-on agents (Table 1.29):**
     - **VMAT-2 inhibitors are first choice:**
       - **valbenazine 80 mg once daily** (KINECT trials);
       - **deutetrabenazine 12–48 mg/day**;
       - **tetrabenazine 25–200 mg/day** (the only licensed TD treatment in the UK; causes depression, drowsiness, parkinsonism and akathisia);
     - benzodiazepines (clonazepam 1–4 mg; inconclusive evidence);
     - **vitamin E 400–1,600 IU** (may slow deterioration);
     - **pyridoxine up to 400 mg**;
     - amantadine 100–300 mg;
     - Ginkgo biloba 240 mg.
  4. **Other options (Table 1.30):**
     - **botulinum toxin** for focal symptoms;
     - **DBS (GPi)** for severe refractory TD;
     - melatonin;
     - levetiracetam;
     - **rTMS**;
     - donepezil, naltrexone, ondansetron, propranolol and others, mostly with low-level evidence.
  - **ECT** may help TD [2].

**Tardive dystonia** [1]:
- **Onset and frequency:** occurs after more than 3 months of dopamine blockers; incidence 1.5–2%.
- **Drug choice:** less likely with clozapine and quetiapine.
- **Refractory cases:** DBS.

**Tardive akathisia:** late onset; provoked or exacerbated by dose reduction or withdrawal [2].

**Conclusion.** Antipsychotic-induced movement disorders range from:
- **acute:** dystonia, parkinsonism, akathisia, NMS;
- **to tardive:** TD, tardive dystonia, tardive akathisia.

They arise from striatal D2 blockade, depend on dose and potency, and are less common with SGAs. Management rests on prevention (lowest effective dose, avoiding polypharmacy, careful drug choice), early detection with rating scales (Simpson–Angus, BARS, AIMS), specific treatments for each syndrome, and emergency care for NMS.

### Lishman (2009): additional detail [3]
- **Potency:**
  - piperazine phenothiazines (trifluoperazine) cause more EPS than aliphatic or piperidine ones (chlorpromazine, thioridazine);
  - butyrophenones are particularly potent;
  - atypicals reduce, but do not eliminate, the risk.
- **Drug-induced parkinsonism:**
  - 20–40% of patients; within the first month; dose-dependent; more common with age;
  - **bradykinesia is the earliest and commonest sign**; upper limbs, then jaw, then legs;
  - **bilateral and symmetrical, with little tremor**;
  - **rabbit syndrome**: late fine perioral tremor;
  - drooling from reduced swallowing (unlike clozapine hypersalivation);
  - may resolve with tolerance or take **up to 18 months** after stopping; failure to recover suggests underlying PD;
  - severe cases resemble **catatonia** or akinetic mutism (differentiate from NMS).
  - **Management:** anticholinergics, **tapered after 3 months**. Routine prophylactic anticholinergics are contraindicated.
- **Akathisia:**
  - **up to 90% of young patients**;
  - deep leg sensations; rocking from foot to foot or walking on the spot, through to an inability to sit;
  - **pseudoakathisia** (objective signs without subjective distress) in negative-symptom patients;
  - **Barnes rating scale**.
  - **Differentiate restless legs syndrome:**
    - 6–9% of the community;
    - evening or night worsening;
    - **periodic limb movements in sleep in 80%**;
    - iron deficiency, renal failure, pregnancy;
    - check ferritin; dopaminergic first line.
  - **Treatment:** dose reduction, **propranolol** (most encouraging), benzodiazepines, amantadine, clonidine. Anticholinergics are rarely useful.
- **Acute dystonia:**
  - about 2%; days after oral or hours after IM dosing; **peak at 24–48 hours**;
  - young males; previous dystonia; cocaine use;
  - oculogyric deviation, trismus, tongue protrusion, **laryngeal stridor**, opisthotonos;
  - can be **mistaken for status epilepticus, tetanus or hysteria**.
  - **Treatment:** IV/IM benzatropine, diphenhydramine or procyclidine; IV diazepam.
  - **Prophylactic anticholinergic** is justified only after a previous dystonic reaction.
- **Tardive dyskinesia:**
  - **orofacial** in three-quarters, limbs in half, trunk in a quarter;
  - **no rhythmic tremor**; worse with stress; less with drowsiness;
  - spontaneous dyskinesia in up to 6% of people in their 70s.
  - **Incidence:** 10–20% after 1 year (APA Task Force); 25% at 1 year rising to **53% at 3 years** (Woerner); disabling in 5–10%.
  - **DSM-IV duration rule:** 3 months of exposure (1 month if over 60) and symptoms for more than 4 weeks.
  - **Risk factors:**
    - age over 40; elderly women;
    - brain damage (ECT or leucotomy history, cognitive impairment, negative symptoms, ventricular enlargement);
    - **affective disorder**; diabetes;
    - **alcohol misuse (threefold)**; earlier acute EPS;
    - **drug holidays**; CYP2D6 and DRD3 variants.
  - **Atypicals:** 5–10 times lower risk (risperidone vs haloperidol).
  - **Course:**
    - 25–50% improve markedly within a year of stopping;
    - at 5 years, half are stable, a quarter fluctuate, 11% improve, 8% worsen;
    - **about 30% of adults are permanent** (especially the elderly); **children recover**.
  - **Other drugs that can cause it:** oral contraceptives, anticonvulsants, TCAs, metoclopramide.
  - **Management:**
    - stop anticholinergics;
    - taper the antipsychotic (30–50% improve; may transiently worsen);
    - switch, including to **clozapine**;
    - tetrabenazine (avoid in depression), clonazepam, **vitamin E**, gabapentin, melatonin, pyridoxine, donepezil;
    - **never increase neuroleptics to mask TD** unless it is life-threatening.
- **Tardive dystonia:**
  - after 3–7 years (can be under 1 year); **more distressing and disabling than TD**;
  - focal onset, spreading to segmental (two-thirds) or generalised (15%);
  - younger patients have more generalised dystonia;
  - laryngospasm can need tracheostomy;
  - **anticholinergics may help** (they worsen TD);
  - remission in under 15%; a better outcome if exposure was under 10 years.
  - **Treatment:** taper the drug; anticholinergic; reserpine or tetrabenazine (two-thirds improve); benzodiazepines, baclofen; **clozapine (drug of choice if antipsychotic needed)**, ± clonazepam; **botulinum toxin** for focal forms.
  - **Exclude Wilson's**; don't mistake it for a psychogenic disorder.
- **Tardive akathisia:**
  - after a mean of 4.5 years; **worsens on dose reduction** (unlike acute akathisia);
  - marching, leg crossing, rocking, moaning;
  - reserpine or tetrabenazine best (one-third resolve); **β-blockers and anticholinergics ineffective**; lorazepam occasionally helps.
- **Pathophysiology:**
  - **parkinsonism:** "chemical denervation" (D2 blockade); fast-dissociation hypothesis for atypicals;
  - **akathisia:** extrastriatal D2 blockade with adrenergic involvement;
  - **acute dystonia:** presynaptic mismatch or D1 over-activity, possibly σ receptors;
  - **TD:** **D2 receptor supersensitivity/upregulation** and D1/D2 imbalance (explains withdrawal emergence and masking by higher doses), plus **excitotoxic or free-radical loss of GABAergic pallido-thalamic neurones** (supports the antioxidant rationale).
