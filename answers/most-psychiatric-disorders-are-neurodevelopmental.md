## Exam answer

**Introduction.**
- A **neurodevelopmental** account holds that a disorder arises from **disrupted brain development**: genes and early environment act in prenatal life or childhood, with signs present **before** the clinical illness.
- The claim that "**most**" psychiatric disorders are neurodevelopmental extends the model from ADHD, autism and ID to schizophrenia, mood, anxiety and personality disorders.

### Arguments FOR
**1. Strong evidence in schizophrenia [1]:**
- **Premorbid deficits:** delayed milestones, poorer social development and lower IQ in children who later develop schizophrenia.
  - 1946 and 1958 UK cohorts.
  - **Swedish conscripts:** a linear IQ–risk gradient; the whole group shifted down 5–10 IQ points.
- **Brain changes at first episode:** ventricular enlargement and cortical volume loss.
- **No gliosis** (+++), which "supports the hypothesis that schizophrenia is a disorder of **prenatal neurodevelopment**".
- **Developmental anomalies** (cavum septum pellucidum, callosal agenesis) and **developmental genes** (neuregulin, DISC1, dysbindin). Disrupted neurodevelopment is "a key aspect if not an essential part" of the disorder.

**2. Heritability and epigenetics begin before birth [3]:**
- High heritability: **schizophrenia about 80%**, **bipolar about 70%**, **ADHD about 76%**.
- **Early-life stress** alters gene expression (methylation of FKBP5, NR3C1). Maternal depression in pregnancy alters the fetal HPA axis and raises the risk of adolescent anxiety and depression.

**3. Early adversity shapes later disorders [3]:**
- **ACEs:** 4 or more ACEs bring a **4-fold** risk of depression and a **12-fold** risk of suicide attempts, plus more PTSD, substance use and personality disorder.
- **Mechanisms:** cortisol effects on the hippocampus and amygdala; inflammation; HPA dysregulation.
- **Bucharest Early Intervention Project:** structural brain changes in institutionalised children correlate with later illness.
- **Attachment:** insecure or disorganised attachment is linked to **borderline traits**, PTSD and chronic anxiety (Bowlby; Fonagy's mentalisation).

**4. Developmental continuity [3]:**
- **ADHD** goes on to substance use, bipolar disorder and depression.
- **Conduct disorder** goes on to antisocial personality.
- **ASD** is associated with schizophrenia, social phobia and OCD.
- **Learning disability** is associated with depression and social anxiety.
- **Dunedin:** childhood self-regulation and attention deficits predicted adult anxiety and antisocial behaviour. **ALSPAC:** emotional dysregulation at 7 predicted depression at 18.

**5. Neurodevelopmental routes in substance-related psychosis [5]:** adolescent cannabis exposure disturbs endocannabinoid-guided brain maturation. Earlier exposure brings a higher risk of psychosis.

### Arguments AGAINST
**1. Not even ADHD is always neurodevelopmental [2]:**
- **Dunedin cohort** (1,037, followed to 38): childhood ADHD prevalence 6%, adult ADHD 3%.
- The two groups were "**virtually non-overlapping**": **90% of adult ADHD cases had no childhood ADHD**.
- Adult ADHD showed **no childhood or adult neuropsychological deficits** and **no polygenic risk** for childhood ADHD.
- The authors raise the possibility that adults presenting with ADHD symptoms "**may not have a childhood-onset neurodevelopmental disorder**".

**2. Bipolar disorder lacks the premorbid deficit [1]:** the premorbid IQ–risk pattern in Swedish conscripts "is **not seen in bipolar disorder**". This is a key point of divergence from schizophrenia.

**3. A neurodegenerative or post-onset element exists even in schizophrenia [1]:**
- Serial MRI shows **grey-matter loss after the first episode**.
- Some patients show **marked decline** despite good symptom control.
- A combined model fits better than a purely developmental one.

**4. Many disorders arise from adult-onset causes [1, 3, 4]:**
- **Adult triggers:** trauma (war, sexual violence), **TBI, stroke**, medical illness, bereavement, substance use [3].
- **Organic disorders:** delirium, dementia, endocrine and metabolic disorders, epilepsy and head injury are acquired [1].
- **Late-onset psychosis in women:** first admission for psychosis after 40 is about **twice as high in women (21%) as men (10%)**, attributed to declining **oestrogen** at menopause [4].
- **Cannabis-related psychosis:** patients who develop psychosis in the context of cannabis appear to have **fewer neurodevelopmental risk factors** and better cognitive and social function [4].

**5. Developmental risk is not destiny [3, 5]:**
- **Multifinality and equifinality:** the same early adversity leads to different outcomes, and different paths lead to the same disorder.
- **Resilience:** high IQ, social skills, one consistent caregiver, education and community support all protect.
- "Childhood is formative but **not deterministic**". **Adult neuroplasticity** allows recovery.
- Cannabis is "**neither a necessary nor a sufficient cause**" of psychosis; most users never develop it [5].

**6. Over-inclusion weakens the concept [1, 2]:**
- If every early influence counts, "neurodevelopmental" loses meaning.
- Lishman warns against "woolly thinking" that ignores real distinctions between classes of disorder [1].
- Classification (DSM-5 places adult ADHD with childhood ADHD) may need revisiting if the evidence does not support childhood onset [2].

### Synthesis
| Disorder group | Neurodevelopmental fit (from sources) |
|---|---|
| ID, ASD, childhood ADHD, SLD | **Strong** (core neurodevelopmental disorders) |
| Schizophrenia | **Strong but incomplete** (developmental origin with possible later progression) |
| Bipolar disorder | **Weaker** (no premorbid IQ deficit; high heritability) |
| Depression, anxiety, PTSD, personality disorders | **Partial** (developmental vulnerability through ACEs, attachment and epigenetics; adult triggers essential) |
| Adult-onset ADHD | **Questioned** (Moffitt) |
| Dementia, delirium, other organic disorders, late-onset psychosis | **Not neurodevelopmental** (acquired or degenerative) |

**Conclusion.** The statement is **an overgeneralisation**. Neurodevelopmental mechanisms are central to ID, ASD, childhood ADHD and much of schizophrenia, and **early-life vulnerability** contributes to many other disorders. But bipolar disorder, adult-onset ADHD, acquired organic disorders, late-onset psychoses and trauma-triggered conditions show that many psychiatric disorders are **not primarily neurodevelopmental**. A **developmental vulnerability plus later-life triggers** model fits best: "the seeds are often sown in childhood, [but] their germination depends on later experiences, context, and resilience" [3].
