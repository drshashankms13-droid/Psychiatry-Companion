## Exam answer

**Introduction.**
- **Definition:** mesial temporal lobe epilepsy (MTLE) is localisation-related epilepsy arising from the **medial temporal structures**: hippocampus, amygdala, parahippocampal gyrus [1].
- **Frequency:** TLE is the commonest anatomically defined syndrome, about **60% of localisation-related epilepsy**.
- **Commonest cause:** **hippocampal sclerosis** (mesial temporal or Ammon's horn sclerosis), 50–70% of temporal lobectomy series.
- **Why it matters to psychiatrists:**
  - its auras mimic psychiatric symptoms;
  - it is associated with depression, psychosis and personality questions;
  - it is the **most surgically remediable** epilepsy.

### Aetiopathogenesis [1]
- **Hippocampal sclerosis:**
  - dense gliosis and neuronal loss of Ammon's horn, amygdala and uncus;
  - usually **unilateral** (bilateral in about 13–15%);
  - commonly begins in the **first decade**;
  - often severe;
  - responds well to surgery.
- **Febrile convulsions:**
  - prolonged or focal **febrile convulsions/status** in early childhood are consistently associated;
  - **the causal direction is unresolved**. Three possibilities:
    1. febrile seizures damage a normal hippocampus;
    2. predisposed brains have febrile seizures that cause sclerosis;
    3. pre-existing hippocampal or dysplastic pathology causes the febrile seizures.
  - Prospective studies have not found sclerosis after prolonged febrile seizures. Cortical dysplasia was found in three-quarters of children with febrile-convulsion histories.
  - Birth injury has only a weak link.
- **Other causes of TLE:** DNETs, cavernous angiomas, gliomas, cortical dysplasia, post-encephalitic or meningitic gliosis.

### Clinical features [1]
- **Seizures:**
  - simple and complex partial seizures (both in about 70%);
  - **gradual onset**, motionless **stare**, prolonged (automatisms about 2 minutes);
  - secondary generalisation is less common than in extratemporal epilepsy.

**Auras (Table 6.3):**

| Aura | Frequency in TLE | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Epigastric** (rising "butterflies") | **30–53%** (commonest) | With autonomic changes: pallor, flushing, tachycardia, piloerection |
| **Affective: fear/anxiety** | 14–24% | Sudden, intense, "unlike anything else"; crude and stereotyped. Possibly **non-dominant mesial** focus (frontal and cingulate spread). Rarely depression, guilt, joy or ecstasy |
| **Dysmnestic: déjà vu / jamais vu** | 7–18% | Compulsive reminiscence, prescience, **panoramic memory**, distorted time sense, "**dreamy state**" |
| **Olfactory / gustatory** | 8–12% / 3–11% | Unpleasant; **amygdala**; tumours are the commonest cause |
| Auditory, complex visual | Less common | More lateral (neocortical) temporal |
| Cognitive | Rare | **Forced thinking**, thought "blocking", crowding of thoughts |
| Illusions | — | Macropsia/micropsia, distortions, depersonalisation/derealisation, heightened significance |
| No aura | 10–51% | — |

Auras often show a **"march"** (e.g., epigastric → gustatory → fear).

**Ictal semiology and lateralising signs (Table 6.4):**
- **Oro-alimentary automatisms** (lip-smacking, chewing, swallowing) and **gestural** automatisms (fumbling, picking).
- **Ipsilateral focus:**
  - unilateral gestural automatisms;
  - **postictal nose-rubbing** (ipsilateral in 90%).
- **Contralateral focus:**
  - **dystonic posturing of the arm** (up to 70%);
  - **late version** before generalisation;
  - unilateral clonic activity;
  - Todd's paresis (rare).
- **Dominant hemisphere:** postictal **dysphasia**.
- **Non-dominant hemisphere:** **ictal speech** automatisms; ictal fear (possibly).

**Postictal:**
- slow recovery with **several minutes of confusion**; headache;
- amnesia for the seizure.

### Investigations [1]
- **EEG:**
  - interictal sensitivity higher in TLE than in frontal epilepsy;
  - about 50% abnormal on the first EEG, about 80% with sleep recordings;
  - **video-EEG telemetry is the gold standard** (confirms epilepsy, localises, excludes dissociative seizures).
- **MRI:**
  - **oblique coronal T1/T2 perpendicular to the hippocampal axis**;
  - hippocampal **atrophy** and **raised T2 signal**;
  - visual inspection detects 20% or greater asymmetry;
  - volumetry is reserved for equivocal pre-surgical cases;
  - **dual pathology** is common, so look for both.
- **Ictal SPECT** (hyperperfusion), interictal PET, ¹¹C-flumazenil PET.
- **Neuropsychology:**
  - **left TLE: verbal memory** deficits; **right: non-verbal**;
  - **Wada test** for language and memory lateralisation;
  - fMRI.
- **Psychiatric assessment** before surgery is essential.

### Treatment [1]
**Medical:**
- **Carbamazepine** was traditionally first choice for localisation-related epilepsy.
- **SANAD:** carbamazepine was slightly better for 12-month remission, but **lamotrigine had longer time to treatment failure**; lamotrigine is a cost-effective alternative. Oxcarbazepine is comparable.
- After **two failed ASMs**, remission with drugs alone is unlikely, so refer for surgery.

**Surgery:**
- **Who:** MTLE is the **largest surgical group**. Consider referral early (within about 2 years) once two ASMs have failed.
- **Procedures:** anteromedial temporal lobectomy, or selective amygdalohippocampectomy.
- **Outcomes:**
  - **RCT (Wiebe 2001): 64% free of disabling seizures at 1 year vs 8% with medical treatment**; about 68% in uncontrolled series;
  - **normal MRI predicts poor outcome** (about 20% seizure-free); foreign-tissue lesions do slightly better than sclerosis (which is sometimes bilateral);
  - **quality of life improves only if seizure-free**.
- **Risks:**
  - serious complications under 2%;
  - superior quadrantanopia (usually unnoticed);
  - word-finding difficulty (dominant side);
  - new memory deficit in about 5% (worse with high preoperative function).
- **Psychiatric issues:**
  - **comorbid dissociative seizures almost contraindicate surgery**;
  - a psychiatric history is *not* a contraindication;
  - **postictal psychosis suggests bilateral disease**;
  - **de novo depression in about one-third** postoperatively (suicide risk);
  - psychosis can arise after surgery;
  - "seizure phobia" may persist despite seizure freedom (CBT).

### Psychiatric associations of TLE [1]

| Domain | Finding |
| --- | --- |
| Ictal mimics | Ictal fear vs **panic disorder** (unprovoked, unique quality, no catastrophic cognitions); auras vs psychosis or depersonalisation |
| Depression | Common in poorly controlled TLE, but **not specific to TLE or a side** when seizure frequency is controlled |
| Psychosis | Postictal (bilateral temporal disease); interictal schizophrenia-like (excess of localisation-related epilepsy, confounded by severity; developmental "alien tissue" lesions more than sclerosis) |
| Personality | Geschwind syndrome (hyposexuality, religiosity, hypergraphia, viscosity) is **not validated as TLE-specific**. Hypergraphia was more common in TLE (Sachdev & Waxman) |
| Sexual | Hyposexuality; more common in localisation-related and possibly right-sided epilepsy; enzyme-inducer effects |
| Cognition | Lateralised memory deficits |

**Conclusion.** MTLE is the commonest focal epilepsy:
- usually due to **hippocampal sclerosis** (linked, uncertainly, to early prolonged febrile convulsions);
- characterised by **epigastric, fear and déjà vu auras**, stare, oro-alimentary automatisms, contralateral dystonic posturing and slow postictal recovery;
- diagnosed with coronal MRI and video-EEG.

It is often drug-resistant but **highly responsive to surgery** (about 64% seizure-free vs 8%). It carries important psychiatric dimensions: aura mimicry, depression, psychosis, and postoperative psychiatric morbidity.

## In depth

### MTLE vs lateral neocortical TLE [1]
- **Neocortical (lateral) TLE:**
  - more **complex auditory and visual auras**;
  - longer preserved awareness at onset;
  - more motor features.
- **Mesial TLE:** epigastric, olfactory and gustatory auras, fear, déjà vu.
