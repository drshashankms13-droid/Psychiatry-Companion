## Exam answer

### Definition and dominance [1]
- **Aphasia** covers all varieties and severities of acquired language disturbance (Lishman prefers "aphasia" to "dysphasia"). Aphasic symptoms are probably **the most useful cognitive sign for localising brain pathology**, though correlations beyond broad limits remain uncertain.
- **Hemispheric dominance:**
  - in right-handers almost all aphasia follows **left** lesions; right-sided lesions cause it in only about **1%**;
  - left-handers also become aphasic more often from left than right lesions (about **2:1**);
  - **Wada test** (Rasmussen & Milner 1977, 396 patients): right-handers 92% left, 6% right, 2% bilateral speech; left-handers/ambidextrous 70% left, 15% right, 15% bilateral.

### Models and classification [1]
- **Wernicke–Lichtheim model:** motor and auditory word-representation centres and their connection. It explains Broca's, Wernicke's, conduction (repetition lost) and transcortical (repetition preserved) aphasias, but leaves out reading and writing.
- **Geschwind:** language depends on cross-modal associations through the **angular gyrus** (heteromodal cortex at the temporo-parieto-occipital junction).
- **Linguistic levels:** phonology/orthography → syntax → lexical semantics → pragmatics.
- **Clinical division:** **non-fluent** (anterior lesions) vs **fluent** (posterior lesions) (Benson & Geschwind).
  - **Fluent:** clear articulation, effortless, normal or excessive output, frequent paraphasias, normal phrase length, rhythm and inflexion preserved.
  - **Non-fluent:** poor articulation, effortful, sparse but meaningful output, phrases of one or two words, disturbed rhythm and inflexion.

### Clinical syndromes [1]

| Syndrome | Speech | Comprehension | Repetition | Naming | Reading | Writing | Lesion |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Broca's** (motor, expressive) | NF: sparse, slow, effortful, **agrammatic/telegraphic**, perseveration; aware of errors | ± (difficulty with grammatical structures) | − | ± | Aloud −; comprehension ± | − | Posterior two-thirds of the **third frontal convolution** (pars triangularis, operculum), ± lower precentral gyrus; often left MCA occlusion |

F, fluent; NF, non-fluent; +, preserved; −, impaired; ±, variable (after Benson & Geschwind 1971).

**Other forms [1]:**
- **Subcortical aphasia:** mutism after haemorrhage → hypophonia → paraphasic speech with good repetition.
  - **Thalamic:** fluent paraphasic jargon, severe anomia, usually transient.
  - **Striatocapsular:** sparse, dysarthric, paraphasic speech; comprehension and repetition good.
  - Its status is uncertain because of **diaschisis** (secondary cortical effects).
- **Jargon aphasia:** voluble meaningless speech with neologisms; usually a severe Wernicke's aphasia. Weinstein linked it to **anosognosia** and bilateral damage.
- **Acquired alexias:** deep dyslexia (semantic errors, "dog" → "animal"), phonological dyslexia (cannot read non-words) and surface dyslexia (regularisation errors; seen in semantic dementia).
- **Dynamic aphasia:** little spontaneous speech, normal when "unlocked" by a prompt; left frontal (perhaps area 45).

### Bedside assessment [1]
1. **Handedness**; history of transient neurological episodes.
2. **Spontaneous speech:** fluency, articulation, paraphasias (semantic, phonemic, neologisms), grammar, prosody, perseveration, echolalia, palilalia, logoclonia; automatic speech (counting, days of the week) and emotional utterances.
3. **Comprehension:** pointing; simple then complex and sequential commands; **Marie's three-paper test**; syntactic commands with book, pen and coin. If commands fail, use yes/no answers, because failure may be **dyspraxia**.
4. **Repetition:** "No ifs, ands or buts"; paraphasias show up most clearly here.
5. **Naming:** common and **low-frequency** items (buckle, lapel, knuckle) and colours.
6. **Reading** aloud and for comprehension; **writing** spontaneously, to dictation and by copying.
7. **Verbal fluency:** FAS (normal total over 30) and category (animals).
8. **Formal tests:** Boston Naming Test, Graded Naming Test, Token Test, PALPA.

### Psychiatric aspects [1]
- **Broca's:** **frustration and depression** are common, as is the **catastrophic reaction** (sudden weeping or anger). Distress indicates awareness and a **better prognosis** for therapy. Its absence suggests widespread damage. Angry negativism can obstruct rehabilitation.
- **Wernicke's:** lack of concern or **unawareness**. **Paranoid reactions**, agitation and impulsive aggression occur; in Benson's experience, nearly every patient needing custodial care had a paranoid reaction secondary to poor comprehension.
- **Social:** the loss of conversation, reading and TV; altered family roles, status and sexual confidence.
- **Differential from schizophrenic thought disorder:**
  - Wernicke's or transcortical sensory aphasia can be **misdiagnosed as psychosis**: the neurological exam is often negative and output seems "confused".
  - Gerson et al.: aphasic patients give **shorter replies**, have some **awareness** and seek help with gestures or pauses; their **circumlocution** reflects word-finding difficulty.
  - Schizophrenic patients show bizarre reiterated themes, are unaware of failed communication, and show **circumstantiality** and tangentiality.
  - "Schizophasia" is an **expressive semantic** disorder with preserved naming.
  - **Sudden onset of speech disorder demands caution, even in established schizophrenia.**
- **Conversion disorder:** mutism or aphonia, or babble without real words; psychogenic dyslexia is very rare.

### Management [1]
1. **Treat the cause** and give organised **stroke-unit care** (better survival and independence at 1 year).
2. **Speech and language therapist:** assessment of **swallowing** and help with communication (stroke and head injury).
3. **Communication strategies (all staff):**
   - establish **emotional contact** first, through the channels most intact;
   - **picture boards**, then word or phrase cards;
   - **gestures and visual signs** with one or two concrete words when comprehension is very poor; avoid a confusing flow of speech;
   - later, instructions spaced, given slowly and **consistently** the same way;
   - prevent withdrawal after early failures; keep the patient active and wanting to communicate.
   - Once even a little communication is re-established, frustration and fear diminish.
4. **Evidence for formal therapy:** Cochrane (Greener 2000): speech and language therapy after stroke "not shown to be clearly effective or clearly ineffective" in RCTs. Retraining clearly helps **morale and emotional adjustment**.
5. **Recovery:** largely spontaneous. Imaging (Price & Crinion 2005) links speech recovery to gradual **left-hemisphere (ipsilesional)** changes. Right-hemisphere activation does not seem to correlate with recovery for speech; both temporal lobes contribute to comprehension.
6. **Psychiatric care:** watch for and treat **depression**; manage catastrophic reactions, paranoid reactions and negativism.
7. **Family and social support:** explain the deficit; social worker; plan for altered roles.
8. **Legal competence** in aphasia can be difficult to assess (Benson & Ardila 1996).

**Conclusion.** Aphasias are classified by fluency, comprehension, repetition and naming, which map onto perisylvian (Broca's, Wernicke's, conduction) and extrasylvian (transcortical) lesions of the dominant hemisphere. Psychiatrists need them because:
- Wernicke's and transcortical sensory aphasia can mimic psychosis;
- Broca's aphasia carries depression and catastrophic reactions;
- Wernicke's aphasia carries paranoid reactions.

Management is the cause, the speech and language therapist, consistent communication strategies by all staff, attention to mood, and family support. Formal speech therapy has unproven efficacy in RCTs but helps morale.
