## Exam answer

**Definition [1].**
- Postpartum psychosis (PPP) is a **severe mental illness with abrupt onset in the postpartum period, usually within 4–6 weeks of childbirth**.
- It presents as one of four syndromes:
  - **acute mania** (with psychotic symptoms);
  - **acute and transient psychotic disorder**;
  - **catatonia**;
  - **psychotic depression**.

**Epidemiology and risk [1, 2]:**
- Incidence **12 per 1000 live births** [1]. Maudsley gives **0.1–0.25%**, or about 1–2 psychiatric hospitalisations per 1000 births [2].
- **Bipolar disorder:** about **1 in 5** women have a postpartum psychotic relapse [2].
- **Highest risk** (up to **50%**): a previous episode, **bipolar I or schizoaffective disorder**, and family loading for PPP, bipolar I or schizoaffective disorder [2].
- **Psychosis during pregnancy predicts PPP.** Pregnancy does not protect against relapse [2].
- Bipolar relapse is **66%** if prophylaxis is stopped vs about **25%** if it is continued [1].
- **Catatonia** is common: about **20%** of perinatal admissions vs about 8% of general acute admissions [1].

### Assessment [1]
**History (Box 1):**
- current illness and functioning;
- past episodes and treatment;
- family history; premorbid personality;
- **past pregnancies and outcomes**; traumatic or violent obstetric experiences;
- childhood sexual abuse or assault;
- whether the pregnancy was planned or wanted, or a "precious" pregnancy;
- spouse and family support;
- **substance use in the woman and spouse** (a risk factor for domestic violence).
- Interview **in privacy**.

**Rule out organic causes:**
- **Examination:** pallor, jaundice, BP, pedal oedema, thyroid, bleeding signs, cardiorespiratory and neurological systems, signs of connective tissue disease.
- **Confusion, perplexity or disorientation** point to a medical or neurological cause.
- **Investigations (Table 3):**
  - CBC and cultures (infection);
  - electrolytes and calcium; urea, creatinine, eGFR;
  - glucose, ketones, HbA1c;
  - haemogram, iron, **B12**;
  - **TSH and anti-thyroid antibodies** (autoimmune thyroiditis);
  - LFT, ammonia;
  - urine drug screen;
  - **neuroimaging** (CT is avoided in pregnancy), EEG, CSF.
- **Conditions to exclude:**
  - head injury, stroke, hypertensive encephalopathy, **PRES, cortical venous thrombosis**;
  - **Sheehan syndrome, Wernicke encephalopathy**;
  - ADEM or MS; **anti-NMDA receptor encephalitis**;
  - meningitis or encephalitis.

**MSE:**
- Note **delusions and hallucinations relating to the infant** (Table 4):
  - that the infant is dead, defective, divine or special;
  - that others will steal the infant;
  - **command hallucinations**, which can lead to harm to the infant or to over-protective clinging.
- **Mania:** clinging to the infant, and **rough handling** that may injure it.

**Risk assessment:**
- **suicide** (IS PATH WARM or another structured tool);
- **infanticide** (depression with suicidality, and psychotic symptoms about the infant, raise the risk);
- harm to others.

**Rating scales (Table 5):** YMRS, PANSS, BPRS, **NIMBUS** (NIMHANS Maternal Behaviour Scale), **PBQ** (bonding), and the **Stafford Interview** (in MBUs and research).

### Management [1, 2]
**Setting:**
- PPP often needs **in-patient care** because of suicide or infant-harm risk [1].
- Under the **MHCA 2017 (Section 21)**:
  - **rooming-in** of mother and child (under 3 years) is recommended when risk is low;
  - if they are separated, review the separation **every 15 days**;
  - for separation beyond **30 days**, inform the **Mental Health Review Board** and seek permission [1].
- **Mother–baby units:** at least 4 beds, with a multidisciplinary team [1].

**Pharmacological** [1]:
- First treat the **specific underlying disorder**, after excluding medical mimics.
- **Mainstay: a second-generation antipsychotic compatible with breastfeeding.**
- **Psychotic depression:** add an antidepressant.
- **Mania:** add a mood stabiliser (lithium) if there is no response to the SGA alone, or if she is already doing well on lithium.
- **Catatonia:**
  - **lorazepam 3–8 mg/day** (1–2 mg three or four times a day), increasing to **8–16 mg/day** if response is inadequate after 24–48 hours;
  - withhold doses if pulse is under 60, BP under 90/60, or fetal heart rate under 100 (in pregnancy);
  - **ECT** if there is no response;
  - catatonia brings risks of dehydration, hypoglycaemia, electrolyte imbalance and **DVT**, so lorazepam's benefits usually outweigh its risks.
- **Agitation:**
  - verbal de-escalation first;
  - then oral lorazepam 1–2 mg, promethazine 25–50 mg, olanzapine 5–10 mg or chlorpromazine 50–100 mg;
  - then IM lorazepam 2 mg, promethazine, haloperidol 2.5–5 mg (with promethazine) or olanzapine 10 mg.

**Lactation [1]:**
- Encourage exclusive, on-demand breastfeeding. There is **no need to time feeds** around doses, because levels reach steady state.
- Prefer drugs with a relative infant dose (RID) **under 10%**.
- **Clozapine is contraindicated** in breastfeeding.
- **Lithium** needs infant monitoring, including investigations; it has an RID of 12–30%, and some advise avoiding breastfeeding.
- **Monitor the infant** for sedation, floppiness or poor suck (antipsychotics and sedatives).

**ECT [1]:** life-saving in **catatonia or severe suicidality** that does not respond to medication. **Postpartum:** feed the infant first, schedule her **early on the ECT list**, and keep expressed milk available.

**Suicide risk (Box 3) [1]:**
- high-intensity care with **temporary separation** from the infant until risk falls;
- maintain breastfeeding with expressed milk;
- ECT;
- **eyeball-to-eyeball** monitoring; **twice-daily risk assessment**; a safe environment.

**Bonding [1]:**
- education, infant-care training, modelling, **video feedback**, mother–infant psychotherapy;
- an additional caregiver (grandmother or father).

**Prevention and relapse [1, 2]:**
- In bipolar disorder or schizophrenia, **continue prophylaxis** through the perinatal period; an SGA is preferred.
- **Lithium:**
  - levels **fall** with the rise in GFR during pregnancy and **return abruptly** after delivery;
  - check levels monthly until 34 weeks, then weekly;
  - check before and 24 hours after delivery, and **frequently in the first postpartum weeks** [1].
- **Relapse prevention:** avoid sleep deprivation, manage stress, keep adherence, avoid substances, and monitor early warning signs [1].

### Newer developments described in the sources [1, 2]
- **Indian MBU model** (Chandra et al., 2015) and the MHCA 2017 provisions for **joint admission**.
- **NIMBUS:** a validated Indian scale of maternal behaviour in severe postpartum mental illness.
- The **Stafford Interview** and the **PBQ** (Tamil validation) for assessing bonding.
- **Catatonia** is now recognised as common in PPP; lorazepam dosing and monitoring protocols exist.
- **Pregnancy and Lactation Labelling Rule (2015)** has replaced the FDA A/B/C/D/X categories.
- **Relative infant dose** is the guide to breastfeeding compatibility.

**Conclusion.** PPP is an abrupt-onset psychiatric emergency, usually affective in nature. Management:
- exclude organic causes and assess suicide and infanticide risk;
- admit, ideally to a mother–baby unit;
- treat with a breastfeeding-compatible SGA (plus a mood stabiliser or antidepressant), lorazepam or ECT for catatonia;
- support bonding;
- give prophylaxis to high-risk women.

## In depth

### Psychopathology relating to the infant (Table 4) [1]
| Psychopathology | Usual context | Implication |
|---|---|---|
| Infant dead, defective, or the death being hidden | Psychotic depression, acute psychosis | Associated maternal medical conditions |
| Infant divine or special; others will steal it | Mania, acute psychosis | Clinging; rough handling |
| Command hallucinations | Schizophrenia, psychotic depression | Harm, or over-protectiveness |
| Negative symptoms | Schizophrenia | Emotional neglect; under-stimulation |
| Agitation, anger, abuse | Borderline states, complex trauma | Bonding disorders |
