## Exam answer

### Definition and nosology [1]
- Not a separate DSM-5-TR diagnosis. It is coded as a **mood episode with psychotic features "with peripartum onset"** (onset in pregnancy or **within 4 weeks of delivery**), or **brief psychotic disorder "with peripartum onset"**.
- "Postpartum psychosis" **typically resembles a manic or mixed episode with psychotic symptoms** and is **strongly associated with bipolar I disorder** [1].

### Epidemiology and risk [1, 2]
- **Incidence:**
  - **1 in 500 to 1 in 1000 deliveries**; perhaps **more common in first-time mothers** [1];
  - **0.1–0.25%** in the general population (about 1–2 psychiatric admissions per 1000 births) [2].
- **Risk factors:**
  - **previous postpartum psychosis**;
  - **bipolar disorder (especially bipolar I)**: about **1 in 5** relapse with psychosis postpartum [2];
  - prior depressive disorder;
  - **family history** of bipolar disorder or postpartum psychosis;
  - **psychosis during pregnancy** (pregnancy does **not** protect against relapse) [2];
  - **stopping mood stabilisers**: lithium stopped before conception gives postpartum relapse **up to 70%** [2].
- **Combined high risk** (previous episode, bipolar I or schizoaffective disorder, and family loading): **up to 50%** [2].
- **Recurrence after one postpartum psychotic episode: 30–50% with each subsequent delivery** [1].

### Clinical features [1, 2]
- Onset after delivery (the specifier requires onset within 4 weeks):
  - **mania or mixed state** with **delusions and hallucinations** [1];
  - confusion-like presentations must be separated from delirium;
  - **catatonia** is recognised in postpartum psychosis [2].
- **Infant-related psychotic content:** **command hallucinations to kill the infant** or **delusions that the infant is possessed**. Infanticide is rare but most often linked to such postpartum psychotic episodes [1].
- **Risks of not treating** [2]:
  - **to the mother:** self-neglect, suicide;
  - **to the baby:** **neglect through to infanticide**.

### Assessment [1, 2]
1. **History:**
   - timing of onset relative to delivery;
   - **previous postpartum or bipolar episodes**; **family history**;
   - **recent discontinuation of medication** (lithium, antipsychotics);
   - substance use.
2. **Mental state:**
   - mood (manic, mixed, depressive), psychotic features, **content about the infant**;
   - **suicidal and infanticidal ideation**;
   - **catatonic signs**; level of awareness.
3. **Differential diagnosis:**
   - **postpartum delirium**: distinguished by a **fluctuating level of awareness or attention** [1];
   - **depressive disorder due to another medical condition**, e.g., **thyroid abnormalities** [1];
   - severe non-psychotic postpartum depression;
   - **maternity blues** (benign, within a week) [1].
4. **Risk assessment** for mother and infant; **capacity to care for the baby**.
5. **Medication history in pregnancy** (to guide drug choice and breastfeeding advice) [2].

### Management [2]
**Setting and team:**
- treat promptly; maternal treatment is **the priority**;
- refer to **specialist perinatal services**;
- liaise with obstetrics and neonatology;
- involve the parents in decisions; **document all decisions** (Box 7.1).

**Acute drug treatment:**
- **an antipsychotic** for acute mania or psychosis (Box 7.4). Use the antipsychotic that **previously worked best**, at the **lowest effective dose**, with **minimal polypharmacy** (Box 7.2);
- **olanzapine or quetiapine** if starting a drug while breastfeeding (Table 7.1);
- **lithium:**
  - may be the most effective mood stabiliser for an individual;
  - **if lithium is used, advise against breastfeeding** (infant levels up to 57% of maternal);
  - lithium requirements **return abruptly to pre-pregnancy levels after delivery**, so levels and fluid balance need close monitoring;
- **avoid valproate** in women of child-bearing potential (UK: two-specialist rule; contraception).

**ECT:**
- if the antipsychotic is ineffective in mania;
- NICE: for severe mania, mixed states, severe depression or **catatonia** where physical health is at serious risk (p. 722).

**Catatonia:** recognised in postpartum psychosis [2]. Prompt treatment prevents dehydration, venous thrombosis, pulmonary embolism and pneumonia.

**Breastfeeding:**
- may continue with most antipsychotics;
- **not with clozapine or lithium**;
- with sedating drugs, **no breastfeeding in bed**; monitor mother–infant interaction.

### Prevention in high-risk women [2]
- **Pre-conception counselling** by a perinatal psychiatrist; a **written perinatal care plan**.
- **Do not stop** effective lithium or antipsychotics abruptly; stopping raises pre- and postpartum relapse.
- In pregnancy:
  - continue the effective antipsychotic or a mood-stabilising antipsychotic (NICE preference);
  - lithium with fetal echocardiography and levels every 4 weeks, then weekly from 36 weeks;
  - deliver in hospital; **stop lithium in labour**; check the level 12 hours after the last dose.
- Close **postpartum monitoring**, the period of greatest risk.

### Update from IPS CPG 2022 [3]
**Definition:**
- an SMI of **abrupt onset postpartum, usually within 4–6 weeks** of childbirth;
- presents as **acute mania** (with psychotic symptoms), **acute and transient psychotic disorder**, **catatonia** or **psychotic depression** (Table 2).

**Assessment:**
- **Rule out medical mimics** (Table 3):
  - infection; electrolytes; uraemia; glucose or DKA; anaemia; **thyroid function and anti-thyroid antibodies**; liver function and ammonia; urine drug screen;
  - **CT** (avoided in pregnancy) or **MRI brain**, **EEG**, **CSF**;
  - for **PRES, cortical venous thrombosis, Sheehan syndrome, Wernicke encephalopathy, ADEM/MS and anti-NMDA-receptor encephalitis**.
- **Confusion, perplexity or disorientation** point to a medical or neurological cause.
- **Infant-related psychopathology** (Table 4):
  - delusions that the **fetus is dead**, that the infant is ill or has died with the fact hidden, or that the infant is **divine** or will be stolen;
  - command hallucinations;
  - these drive **infanticidal risk**.
- **Postpartum mania** can cause **rough handling** of the infant, and verbal or physical aggression.

**Management:**
- **In-patient care** is often needed (suicide or infant-harm risk).
- **Mainstay:** an **SGA compatible with breastfeeding**.
  - Add an **antidepressant** for psychotic depression and a **mood stabiliser (lithium)** for mania if the SGA fails (Table 9).
- **Catatonia** is common: **about 20%** of perinatal admissions vs about 8% of general acute admissions.
  - **Lorazepam 3–8 mg/day** (1–2 mg three or four times daily), rising to **8–16 mg/day** if the response is inadequate at 24–48 hours.
  - **Withhold** a dose if maternal **pulse is under 60**, **BP is under 90/60** or **fetal heart rate is under 100**.
  - Its benefits usually exceed the risks: food refusal, dehydration and **DVT** from immobility.
  - **ECT** if lorazepam fails.
- **ECT postpartum:** feed the infant **before** the procedure, **schedule the mother first** on the list, and have **expressed milk** available (Box 4).
- **Suicidal mothers** (Box 3):
  - temporary separation from the infant in a high-intensity unit, with **expressed breast milk** or infant visits, and joint admission once the risk falls;
  - ECT;
  - **eyeball-to-eyeball** family monitoring;
  - **twice-daily** risk assessment;
  - no sharps.
- **Bonding:** education, infant-care training, modelling, **video feedback**, and **mother–infant psychotherapy** if severe. An extra caregiver (grandmother or father) helps.
- **MHCA 2017:**
  - least restrictive care; **rooming-in** of mother and child (under 3 years) under **Section 21**;
  - review any separation **every 15 days**; inform the **Mental Health Review Board** beyond **30 days**.
- **Mother–baby units:** at least **4 beds** with rooming-in, run by a multidisciplinary team. India needs more.

⚠️ **Discrepancy (not resolved):** IPS Table 2 gives PPP as **"12/1000 live births"**. DSM-5-TR gives 1 in 500 to 1 in 1000, and Maudsley gives 0.1–0.25% (about 1–2 per 1000). The IPS figure is about **ten times higher** and may be a misprint for "1–2/1000". Please verify; it is not corrected here.

**Conclusion.** Postpartum psychosis (1–2 per 1000 births) is usually a manic or mixed episode with psychosis, closely tied to bipolar I disorder. Risk reaches about 50% with a previous episode, bipolar I or schizoaffective disorder, and a family history, and it recurs in 30–50% of later deliveries. Assessment must exclude delirium and medical (e.g., thyroid) causes and assess the risks of suicide and infanticide. Management is an urgent psychiatric emergency:
- specialist perinatal care;
- an antipsychotic (olanzapine or quetiapine if breastfeeding), with lithium as an option but no breastfeeding on lithium;
- ECT for refractory illness or catatonia;
- careful planning in future pregnancies so that effective prophylaxis is not stopped.

### Not covered by the sources (deliberately left blank)
