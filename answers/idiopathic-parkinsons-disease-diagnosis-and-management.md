## Exam answer

**Introduction.** Parkinsonism is defined by four cardinal deficits [1]:
- **tremor**;
- **rigidity**;
- **bradykinesia**;
- **postural abnormality/instability**.

**Idiopathic PD** (paralysis agitans; James Parkinson, 1817) is by far the commonest cause. It results from degeneration of pigmented brainstem neurones, especially the **substantia nigra pars compacta**.

### Epidemiology and genetics [1]
- **Risk:** lifetime risk about 2%, doubled with a positive family history.
- **Sex:** slightly more common in men.
- **Onset:** mean about 55 years.
- **Genetic contribution:** more likely if onset is before 50.

| Gene | Inheritance and notes |
| --- | --- |
| **α-synuclein** (PARK1, chromosome 4) | AD; rare families. Key to pathogenesis (Lewy bodies) |
| **Parkin** (PARK2, chromosome 6) | Up to 50% of early-onset AR cases |
| **LRRK2** (PARK8) | AD; up to 6% of apparently sporadic PD |

- **Pathogenesis:** failure of the **ubiquitin–proteasome system** leads to α-synuclein aggregation (Lewy bodies) and apoptosis.
- **Environmental factors:** MPTP and other toxins; **mitochondrial complex I deficiency** in the nigra.
- **Smoking appears protective.**

### Pathology [1]
- **Nigral pallor**: loss of pars compacta neurones.
- **Lewy bodies:** eosinophilic inclusions of α-synuclein and ubiquitin, the hallmark. They are also found in the locus caeruleus, raphe, dorsal vagal nucleus, nucleus basalis and, in up to one-third, the cortex.
- **Braak stages 1–6:** symptoms appear only after about **70% neuronal loss**.
- **Striatal dopamine** falls to 10–20% of normal, with a cholinergic–dopaminergic imbalance.
- **Non-dopaminergic loss:** 5-HT (raphe), noradrenaline (locus caeruleus) and acetylcholine (nucleus basalis). This underlies the non-motor features.

### Clinical features: motor [1]

| Feature | Characteristics |
| --- | --- |
| **Tremor** (presenting feature in about 75%) | 4–6 Hz, **at rest**, flexion–extension at the metacarpophalangeal joints of fingers and thumb; may involve jaw and tongue; worse with anxiety and fatigue; absent in sleep; often unilateral at first |
| **Rigidity** | Lead-pipe ("plastic") through the full range; **cogwheel** when tremor is superimposed; persists in sleep |
| **Bradykinesia** (often the most disabling) | Slow initiation and execution; reduced arm swing; **hypomimia**; reduced blinking; crabbed (small) handwriting; monotonous speech with disordered prosody. Strongly modulated by emotion: severely disabled patients may move surprisingly well with fear or excitement |
| **Posture and gait** | Flexed posture; shuffling **festinant** gait; trouble starting and turning; **freezing**; postural instability with falls |

### Clinical features: non-motor (asked for in the question) [1, 2]
1. **Neuropsychiatric** [1, 2]:
   - depression (about 40%);
   - anxiety (about 25%);
   - apathy;
   - dementia (risk two- to sixfold);
   - psychosis (mostly drug-related);
   - personality change;
   - dopamine dysregulation;
   - bradyphrenia.
   - Depression and anxiety **often precede motor signs**.
2. **Olfactory:** **hyposmia in 70–100%**, which can precede motor signs by over a year and so aids early diagnosis.
3. **Autonomic:**
   - **constipation** (major and distressing);
   - urinary frequency, urgency and incontinence;
   - sialorrhoea (from dysphagia);
   - seborrhoea;
   - postural hypotension.
4. **Sleep:** vivid dreams (about 25%), **REM sleep behaviour disorder**, daytime REM intrusions.
5. **Sensory:** pain, tightness and cramps.
6. **Fatigue:** marked, and often independent of motor severity and depression.
7. **Oculomotor:** reduced blinking; convergence paresis.

### Diagnosis [1]
**It is clinical.** Useful early signs:
- difficulty with repetitive rhythmic tapping;
- handwriting change; spiral drawing;
- **glabellar tap**: blinking continues with each tap and fails to habituate;
- reduced arm swing, difficulty turning, tremor brought out by walking.

**Investigations:**
- **DAT-SPECT/PET** (tropane tracers; ¹⁸F-dopa PET):
  - reduced presynaptic uptake, posterior putamen worst;
  - about **90% sensitivity** for distinguishing early PD from normal subjects and **essential tremor**;
  - a normal scan does not reliably exclude PD.
- **Exclusions:**
  - drug-induced parkinsonism (neuroleptics, reserpine, methyldopa);
  - **Wilson's disease** if onset is young (caeruloplasmin, slit lamp);
  - imaging for structural causes.
- **Levodopa response:** a poor response (about 15% non-responders) suggests **PSP or MSA**.

**Diagnostic accuracy:** in the classic autopsy study (Hughes 1992), **24% of clinically diagnosed PD was wrong** (PSP, MSA, Alzheimer's, vascular). A later series found 83% accuracy.

**Differential diagnosis:** see the separate answer on the differential diagnosis of parkinsonism. Key points:
- essential tremor;
- drug-induced parkinsonism;
- **depression and myxoedema** (bradykinesia);
- Alzheimer's or DLB;
- PSP, MSA, CBD;
- vascular parkinsonism;
- Wilson's disease;
- akinetic-rigid Huntington's;
- "hysteria" (when early fluctuations occur).

### Management [1]

**General measures:**
- keep the patient active; **physiotherapy**, mechanical aids;
- attend to psychosocial stress and depression, which worsen disability.

**Drugs:**

| Class | Notes |
| --- | --- |
| **Levodopa + peripheral decarboxylase inhibitor** (carbidopa: Sinemet; benserazide: Madopar) | **Treatment of choice** if tolerated. One-third marked, one-third moderate and one-third modest benefit; about 15% no response. Best for **bradykinesia and postural instability**; also rigidity; tremor less consistent. Give a 6–12-month trial. ELLDOPA: no evidence of toxicity. Adverse effects: nausea, hypotension, dyskinesias, psychiatric |
| **Dopamine agonists** (pramipexole, ropinirole; ergot: bromocriptine, pergolide, cabergoline) | Early monotherapy **delays dyskinesias** but gives less benefit than levodopa. Adjunct: reduces "off" time. Ergots cause fibrosis and valvulopathy. **Psychosis two to three times more than with levodopa** |
| **Apomorphine** (subcutaneous, with domperidone) | Rescue for refractory "off" periods (relief within 15 minutes, lasting up to 1 hour) or pump infusion |
| **MAO-B inhibitors** (selegiline, rasagiline) | Modest effect; potentiate levodopa. DATATOP's "neuroprotection" was disputed; no excess mortality on meta-analysis |
| **COMT inhibitor** (entacapone) | Adjunct; prolongs levodopa; no titration needed; combined tablet available. Tolcapone withdrawn |
| **Amantadine** | Mild disease; helps bradykinesia and rigidity; **reduces levodopa dyskinesia** (short-lived). Hallucinations and confusion |
| **Anticholinergics** (trihexyphenidyl, benzatropine, procyclidine) | Mild cases or tremor; little effect on bradykinesia. **Avoid in the elderly or cognitively impaired** (confusion, psychosis) |

**Long-term levodopa complications:**
- **Motor fluctuations:**
  - early-morning akinesia;
  - **wearing off** (end-of-dose);
  - **on–off** swings.
- **Dyskinesias:**
  - peak-dose chorea or dystonia;
  - diphasic dyskinesia;
  - off-period painful foot dystonia.
- Motor complications accrue at about **10% per year**.
- After 5 years: one-third keep their benefit, one-third lose some, one-third lose all.
- **Management:**
  - smaller, more frequent doses or sustained-release preparations;
  - add an agonist, MAO-B inhibitor or entacapone;
  - amantadine for dyskinesia;
  - apomorphine.
- **Non-motor fluctuations:** mood and psychosis can swing with on–off states.

**Surgery** (best for levodopa-responsive idiopathic PD):
- **thalamotomy or thalamic (VIM) DBS** for tremor;
- **pallidotomy** for contralateral tremor, rigidity, bradykinesia and drug-induced dyskinesias (two RCTs);
- bilateral lesions risk speech, swallowing and cognitive problems;
- **thalamic stimulation** is as effective as thalamotomy for tremor, with fewer adverse effects, and its side effects can be reversed;
- fetal or adrenal transplantation is experimental.

**Course:**
- Progressive.
- Life expectancy near-normal when well treated (mortality ratio about 1.6; mean about 14 years from diagnosis).
- **Worse prognosis:** age, **dementia, depression**, poor levodopa response.

**Conclusion.** Idiopathic PD:
- is a clinical diagnosis: rest tremor, rigidity, bradykinesia, postural instability, plus levodopa response;
- is supported by DAT imaging, after excluding drug-induced, vascular, Wilson's and atypical parkinsonism.

Treatment is levodopa-based, with agonists, MAO-B or COMT inhibitors and amantadine for complications, and surgery for refractory cases. Non-motor features (mood, cognition, psychosis, sleep, smell, autonomic) are common and often determine quality of life.

## In depth

### Treatment of non-motor psychiatric features [2]
- **Depression:** SSRI (sertraline); SNRI; agonist; ECT.
- **Psychosis:** reduce drugs; quetiapine trial; **clozapine** (6.25–35 mg); pimavanserin.
- **Dementia:** cholinesterase inhibitors.

See the separate answer on psychiatric aspects of PD.
