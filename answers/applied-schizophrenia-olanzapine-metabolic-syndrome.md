## Exam answer

**Case summary.** A 35-year-old man with 8 years of **paranoid schizophrenia** (persecutory and referential delusions, somatic passivity, second-person auditory hallucinations; no mood symptoms) responded well to olanzapine 20 mg/day. He developed increased sleep and appetite and his BMI rose from **24.8 to 32.1 kg/m²** (a gain of about 29%, now in the obese range by both WHO and Asian cut-offs). He stopped the drug because of these effects and relapsed.

### (a) What is metabolic syndrome? What assessments and investigations would you do in this patient?

**Definition.** A cluster of central obesity, dyslipidaemia, hypertension and dysglycaemia that predicts type 2 diabetes and cardiovascular disease. Harmonised/IDF criteria (South Asian): waist ≥90 cm (men), plus two or more of: triglycerides ≥150 mg/dL; HDL <40 mg/dL; BP ≥130/85 or on treatment; fasting glucose ≥100 mg/dL or diabetes [1,4].

**Assessment in this patient**

| Domain | Specific points |
| --- | --- |
| History | Time course of weight gain and its relation to olanzapine dose; appetite, night eating, sugary drinks; activity level; sleep (snoring, daytime sleepiness — obstructive sleep apnoea); smoking and alcohol; polyuria/polydipsia; sexual dysfunction; family history of diabetes, premature CVD; other drugs (valproate, mirtazapine) |
| Psychiatric | Current relapse severity (PANSS), risk, insight, attitude to medication, reasons for stopping (weight was the main one); previous drug trials and responses |
| Examination | Weight, height, BMI, **waist circumference**, BP (both arms), pulse; acanthosis nigricans; fundus; peripheral pulses; thyroid; signs of liver disease |
| Investigations | Fasting glucose and **HbA1c**; fasting lipid profile; LFT (NAFLD; ALT); renal function and eGFR (before metformin); TSH; serum prolactin; ECG (QTc before any switch); urine albumin if diabetic; vitamin B12 (baseline before metformin); consider uric acid; sleep study if OSA is suspected |
| Risk score | 10-year CVD risk (QRISK3 or WHO/ISH chart) |

### (b) Relationship between metabolic syndrome and psychosis. What lifestyle modifications would you recommend?

**The relationship is bidirectional and multifactorial:**
1. **Illness-related:** possible intrinsic insulin resistance and impaired glucose tolerance in drug-naïve first-episode patients; HPA overactivity (cortisol) from stress; inflammation; genetic overlap.
2. **Lifestyle-related:** negative symptoms and amotivation → inactivity; poor diet; smoking (60–65%); alcohol; poverty; disrupted sleep [1].
3. **Drug-related:** H1 and 5-HT2C blockade → hyperphagia; M3 blockade → impaired insulin secretion; direct insulin resistance and dyslipidaemia (olanzapine, clozapine), sometimes before weight gain [1].
4. **Consequences:** weight gain → non-adherence (as here) → relapse; stigma and low self-esteem; CVD → 15–20-year mortality gap.
5. Metabolic syndrome is associated with worse cognition in schizophrenia [2].

**Lifestyle plan for this patient**
- **Psychoeducation:** explain that weight gain is a side effect that can be managed, so he does not need to stop treatment. Use motivational interviewing.
- **Diet** (culturally adapted Indian diet): regular meals, smaller portions of rice/roti, more dal, vegetables and protein; cut sugar, sweets, fried snacks, sugary tea and soft drinks; avoid late-night eating; involve the family member who cooks.
- **Physical activity:** brisk walking 30 minutes, 5 days a week (150 minutes/week), building up gradually; yoga; group exercise.
- **Sleep:** regular schedule; reduce daytime napping (sedation also reduces as the drug is changed).
- **Smoking and alcohol:** cessation support (NRT, varenicline). If he smokes and is later on clozapine/olanzapine, monitor drug levels when he quits [1].
- **Structure:** a behavioural lifestyle programme with regular weighing and booster sessions; BAP data show about −3 kg vs control, with benefits fading unless sustained [1].
- **Targets:** 5–10% weight loss over 6 months; waist under 90 cm.

### (c) Relationship between antipsychotics and metabolic syndrome. Which antipsychotic would you prefer?

| Liability | Drugs |
| --- | --- |
| High | Clozapine, olanzapine |
| Medium | Quetiapine, risperidone, paliperidone, iloperidone, asenapine |
| Low | Aripiprazole, brexpiprazole, cariprazine, lurasidone, ziprasidone, amisulpride, haloperidol |
| None/minimal (non-D2) | Xanomeline–trospium (≥7% weight gain RR 0.46 vs placebo; lowers LDL; GI effects and raised heart rate) [3] |

Sources: [1,3,5].

**Preferred choice for this patient: aripiprazole** (10–15 mg/day, maximum 30 mg).
- Good evidence for weight-neutral effect and improved lipids and glucose after switching from olanzapine [1].
- Effective for positive symptoms; low prolactin; available as a monthly or 2-monthly LAI. This matters because he has relapsed after non-adherence.
- **Alternatives:** lurasidone 40–160 mg with food (weight-neutral); amisulpride (low metabolic risk, but raises prolactin); cariprazine.

**Plan**
- Re-start an antipsychotic now: he is relapsing.
- If he needs rapid control, consider cross-titration; otherwise start aripiprazole directly.
- Once stable, offer **aripiprazole LAI** (400 mg monthly after 14 days of oral overlap, or a 2-day initiation regimen).
- **Add metformin** given his BMI gain (>3% in a year and BMI ≥30): 500 mg once → 1 g twice daily over 6 weeks, checking eGFR [4].
- If BMI stays ≥30 despite this, a **GLP-1 agonist** (semaglutide/liraglutide) [4].
- Treat each metabolic component (statin, antihypertensive) according to risk.

**If aripiprazole fails:** olanzapine was effective, so consider lurasidone or amisulpride. Treatment resistance is not established here, so clozapine is not yet indicated. If olanzapine is truly the only effective drug, restart it at the lowest effective dose with co-prescribed metformin from the start, or adjunctive aripiprazole (about −2 kg) [1]. In countries where it is available, olanzapine–samidorphan is another option.

**Conclusion.** This is a classic, preventable cycle: effective drug → metabolic harm → non-adherence → relapse. The answer is to keep antipsychotic treatment but change to a metabolically benign drug (aripiprazole, ideally as an LAI), add metformin and a structured lifestyle programme, and monitor regularly. Making metabolic health part of the psychiatric treatment plan prevents the next relapse.

## In depth

### Background
The olanzapine dilemma is well known: olanzapine is among the most effective non-clozapine antipsychotics in network meta-analyses (CATIE: longest time to discontinuation) but causes the most weight gain after clozapine. This trade-off drove the development of metformin co-prescription guidance (2025) and combination products (olanzapine–samidorphan) [1,4].

### Epidemiology
- Olanzapine: mean gain about 4–5 kg at 10 weeks and about 10 kg over 1 year in some studies. Youth gain more (SATIETY: +8.5 kg in 11 weeks).
- About 80% of people on antipsychotics gain weight; low and normal BMI at baseline predicts larger gain [4].
- About 30–35% of patients on long-term antipsychotics meet metabolic syndrome criteria.
- Non-adherence due to weight gain: weight gain is one of the most-cited reasons for stopping; about 50% are non-adherent by 1 year [1].

### Classification and criteria (applied)
- **Clinical:** ICD-11 6A20 Schizophrenia, multiple episodes, currently symptomatic; symptom specifiers (positive). DSM-5-TR schizophrenia, multiple episodes, currently in acute episode.
- **Metabolic:** ICD-11 5A44 Metabolic syndrome; 5B81 Obesity; drug-induced codes for adverse effects.
- **Indian BMI categories:** 23–24.9 overweight; ≥25 obese. This patient is obese class II by Asian criteria.

### Mechanism in detail
Olanzapine has high affinity for H1 (Ki ≈ 0.1–7 nM), 5-HT2C and M1–M5. It causes rapid insulin resistance (euglycaemic clamp studies show this within 1–2 weeks), increases hepatic lipogenesis (SREBP) and raises leptin with leptin resistance. Aripiprazole is a D2/5-HT1A partial agonist with weak H1 affinity, which accounts for its metabolic neutrality. Xanomeline acts through M1/M4 agonism with no D2 or H1 blockade [3].

### Clinical features in detail — the relapse
Relapse after stopping is typical: discontinuation leads to relapse in about 60–80% within a year in multi-episode illness [2]. Abrupt cessation can also cause withdrawal-associated relapse (dopamine supersensitivity) and cholinergic rebound (insomnia, agitation, nausea) [2]. Assess suicide risk, violence and need for admission. Under MHCA 2017, independent admission is preferred; use supported admission (Sec 89) if criteria are met.

### Assessment in detail
- **Adherence and attitude:** Drug Attitude Inventory (DAI-10), Brief Adherence Rating Scale.
- **Side-effect burden:** GASS or LUNSERS, to show him that side effects are taken seriously.
- **Sexual dysfunction and prolactin** before switching (aripiprazole lowers prolactin).
- **Eating behaviour:** screen for binge eating and night eating.
- **Physical:** fasting labs as above; ECG before ziprasidone or high doses.

### Differential diagnosis
- **Of the psychosis:** schizophrenia (most likely); schizoaffective disorder (no mood symptoms reported, so unlikely); delusional disorder (excluded by hallucinations and passivity); substance-induced relapse (check cannabis and alcohol); organic causes if the presentation changes.
- **Of the weight gain:** drug effect; hypothyroidism; binge eating; reduced activity; Cushing's (unlikely). See also the metabolic syndrome answer for the full differential.

### Management details
- **Switch technique:** with olanzapine stopped and the patient relapsing, start aripiprazole 10 mg and increase to 15–20 mg. Add short-term lorazepam for agitation and sleep. Watch for akathisia (propranolol 10–20 mg TDS if needed).
- **LAI:** aripiprazole monohydrate 400 mg monthly (300 mg if poor CYP2D6 metaboliser or on CYP2D6 inhibitors); aripiprazole lauroxil; 2-monthly aripiprazole (960 mg).
- **Metformin:** see dose schedule above; stop it if he develops acute illness or dehydration (sick-day rule); check B12 yearly [4].
- **GLP-1:** semaglutide 0.25 mg weekly, titrated to 1–2.4 mg. Counsel on nausea.
- **Monitoring:** weight monthly for 3 months then 3-monthly; HbA1c and lipids at 3 months then yearly [1].
- **Psychosocial:** family psychoeducation; relapse signature plan; CBTp for persistent delusions; vocational support.
- **If adherence remains poor despite a good drug:** consider the reasons (insight, stigma), use adherence therapy, and involve the nominated representative.

### Critical appraisal
- Switching from an effective drug risks relapse. Evidence suggests switching to aripiprazole maintains symptom control in most patients, but some lose response. Monitor closely [1].
- Metformin evidence is moderate, with heterogeneous trials, many from China [1]. The 2025 guideline makes a strong practical recommendation for co-commencement [4].
- GLP-1 agonists are promising but expensive in India; generic liraglutide and semaglutide are becoming available.
- Lifestyle programmes work modestly and fade without boosters [1].
- Patient choice and dignity: he stopped because of weight, a rational concern that should be respected and addressed rather than labelled as "lack of insight".

### Indian context
- High background diabetes prevalence (ICMR-INDIAB ≈11%) and central obesity at lower BMI.
- Aripiprazole and metformin are inexpensive generics in India. Aripiprazole LAI is available but costly; generic versions are increasing.
- Community health workers and NCD clinics (Ayushman Arogya Mandirs) can support BP and glucose monitoring.
- Family involvement in diet (the family member who cooks) is key.
- Rights under MHCA 2017: informed consent for the switch and the LAI; advance directive if he wishes.

### Recent advances
- 2025 metformin guideline: co-commence metformin with high-risk antipsychotics [4].
- Xanomeline–trospium (approved in the US 2024; check Indian status) offers a weight-neutral option [3].
- Olanzapine–samidorphan.
- GLP-1 receptor agonists and tirzepatide (GIP/GLP-1) in severe mental illness: trials ongoing.
