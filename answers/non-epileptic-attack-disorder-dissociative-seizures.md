## Exam answer

### Definition and terminology [1, 2]
- **Definition:** paroxysmal episodes resembling epileptic seizures (or syncope), **due to psychological processes believed to be unconscious**, without epileptiform discharge [1].
- **Terms:**
  - ICD-10: "dissociative convulsions";
  - **DSM-5-TR:** **functional neurological symptom disorder (conversion disorder), with attacks or seizures (F44.5)** [2].
- **Choice of name:**
  - "Pseudoseizures" and "hysterical seizures" are **pejorative** and abandoned.
  - "Non-epileptic attack disorder" and "PNES" define the disorder by what it is *not*.
  - **"Functional seizures"** is acceptable to patients and neutral about conscious or unconscious mechanisms [1].
- **Spectrum of awareness:**
  - **dissociative** (unconscious) → **"self-deception"** (middle ground) → **factitious** (conscious, sick-role motive) → **malingering** (external gain; not a diagnosis);
  - these are better seen as **dimensions** than categories [1].
- **Evidence that most attacks are unconscious:**
  - adherence to ASMs before diagnosis;
  - attacks occur during telemetry;
  - attacks are poor imitations of epilepsy [1].

### Epidemiology [1, 2, 3]
- **About 1 in 5** referrals to specialist centres with "intractable epilepsy"; similar in new-onset seizure clinics [1].
- **Female over two-thirds** [1]; FND overall two to three times more common in women [2].
- **Onset:** late teens or early 20s; DSM peak **20–29 years** [1, 2].
- **Comorbid epilepsy:**
  - fewer than 20% have epilepsy preceding the attacks [1];
  - **about 1 in 5** with functional seizures also have epilepsy [2];
  - 7–32% [3].
- **Diagnostic delay:**
  - typically **more than 3 years**;
  - iatrogenic harm: ASM toxicity, teratogenicity, emergency "status" treatment in up to 10% [1].

### Aetiology: formulation [1, 2]

| | Biological | Psychological | Social |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Predisposing** | Learning difficulties; ?genetic personality influences; ?effect of early adversity on brain development | Adverse childhood experience; **somatising and dissociative traits**; avoidant coping; **personality disorder** (emotional instability [2]); mood disorder | **Childhood abuse** (sexual, physical, emotional), bullying, poor family functioning; adult trauma; **modelling** (relatives with epilepsy) |
| **Precipitating** | — | Life events perceived as negative; **an initial panic attack or vasovagal syncope** | Adverse life events (more in the year before onset) |
| **Maintaining** | — | Belief that attacks are beyond control or due to disease; **agoraphobic avoidance and safety behaviours**; angry or confused reaction to the diagnosis | Carers' reactions; conflicting medical advice; **benefits of the sick role** / fear of responsibilities of being well; stigma of mental illness |

**Further points:**
- **Up to 80%** have previous medically unexplained presentations [1].
- **Stress or trauma may be absent in up to 50%**; do not withhold the diagnosis [2].
- **Mechanism:** a **dissociative response to arousal**. Two-thirds report autonomic arousal (hyperventilation, palpitations, sweating, paraesthesiae) before attacks, but **deny fear**, describing derealisation ("cut off"). This is akin to PTSD-type cue-triggered responses [1].

### Clinical features: distinguishing from epilepsy [1, 2, 3]

| Feature | Dissociative | Epileptic |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Duration over 2 minutes** | Common | Rare |
| **Gradual onset** | Common | Rare |
| **Fluctuating course** | Common | Very rare |
| **Eyes closed** (resisting opening) | Common | Rare |
| **Thrashing, violent movements** | Common | Rare |
| **Side-to-side head movement** | Common | Rare |
| **Asynchronous clonic movements** | Common | Very rare |
| Pelvic thrusting | Occasional | Rare |
| Opisthotonus / arc de cercle | Occasional | Very rare |
| Automatisms | Rare | **Common** |
| **Weeping** | Occasional | Very rare |
| **Recall of the period of unresponsiveness** | Common | Very rare |
| *Unhelpful features*: stereotyped attacks, incontinence, injury, biting inside the mouth, "nocturnal" attacks | Common/occasional | Common |

Additional clues:
- **Prolonged motionless unresponsiveness over 5 minutes**, with recovery, almost never has an organic cause [1]. DSM: duration over 5 minutes; **bilateral movements with preserved awareness**; **persistent eye closure** [2].
- **Injury pattern** [1]:
  - bites to the **tongue tip, cheek or lips**; carpet burns;
  - **severe lateral tongue scarring, posterior shoulder dislocation and vertebral fractures point to epilepsy**.
- **Attacks "in sleep":** EEG shows **waking (pseudosleep) before onset** [1].
- **Attacks in clinic or A&E;** situational triggers; agoraphobic avoidance [1].
- **Bedside tests during an attack** [1]:
  - responsiveness to voice (avoid painful stimuli);
  - **arm-drop test** (the hand avoids the face);
  - **resistance to eye opening**;
  - **Henry–Woodruff sign** (gaze deviates to the ground whichever side the patient lies on);
  - **mirror test** (convergence or fixation; may stop the attack);
  - after a GTCS: absent corneal reflexes and extensor plantars.
- **No single sign is diagnostic.** It is the **deviation from recognised epileptic patterns** that matters [1, 2].

### Investigations [1, 2]
- **Video-EEG telemetry is the gold standard.**
  - Normal ictal EEG with **preserved alpha** in an unresponsive patient; no postictal slowing.
  - **Confirm with an informant that recorded attacks are typical.**
  - **Document every attack type**, because epilepsy and NEAD coexist.
- **Interictal EEG pitfalls:**
  - non-specific abnormalities are **over-represented** in NEAD (and over-read);
  - epileptiform changes appear with learning disability;
  - a normal EEG never excludes epilepsy.
- **Provocation by suggestion:**
  - induces typical attacks in 50–90%;
  - disclosed suggestion with photic stimulation or hyperventilation works in about two-thirds and reduces telemetry need by 47%;
  - placebo methods are ethically problematic.
- **Prolactin:** a modest rise can occur, so a raised value doesn't prove epilepsy, but a **normal 10–20-minute value after an apparent GTCS** supports a non-epileptic event.
- **MRI** if epilepsy remains possible; cardiac work-up if syncope is suspected.
- **Psychiatric assessment:**
  - comorbidity (depression, anxiety and panic, PTSD, personality disorder);
  - trauma history;
  - **suicide risk** (higher in FND [2]).

### Management [1, 2, 3]
**1. Communicating the diagnosis (the most important and most difficult step)** [1]:
- **Explain:**
  - why it is not epilepsy;
  - what it *is* (dissociation; a common, genuine condition);
  - **that the patient is not suspected of "putting it on"**.
- **Causes:** triggers may not be obvious; relevant aetiological factors; the role of maintaining factors.
- **Treatment:**
  - attacks often reduce after the correct diagnosis (especially if recent);
  - **taper ASMs gradually** (if no epilepsy); never stop abruptly;
  - psychotropics have a limited role;
  - describe psychological treatment.
- **Involve carers** (with consent) early.
- Showing telemetry videos is invaluable.

**2. Psychological treatment (mainstay):**
- **CBT modelled on panic disorder and PTSD:**
  - work on arousal cues and dissociation;
  - reduce avoidance and safety behaviours.
  - Rusch: 63% attack-free (short-duration cases). Goldstein: 20% attack-free but better work and social function.
- **Psychodynamic** or insight-oriented, group therapy (especially with childhood trauma).
- **Operant behavioural programmes** in learning disability.
- **There were no controlled trials** (as of 2009) [1].

**3. Treat comorbidity:**
- antidepressants for depression or anxiety;
- **pharmacotherapy otherwise plays a minor role** [1].

**4. Social and system measures:**
- avoid iatrogenic harm (unnecessary emergency "status" treatment, ASM toxicity);
- address the sick role, benefits and occupational issues;
- long-term follow-up for persistent cases to limit unnecessary investigation.

**Prognosis** [1, 2]:
- **Two-thirds still have attacks after 3 years.**
- Even if attacks remit, many stay unemployed and dependent.
- **Best predictor: short duration before diagnosis**; agreement with the diagnosis [2].
- **Poor prognostic factors:** maladaptive personality traits, comorbid physical disease, receipt of disability benefits [2].

### Case: 30-year-old woman with recurrent PNES
*Sudden loss of consciousness, limb thrashing, post-event amnesia; normal EEG and MRI; childhood trauma and health anxiety; neurology suspects FND.*

**1. Diagnosis:** **functional neurological symptom disorder with attacks or seizures (F44.5)**, persistent if 6 months or more [2].
- **Thrashing** with loss of awareness is typical of dissociative seizures [1].
- Childhood trauma (predisposing) and **health anxiety** (somatising tendency; maintaining) fit the formulation [1].
- **Caution:**
  - "post-event amnesia" and a **normal routine EEG/MRI do not by themselves prove NEAD**; the interictal EEG is normal in up to half of people with epilepsy [1];
  - the diagnosis must rest on **positive features** (duration, eye closure, asynchronous thrashing, fluctuation, pelvic thrusting, weeping), ideally with **video-EEG capture of a typical attack** [1, 2].

**2. Further assessment:**
- detailed attack description from an eyewitness or **video** (mobile phone);
- video-EEG telemetry (± disclosed suggestion);
- **exclude coexisting epilepsy** (about 1 in 5) and syncope;
- **psychiatric history:**
  - nature of the childhood trauma; PTSD symptoms; dissociation;
  - depression; **panic** (DSM: loss of awareness with amnesia occurs in functional seizures, not panic [2]);
  - illness anxiety / somatic symptom disorder (may be comorbid);
  - personality; **suicide risk**;
  - substance use (alcohol);
- current ASMs; past medically unexplained symptoms;
- family responses; benefits; life events in the past year;
- **differential:** epilepsy (especially frontal lobe), syncope, panic, factitious disorder or malingering.

**3. Formulation (4 Ps):**
- **Predisposing:** childhood trauma; likely dissociative/somatising traits; health-anxious cognitive style.
- **Precipitating:** to explore: recent stress, a first panic or faint.
- **Perpetuating:**
  - health anxiety and disease attribution;
  - avoidance;
  - medical reinforcement (A&E visits, ASMs, investigations);
  - carers' responses.
- **Protective:** to explore.

**4. Management plan:**
- **Joint neurology–psychiatry explanation** of the diagnosis.
  - Validate that the attacks are real and not feigned.
  - Explain dissociation and its link to trauma and arousal.
- **Gradual ASM withdrawal** if epilepsy is excluded; avoid iatrogenic emergency treatment.
- **CBT modelled on panic and PTSD approaches** [1]:
  - attack-focused (arousal, dissociation, avoidance);
  - also targets the **health anxiety** maintaining factor (disease attribution, avoidance).
  - Psychodynamic therapy (often used with childhood trauma) is an alternative [1].
- **Treat comorbid depression or anxiety** (an antidepressant if indicated).
- **Family psychoeducation.**
- **Long-term follow-up** if attacks persist, to limit further unnecessary investigation [1].
- **Prognosis** is better if treated early and she accepts the diagnosis.

**Conclusion.** NEAD (functional/dissociative seizures):
- is common (about 20% of "refractory epilepsy"), predominantly in young women;
- is often rooted in childhood adversity and maintained by avoidance and medical reinforcement;
- is diagnosed **positively** from semiology (long, fluctuating, asynchronous, eyes closed, recall) and **video-EEG**, remembering that **1 in 5 also have epilepsy**.

Management hinges on **clear, non-judgemental communication of the diagnosis**, gradual ASM withdrawal, and psychological therapy (CBT), with treatment of comorbidity. Outcome is best when diagnosis is early.

## In depth

### Discrepancies between the sources
- **Comorbid epilepsy:** fewer than 20% have prior epilepsy [1]; about 20% also have epilepsy [2]; 7–32% [3].
- **The "over 5 minutes" sign:** DSM-5-TR uses duration over 5 minutes [2]. Lishman uses over 2 minutes for motor attacks, and over 5 minutes for motionless unresponsiveness [1].

### Not covered by the sources (deliberately left blank)
- ILAE (LaFrance 2013) diagnostic levels (possible, probable, clinically established, documented).
- RCT evidence after 2009 (CODES CBT trial; sertraline pilot).
- Paediatric functional seizures.
- Indian epidemiology beyond the slides' 2.9/1,000.
