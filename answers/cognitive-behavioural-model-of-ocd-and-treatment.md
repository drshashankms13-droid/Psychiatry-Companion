## Exam answer

**Introduction.** Obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD) consists of **obsessions** — intrusive thoughts, images or impulses that a person tries to suppress, neutralise or ignore because they raise anxiety — and/or **compulsions** — repetitive overt or covert acts performed to reduce distress or prevent a feared event [1]. Cognitive-behavioural theory explains how ordinary intrusions become obsessions and why rituals maintain the disorder, and leads directly to **exposure and response prevention (ERP)** and **cognitive therapy** [1, 2].

### 1. Behavioural model — Mowrer's two-factor theory (learning theory)

- **Classical conditioning:** a neutral stimulus (e.g. door handle) becomes paired with anxiety (contamination fear).
- **Operant conditioning:** rituals and avoidance reduce anxiety → **negative reinforcement** → rituals strengthen; the person never stays long enough to learn that anxiety falls on its own and the feared outcome does not occur [1, 2].
- (Mowrer attribution: author's knowledge.)

### 2. Cognitive model — appraisal of intrusions (Salkovskis, Rachman)

**Key observation:** most people without OCD have intrusive thoughts; what differs is how they **appraise** them [1].

Normal intrusion ("image of dropping my baby")
→ **catastrophic appraisal** — excessive importance and responsibility ("Having this image means I am a bad person and I will lose control")
→ anxiety, guilt, attention fixed on the intrusion
→ **neutralising** — overt compulsions (washing, checking, ordering) or covert rituals (praying, counting, thought suppression), avoidance, reassurance seeking
→ short-term relief → **negative reinforcement**
→ no disconfirmation of the belief → intrusions become more frequent and salient → **obsession** maintained [1].

### Key dysfunctional beliefs (Obsessive Compulsive Cognitions Working Group)

| Belief domain | Example |
| --- | --- |
| **Inflated responsibility** | "If I don't check, the house will burn and it will be my fault" [1] |
| **Overimportance of thoughts / thought–action fusion** | "Thinking about harming my baby makes it more likely" — **likelihood TAF**; "thinking it is as bad as doing it" — **moral TAF** [1] |
| **Need to control thoughts** | "I must get rid of this thought" — suppression causes rebound |
| **Overestimation of threat** | "Germs on the handle will make me seriously ill" |
| **Intolerance of uncertainty** | "I must be 100% sure the door is locked" |
| **Perfectionism / "not just right"** | "Things must be exactly right" |

K&S also describes **thought–event fusion** (thinking about an event means it will happen or has happened) and **thought–object fusion** (thoughts and feelings can transfer onto objects) [1]. (Belief-domain grouping from author's knowledge of OCCWG.)

### Application in treatment

**A. Exposure and response prevention (ERP) — treatment of choice** [2]
1. Assessment, Y-BOCS, psychoeducation using the model.
2. Build a **fear hierarchy** with SUDS ratings (0–100).
3. **Graded exposure** — in vivo and imaginal — to feared stimuli and obsessions.
4. **Response prevention** — block rituals, avoidance and reassurance seeking.
5. Sessions initially about **90 minutes**; stay until anxiety falls close to baseline (**within-session habituation**); repeated across sessions so peak anxiety and time to recovery fall (**between-session habituation**) [2].
6. Daily homework; family taught not to accommodate rituals.
7. Usually 12–20 sessions.

**B. Cognitive therapy** [1]
- Identify and test appraisals: responsibility pie charts, double-standard technique, probability estimation, surveys of intrusive thoughts in others.
- **Behavioural experiments** — e.g. touching the sole of a shoe and not washing, to learn that the obsession is inaccurate, anxiety is tolerable and does not rise indefinitely [1].
- Thought-suppression experiments (white-bear) to show that suppression increases intrusions.
- Especially useful for **pure obsessions**, poor insight, and patients who refuse ERP.

**C. Additional approaches** — mindfulness and acceptance (ACT) when thinking is ruminative [1]; family involvement; intensive or home-based ERP for severe cases.

**Evidence:** ERP and CBT have large effect sizes and are first-line alone (mild–moderate OCD) or combined with SSRIs (moderate–severe); relapse after ERP is lower than after stopping medication (author's knowledge).

**Conclusion.** Cognitive-behavioural theory explains OCD as a normal intrusion turned into an obsession by catastrophic appraisals of responsibility and threat, and maintained by rituals that bring short-term relief. Treatment follows directly: ERP breaks the reinforcement cycle and cognitive techniques correct the appraisals, making CBT with ERP the first-line psychological treatment for OCD.

## In depth

### Background

- **Victor Meyer (1966)** introduced exposure and response prevention for OCD in hospitalised patients (author's knowledge).
- **Rachman and de Silva (1978)** showed that almost everyone has intrusive thoughts; **Salkovskis (1985)** proposed inflated responsibility as the key appraisal; the **OCCWG (1997, 2005)** defined belief domains and the OBQ (author's knowledge).
- Psychodynamic theory viewed obsessions as defences against unacceptable impulses (isolation, undoing, reaction formation) — explored in K&S 33.2 — but it has little evidence as a treatment for core OCD symptoms [3].

### Mechanism in detail

| Process | Effect |
| --- | --- |
| Negative reinforcement | Rituals reduce anxiety → repeated [1, 2] |
| Prevented disconfirmation | Feared outcome never tested |
| Attentional bias | Monitoring for intrusions increases their frequency [1] |
| Thought suppression | Paradoxical rebound |
| Reassurance seeking | Short-term relief, long-term doubt |
| Family accommodation | Others perform or support rituals, maintaining OCD |

**Inhibitory learning model** (Craske): exposure works by forming new non-threat associations, not just habituation — variability, expectancy violation and removing safety behaviours maximise learning (author's knowledge).

### Assessment in detail

- **Y-BOCS** symptom checklist and severity (0–40); **DOCS** dimensions.
- **Obsessive Beliefs Questionnaire (OBQ-44)**; **Thought–Action Fusion Scale**.
- Insight assessment (ICD-11 qualifier: fair to good, poor to absent) — ICD-11 also broadened obsessions to images and urges and compulsions to covert acts [4].
- Comorbidity: depression, tics, other OCRDs.

### Management details

| Symptom dimension | CBT emphasis |
| --- | --- |
| Contamination/washing | ERP — touching contaminants, delaying and stopping washing |
| Checking | ERP + responsibility work; stop checking and reassurance |
| Symmetry/"just right" | ERP — leaving things "wrong" |
| Taboo thoughts (sexual, religious, harm) | Imaginal exposure, scripts; cognitive work on TAF and meaning of thoughts |
| Hoarding | Separate model (hoarding disorder) — discarding practice, acquisition control |

**Factors predicting poorer response:** poor insight, severe depression, hoarding, comorbid personality disorder, family accommodation, low motivation (author's knowledge).

**Combination with drugs:** SSRIs at higher doses for 8–12 weeks; adding ERP to SSRIs improves response more than adding antipsychotics (Simpson et al., 2013 — author's knowledge).

### Critical appraisal

- ERP is effective but has high refusal and dropout (about 25%) because it is anxiety-provoking (author's knowledge).
- Cognitive therapy alone is roughly as effective as ERP in some trials but less studied.
- Many patients keep residual symptoms; relapse prevention matters.

### Indian context

- Religious obsessions (purity, sin, blasphemy) and contamination themes linked to ritual purity are common in Indian patients; exposure must be culturally sensitive and negotiated.
- Family accommodation is very common in joint families; family-based ERP is valuable.
- Trained ERP therapists are limited; group CBT, guided self-help and telepsychiatry (including Tele-MANAS) extend access.

### Recent advances

- **Internet-delivered CBT (ICBT)** for OCD with therapist support shows good effects (author's knowledge).
- Inhibitory learning-informed exposure; family-based CBT for paediatric OCD.
- Augmentation of exposure with D-cycloserine (mixed results) (author's knowledge).
