## Exam answer

### Introduction
- **Hippocrates:** "melancholics become epileptics and epileptics melancholics" [1].
- **A bidirectional relationship:**
  - people with depression, anxiety, psychosis or suicide attempts are more likely to develop new-onset epilepsy [2];
  - the proposed shared pathology is disturbed neurotransmission, **neuroinflammation** and **HPA-axis** dysfunction [2].
- **Burden:** up to **30% of newly diagnosed** and **50% of pharmacoresistant** patients have psychiatric, behavioural, cognitive or social problems (Tolchin 2020) [1].
- **Definition of epilepsy** [1]: either
  - two unprovoked seizures more than 24 hours apart; or
  - one unprovoked seizure with a recurrence risk of at least 60% over 10 years; or
  - an epilepsy syndrome.

**Prevalence of psychiatric disorders in people with epilepsy (PWE):**

| Disorder | Maudsley 15e [2] | Seminar (Sharma 2020 and others) [1] |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Depression | 13–37% | 30% (lifetime 30–35%); **most frequent comorbidity** |
| Anxiety | 20% | 22% (lifetime 22.8%) |
| Psychosis | 5% | 12%. Meta-analysis 5.6% (about 8× risk); 7% in temporal lobe epilepsy |
| Personality disorder | — | 18–22% |
| Suicide | 3× general population | >5× general population; completed suicide 3–7% |

### Classification by temporal relation to seizures [1, 2]

| Phase | Manifestations |
| --- | --- |
| **Pre-ictal** | **Prodrome**, typically dysphoric mood, 30 minutes to hours (up to 2–3 days) before a seizure [2]. Present in 7–21%; mostly starts about 90 minutes before [1] |
| **Ictal** | **Aura** (psychic: fear, déjà vu, hallucinations); **automatisms**; non-convulsive status epilepticus. Ictal fear or panic is the commonest ictal psychiatric symptom; depressive or psychotic ictal symptoms are rare [2] |
| **Post-ictal** | **Delirium**, **post-ictal psychosis**, depression, anxiety, suicidal ideation. Occurs hours to 7 days after a seizure [2] |
| **Inter-ictal** | Schizophrenia-like psychosis, affective disorders, anxiety, personality change, cognitive deficits, ADHD/ASD, **dissociative seizures** |
| **Para-ictal** | **Forced normalisation / alternative psychosis**: psychiatric symptoms emerging when seizures are controlled [2] |
| **Iatrogenic** | Antiseizure medication (ASM) effects; epilepsy surgery [2] |

### 1. Pre-ictal and ictal phenomena [1]

**Prodrome vs aura:**

| | Prodrome | Aura |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Onset | Insidious, minutes to hours | Sudden, seconds to minutes |
| Relation to seizure | Precedes it | Part of the seizure |
| Progression | Variable | Progresses into the seizure |

**Auras** are brief, paroxysmal, stereotyped and remembered:
- somatosensory: tingling, electric shock, heat;
- simple visual: flashes, colours;
- autonomic: **epigastric sensation, the commonest temporal-lobe aura**.

**Psychic auras by localisation:**
- dysphasic: frontal or temporoparietal;
- dysmnestic (e.g., déjà vu) and cognitive: temporal;
- **affective (fear): mesiobasal temporal**;
- illusions and hallucinations: temporal or parieto-occipital.

**Ictal fear vs panic disorder:**

| Feature | Ictal fear | Panic attack |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Anticipatory anxiety | Absent | Present |
| Duration | Shorter | Longer (often >10 minutes) |
| Phobic avoidance | Absent | Common |
| Situational or cued attacks | No | Yes |
| EEG | Helps diagnosis | May exclude epilepsy |

**Automatisms (ILAE 2017):** more or less coordinated activity during impaired cognition, usually followed by amnesia. 80% last under 5 minutes; 12% over 15 minutes. Types:
- oro-alimentary: chewing, lip smacking;
- mimicry: laughter, fear;
- gestural: fiddling, undressing;
- ambulatory;
- verbal;
- responsive.

**Non-convulsive status epilepticus** (prolonged electrographic seizure; arbitrarily over 30 minutes):
- **simple partial (aura continua):** consciousness preserved, protracted aura;
- **complex partial:** **fluctuating delirium**, automatisms;
- **absence status:** prolonged impaired consciousness, eyelid myoclonus, 3 Hz spike-and-wave.

**Management:** peri-ictal psychiatric symptoms are treated first by **optimising ASMs**. Peri-ictal depressive symptoms do not appear to respond to antidepressants, and **no psychotropic prevents ictal symptoms** [2].

### 2. Post-ictal disorders

**Post-ictal delirium** [1]:
- **Course:** recovery is gradual. It is longer after temporal-lobe seizures, abrupt after frontal seizures, and most protracted after generalised tonic–clonic seizures. Most patients are alert within 15 minutes.
- **Risk factors for prolonged delirium:** prolonged or repeated complex partial seizures; baseline intellectual, neurocognitive or psychiatric disorder; substance use.

**Post-ictal psychosis (PIP)** [1]:
- **Course:** brief, self-limiting, abrupt onset after seizures (often a **cluster**).
- **Prevalence:** about 6%.
- **Duration:** mean **3.5 days** (16 hours to 18 days).
- **Lucid interval:** **2.5–48 hours** between the seizure and the onset of psychosis.
- **Phenomenology:** markedly **abnormal mood** and **hallucinations**; first-rank symptoms rare; negative symptoms not prominent.
- **Risk factors:**
  - age over 30;
  - localisation-related epilepsy;
  - bilateral foci;
  - seizure clustering or secondary generalisation;
  - **family history of mood disorder (not psychosis)**;
  - usually more than **10 years** of epilepsy.

| Post-ictal delirium | Post-ictal psychosis |
| --- | --- |
| More common | Less common |
| Minutes to hours | Days |
| No lucid interval | Lucid interval present |
| No antipsychotic needed | May need antipsychotic |

**Treatment of PIP:**
- **Course:** may remit spontaneously, or respond to **low-dose antipsychotics**.
- **Short-term treatment:** a **benzodiazepine or antipsychotic for up to 3 months**, then taper carefully [1, 2]. The seminar (Kerr/ILAE) suggests antipsychotics for **1–2 months after complete remission** [1].
- **Long-term antipsychotics are not routinely needed** [1].
- **Breakthrough symptoms:** previously remitted interictal disorders can relapse post-ictally [2].

### 3. Interictal psychosis (schizophrenia-like psychosis of epilepsy)

**Concept and course** [1]:
- **Definition:** a psychotic disorder that would meet criteria for schizophrenia were it not for the epilepsy.
- **Onset:** **10–15 years after epilepsy onset**; lasts months to years.
- **Chronic interictal psychosis:** develops in more than 5% with long-standing uncontrolled seizures (Trimble).
- **Classification:** DSM-5 "psychotic disorder due to another medical condition"; ICD-10 F06.

**Phenomenology** [1]:
- closer to schizophrenia, but with a **less intense affective component**;
- **persecutory auditory hallucinations** common;
- first-rank symptoms involving loss of mental boundaries usually absent;

**Pathogenesis** [1]:
- **Kindling (Goddard 1969):** repeated focal stimulation increases excitability and spread. Seizures facilitate psychosis. Amygdala kindling is suppressed by dopamine-receptor supersensitisation (cocaine, apomorphine) and facilitated by haloperidol (Stevens & Livermore 1978).
- **Forced normalisation:**
  - **History:** von Meduna proposed an antagonism between seizures and psychosis. **Landolt (1953)** described EEG normalisation with psychosis, and **Tellenbach (1965)** called the clinical counterpart "**alternative psychosis**".
  - **Mechanism:** the cortex normally inhibits the limbic system, and this balance destabilises when seizures are controlled.
  - **Risk factors** [2]: rapid ASM titration, rapid seizure control, previously drug-resistant epilepsy, temporal lobe epilepsy.

**Treatment:**
- **Ictal psychosis:** treat the seizures [1].
- **Interictal psychosis:** treat **as primary schizophrenia** [1].
- **Forced normalisation:** shared decision-making with carers about ASMs; symptomatic antipsychotics or antidepressants [2].
- **Choosing an antipsychotic:** consider its effect on seizure threshold and ASM interactions:

| Seizure risk (Maudsley) [1, 2] | Antipsychotics |
| --- | --- |
| **Low – good choices** | **Amisulpride/sulpiride** (renally excreted, few interactions), **aripiprazole**, ziprasidone, high-potency FGAs (haloperidol, fluphenazine, flupentixol, trifluoperazine), **risperidone** |
| Probably low (limited data) | Asenapine, brexpiprazole, cariprazine, lurasidone |
| Moderate | Olanzapine, quetiapine (quetiapine has a high interaction risk) |
| Higher – care | **Clozapine**: most proconvulsive, but usable with stable ASMs. Do **not** combine with carbamazepine; **lamotrigine is the ASM of choice** |
| Higher – avoid | Low-potency FGAs (**chlorpromazine** above 1 g/day: 9% seizures), **loxapine**, zotepine. Depots need extreme care |

### 4. Interictal depression, anxiety and suicide

**Depression** [1]:
- **Frequency:** the most frequent comorbidity. 21–33% with persistent seizures vs 4–6% if seizure-free.
- **Atypical presentation in 30–50%:** depression mixed with brief euphoria, irritability, anxiety, paranoid feelings and somatic symptoms, with a chronic course and symptom-free intervals. This is the **interictal dysphoric disorder**.
- **Psychological mechanisms:** chronic illness, coping, **stigma**, low expectations.
- **Biological mechanisms:** hippocampal shrinkage, amygdala hypertrophy, head injury, neurotransmitter changes, ASM effects (GABA augmentation).

**Treatment of depression** [1, 2]:
- **First:** exclude an iatrogenic cause and previous mania or hypomania. Consider seizure risk and ASM interactions.
- **Antidepressants:** **SSRIs first line**. **Citalopram/escitalopram, then sertraline**, are preferred for fewest interactions; escitalopram is safer than citalopram in overdose [2]. Mirtazapine and duloxetine are also recommended.
- **Higher seizure risk:**
  - moderate: venlafaxine (mixed data), trazodone, vilazodone, lithium;
  - **avoid:** amoxapine, maprotiline, **bupropion** (dose-related; immediate-release), **TCAs** (especially clomipramine and amitriptyline).
- **Dose adjustment:** **raise doses with enzyme-inducing ASMs** [1].
- **Psychotherapy** should not be overlooked [1].
- **ECT is not contraindicated.** It has anticonvulsant properties, is worth considering with unstable epilepsy, and does not cause or worsen epilepsy [1, 2].

**Anxiety** [1]:
- **Prevalence:** lifetime 22.8% and 12-month 12.8%. Social phobia 7.2%, specific phobia 6.2%, panic disorder 5.1%, GAD 3.1%, OCD 2.1%, PTSD 1.0%.
- **Seizure phobia:** fear of having a seizure.
- **Treatment:** SSRIs/SNRIs, benzodiazepines, and anxiolytic ASMs (**pregabalin** for social phobia and GAD, gabapentin for social anxiety, lamotrigine for PTSD), plus psychotherapy.

**Suicide** [1]:
- **Burden:** more than 10% of deaths in PWE.
- **Associations:**
  - interictal dysphoric episodes;
  - post-ictal depression;
  - early-onset, severe or temporal-lobe epilepsy;
  - psychiatric comorbidity.
- **Follow-up closely:** patients on ASMs with negative psychotropic effects, or with a psychiatric history, episodic behavioural change or suicidal ideation.
- **SUDEP:** 5.2% of deaths; 36% of deaths at ages 0–15.

### 5. Personality, cognition and neurodevelopment [1]
- **Personality disorder** (18–22%): dependent and avoidant traits, from stigma, loss of control and unpredictable seizures. The Big Five profile shows **high neuroticism** and low openness.
- **Gastaut–Geschwind syndrome (TLE):**
  - **hypergraphia**;
  - atypical sexuality;
  - **hyper-religiosity**;
  - **viscosity**;
  - intensified mental life;
  - humourlessness and irritability.
- **Syndrome-specific traits:**
  - **juvenile myoclonic epilepsy:** irresponsibility, impulsivity, self-centredness, emotional instability;
  - juvenile absence epilepsy: academic and behavioural difficulty.
- **Cognition:**
  - **children:** 26% meet criteria for intellectual disability;
  - **adults:** 54–72% of new, untreated cases have mild attention, executive or memory deficits;
  - **left TLE** → verbal memory; **right TLE** → non-verbal memory; **frontal lobe epilepsy** → speed, attention, working memory, inhibition, planning;
  - **worse with:** early onset, long duration, polypharmacy, depression and anxiety.
- **ADHD:**
  - 2–3× more common in PWE, mostly inattentive;
  - **methylphenidate 0.3–1 mg/kg/day** is safe (60–70% response), with low starting doses [1, 2].
- **ASD:** 21% of children and 8% of adults with epilepsy; associated with ID, West syndrome and Lennox–Gastaut.

### 6. Dissociative (psychogenic non-epileptic) seizures [1]
- **Epidemiology:**
  - India 2.9 per 1,000;
  - **comorbid with epilepsy in 7–32%**; up to 50% of "refractory epilepsy" may involve PNES;
  - peak at 20–30 years; 90% female.
- **Pointers favouring PNES over epileptic seizures:**
  - duration over 2 minutes;
  - gradual onset and fluctuating course;
  - **side-to-side head movement**, pelvic thrusting, thrashing;
  - **weeping**, and recall of the event;
  - emotional vocalisation.
- **Pointers favouring epileptic seizures:** synchronous clonic movements, automatisms.

### 7. Iatrogenic: psychiatric effects of antiseizure medications [1, 2]
- **Frequency:** psychiatric effects occur in **15–20%** of PWE, more with a psychiatric history and refractory epilepsy [1].
- **Mood and folate:** stopping a mood-stabilising ASM can unmask mood disorder. ASMs can lower folate and affect mood, so check folate and replace it [2].
- **Surgery:** epilepsy surgery can cause de novo depression, anxiety or, rarely, psychosis [2].

| Adverse psychiatric effects | Beneficial effects |
| --- | --- |
| **Levetiracetam** (anxiety, depression, aggression, suicidality), brivaracetam, **topiramate** (depression, psychosis), **zonisamide**, **perampanel**, **vigabatrin** (psychosis), tiagabine, ethosuximide, **phenobarbital/primidone** (depression, ADHD-like symptoms) | **Lamotrigine** (antidepressant, mood stabilising), **valproate** and **carbamazepine/oxcarbazepine** (mood stabilising, antimanic), **gabapentin/pregabalin** (anxiolytic; withdrawal causes depression and anxiety) |

### 8. Principles of prescribing psychotropics in epilepsy [2]
1. Exclude peri-ictal and iatrogenic causes.
2. **Optimise epilepsy treatment first.**
3. Prefer psychotropics with antiseizure properties (e.g., anticonvulsant mood stabilisers in bipolar disorder).
4. **Check interactions:**
   - **enzyme-inducing ASMs** (carbamazepine, phenytoin, phenobarbital, primidone; weakly oxcarbazepine at 900 mg or more and topiramate at 400 mg or more) lower psychotropic levels;
   - fluoxetine, fluvoxamine and paroxetine raise carbamazepine and phenytoin levels.
5. **Start low and go slow**, because proconvulsant effects are dose-related.
6. If seizures occur, change the psychotropic or optimise the ASM.
7. **Watch for overlapping adverse effects:**
   - weight gain;
   - sexual dysfunction;
   - **hyponatraemia** (carbamazepine, oxcarbazepine, and most antidepressants and antipsychotics), which can itself provoke seizures;
   - osteoporosis;
   - blood dyscrasias.
8. **Reframe the seizure risk:** depression and psychosis themselves raise seizure risk. Placebo arms of RCTs show about 15× the population rate of unprovoked seizures, and SSRIs had *fewer* seizures than placebo.

### 9. Substance use and legal aspects [1, 2]
- **Alcohol:**
  - **a single withdrawal seizure:** benzodiazepines for 7 days plus vitamins;
  - **independent epilepsy in heavy drinkers:** prefer **levetiracetam, lamotrigine or topiramate**. Avoid phenytoin, carbamazepine and phenobarbital (hepatic induction) and valproate (hepatotoxicity) [1].
- **Sexual dysfunction:** erectile dysfunction in men (1.5–2×); reduced desire and arousal in 10–75% of women. Risk is higher with uncontrolled, focal or long-standing epilepsy and polytherapy [1].
- **Legal:**
  - epilepsy is not explicitly named in the RPWD Act [1];
  - **driving:** most countries require a **6–36-month** seizure-free period [2];
  - other issues are employment and marriage [1].

**Conclusion.** Psychiatric disorders are common in epilepsy and bidirectionally linked to it. They are best understood by their relation to seizures:
- **pre-ictal:** dysphoria;
- **ictal:** fear, automatisms, NCSE;
- **post-ictal:** delirium and brief psychosis after a lucid interval;
- **interictal:** schizophrenia-like psychosis, interictal dysphoric disorder, anxiety, suicide, cognitive and personality change;
- **para-ictal:** forced normalisation;
- **iatrogenic:** ASM effects.

Management principles:
- optimise seizure control first;
- treat peri-ictal symptoms briefly;
- treat interictal disorders as their primary counterparts, choosing low-risk psychotropics (SSRIs, mirtazapine, amisulpride, aripiprazole, risperidone) with attention to ASM interactions;
- remember that ECT is safe.

## In depth

### Ictal phenomena relevant to psychiatric differential diagnosis [1]
- **Temporal-lobe auras:**
  - mimic **panic** (ictal fear);
  - mimic **depersonalisation and derealisation** (dysmnestic and cognitive auras);
  - mimic **psychosis** (illusions and hallucinations).
- **NCSE** can present as fluctuating delirium; **EEG** is essential.

### Critical appraisal (from the sources)
- **Prevalence figures:**
  - psychosis is 5% [2] vs 12% [1];
  - suicide risk is 3× [2] vs more than 5× [1];
  - the differences likely reflect differences in samples and definitions.
- **PIP treatment duration:** "up to 3 months" (Maudsley) vs "1–2 months after remission" (ILAE/Kerr) [1, 2].
- **Antidepressants and seizures:** observational associations are confounded by depression itself, which raises seizure risk [2].
