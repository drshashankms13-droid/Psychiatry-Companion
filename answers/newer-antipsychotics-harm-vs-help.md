## Exam answer

**Definition.** "Newer antipsychotics" here means:
1. **Second-generation (atypical) antipsychotics (SGAs)** introduced from the 1990s: clozapine (re-introduced 1990), risperidone, olanzapine, quetiapine, ziprasidone, amisulpride, aripiprazole, paliperidone, asenapine, lurasidone, iloperidone.
2. **Third-generation/novel agents:** D2/D3 partial agonists (brexpiprazole, cariprazine), lumateperone, and non-D2 drugs such as the **M1/M4 muscarinic agonist xanomeline–trospium** (FDA approval 26 September 2024) [1].

The question asks whether their gains (fewer extrapyramidal side effects, broader efficacy) are outweighed by harms (metabolic syndrome, cost, overuse).

### Mechanism
- **FGAs:** potent D2 blockade (>80% occupancy) → antipsychotic effect, plus EPS, TD and hyperprolactinaemia.
- **SGAs:** D2 blockade with 5-HT2A antagonism, fast D2 dissociation (Kapur & Seeman), or partial agonism → fewer EPS. H1 and 5-HT2C blockade and muscarinic M3 effects → weight gain and insulin resistance (olanzapine, clozapine).
- **Muscarinic agonists:** M4 agonism reduces presynaptic dopamine release in the associative striatum, sparing the sensorimotor striatum (so no EPS); M1 agonism improves cortical E/I balance and may help cognition. Trospium blocks peripheral cholinergic effects [1].

### Benefits vs harms

| Domain | Help | Harm |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Efficacy | Clozapine is uniquely effective in treatment-resistant schizophrenia (~40% respond) and reduces suicide (InterSePT); amisulpride, olanzapine and risperidone slightly more effective than many FGAs (Leucht 2009, Huhn 2019) | Most SGAs no better than FGAs (CATIE, CUtLASS) |
| Neurological | Fewer EPS, akathisia and TD (TD ~1%/year vs ~5%/year with FGAs) | Not zero: risperidone EPS at higher doses; aripiprazole akathisia |
| Metabolic | Aripiprazole, lurasidone, ziprasidone, cariprazine and xanomeline–trospium are weight-neutral | Olanzapine and clozapine: weight gain, type 2 diabetes, dyslipidaemia, metabolic syndrome in ~30–40%; higher cardiovascular mortality |
| Endocrine | Aripiprazole and quetiapine are prolactin-sparing | Risperidone, paliperidone and amisulpride cause marked hyperprolactinaemia (galactorrhoea, amenorrhoea, osteoporosis) [2] |
| Cognition and negative symptoms | Xanomeline–trospium improved negative symptoms (SMD −0.33) and cognition post hoc in impaired patients [1]; cariprazine for predominant negative symptoms | Claimed cognitive advantage of SGAs was largely a trial artefact |
| Other | LAIs (paliperidone 6-monthly); mood stabilisation; bipolar depression (quetiapine, lurasidone) | Off-label overuse (insomnia, dementia, personality disorder); stroke and death in dementia (black-box warning); QTc (ziprasidone); cost |

### Assessment (before and during treatment)
Baseline weight, BMI, waist, BP, fasting glucose/HbA1c, lipids, prolactin, ECG; repeat at 12 weeks, then yearly. Use AIMS and BARS for movement disorders. Ask about sexual function and menstrual history.

### Management principles
- Choose the drug by **side-effect profile**, matched to the patient (shared decision-making).
- Prefer metabolically benign drugs first-line in young and first-episode patients.
- Use clozapine early for treatment-resistant schizophrenia.
- Use the lowest effective dose and avoid polypharmacy.
- Co-prescribe metformin for antipsychotic-induced weight gain; add lifestyle interventions.
- Avoid off-label use without clear indication; deprescribe in dementia.

### Key evidence
- **Leucht 2009 (Lancet):** only four SGAs (clozapine, amisulpride, olanzapine, risperidone) were more efficacious than FGAs; others were not.
- **CATIE 2005 (n=1,493)** and **CUtLASS 2006:** no overall effectiveness advantage over perphenazine or FGAs; olanzapine had the longest time to discontinuation but the most weight gain.
- **Huhn 2019** network meta-analysis of 32 antipsychotics: small efficacy differences, large side-effect differences.
- **Fabiano 2025** meta-analysis of xanomeline–trospium (3 RCTs, n=690, 5 weeks): PANSS total SMD −0.56; response RR 2.13 (NNT 4); no EPS or akathisia; less weight gain (≥7% gain RR 0.46); more nausea (19% vs 4%), vomiting (14% vs 2%), constipation (17% vs 6%), dyspepsia, dry mouth, hypertension and tachycardia [1].

**Conclusion.** "More harm than help" is **too sweeping**. The "atypical" class was oversold: most newer drugs are not more effective than older ones, and several cause serious metabolic harm. But clozapine's unique efficacy, the lower burden of EPS and TD, prolactin-sparing and weight-neutral options, and the first non-D2 mechanism (muscarinic agonism) are real gains. Harm comes mainly from **indiscriminate choice and overuse**, not from the drugs as a class. The answer is individualised prescribing with metabolic vigilance.

## In depth

### Background
- **1952:** chlorpromazine (Delay and Deniker). **1958:** haloperidol (Janssen).
- **1971:** clozapine introduced; withdrawn in 1975 after agranulocytosis deaths in Finland; reintroduced in 1989–90 after Kane's 1988 trial in treatment-resistant schizophrenia.
- **1990s:** risperidone (1993), olanzapine (1996), quetiapine (1997) were marketed as "atypicals" with claims of better efficacy, especially for negative and cognitive symptoms.
- **2000s:** aripiprazole (2002), the first D2 partial agonist. Pragmatic trials (CATIE, CUtLASS, EUFEST) tempered enthusiasm. Tyrer and Kendall (2009) called the atypicals "the spurious advance".
- **2010s–2020s:** cariprazine, brexpiprazole, lumateperone; TAAR1 agonists failed in phase 3; xanomeline–trospium (KarXT) became the first non-D2 antipsychotic (2024) [1].

### Epidemiology of use and harm
- SGAs account for over 80% of antipsychotic prescriptions in most countries, India included. More than half of UK antipsychotic prescriptions are for non-psychotic indications (insomnia, anxiety, personality disorder, dementia) [2].
- Metabolic syndrome prevalence is about 30–35% in people with schizophrenia; antipsychotic-naïve first-episode patients already show some glucose dysregulation.
- The mortality gap is 15–20 years, mostly from cardiovascular disease. However, long-term antipsychotic use is associated with **lower** all-cause mortality than non-use in Finnish and Swedish cohorts (Tiihonen; clozapine lowest).
- In discontinuation trials, rapid stopping may exaggerate apparent benefit [2].

### Classification

| Class | Examples | Distinctive features |
| --- | --- | --- |
| FGA high potency | Haloperidol, fluphenazine | EPS, prolactin |
| FGA low potency | Chlorpromazine | Sedation, anticholinergic, hypotension |
| SGA — SDA (serotonin–dopamine antagonist) | Risperidone, paliperidone, ziprasidone, lurasidone, iloperidone | Prolactin (risperidone/paliperidone) |
| SGA — MARTA | Clozapine, olanzapine, quetiapine, asenapine | Metabolic, sedation |
| Substituted benzamides | Amisulpride, sulpiride | Prolactin; renally excreted |
| D2/D3 partial agonists | Aripiprazole, brexpiprazole, cariprazine | Akathisia; low metabolic risk |
| Multimodal | Lumateperone | Low EPS and metabolic risk |
| Muscarinic agonist | Xanomeline–trospium | No D2 blockade; cholinergic GI effects [1] |
| Neuroscience-based Nomenclature (NbN) | Names by mechanism, not "generation" | Preferred by ECNP and other bodies |

### Mechanism in detail — why harms differ

| Receptor | Effect of blockade |
| --- | --- |
| D2 (striatal) | EPS, TD (supersensitivity) |
| D2 (tuberoinfundibular) | Hyperprolactinaemia |
| H1 | Sedation, appetite, weight gain |
| 5-HT2C | Appetite, weight gain |
| M3 (pancreatic β-cells) | Impaired insulin secretion (olanzapine, clozapine) |
| α1 | Postural hypotension |
| M1 (central) | Cognitive impairment, constipation |
| hERG channel | QTc prolongation |

Xanomeline acts as an **agonist** at M1/M4; trospium (a quaternary amine that does not cross the BBB) blocks peripheral M2/M3, but nausea, vomiting, constipation, dyspepsia, raised heart rate and BP still occur [1].

### Clinical features in detail — the harms
- **Metabolic:** weight gain (olanzapine and clozapine about 4–10 kg in the first year), insulin resistance, new-onset diabetes (including DKA early in treatment), dyslipidaemia, hypertension.
- **Cardiac:** QTc prolongation; myocarditis and cardiomyopathy with clozapine.
- **Neurological:** EPS, akathisia (aripiprazole, cariprazine), TD, NMS (rare), seizures (clozapine dose-related).
- **Endocrine and sexual:** hyperprolactinaemia leads to galactorrhoea, amenorrhoea, infertility, low oestrogen and bone loss, especially in premenopausal women [2].
- **Haematological:** clozapine neutropenia and agranulocytosis (~0.8%).
- **GI:** clozapine hypomotility (potentially fatal ileus).
- **Dementia:** about 1.6–1.7 times higher mortality and higher stroke risk.

### Assessment in detail
- **Metabolic monitoring:** weight weekly for 6 weeks, then at 12 weeks and yearly; BP; HbA1c or fasting glucose and lipids at baseline, 12 weeks and yearly. Prioritise lifestyle and metformin when weight gain exceeds 5–7%.
- **Prolactin:** at baseline and if symptomatic; DEXA in long-term hyperprolactinaemia [2].
- **ECG:** at baseline when cardiac risk or a QT-prolonging drug is involved.
- **Rating scales:** GASS, LUNSERS, AIMS, BARS, SAS.

### Differential — choosing between agents (examples)

| Patient priority | Reasonable choice | Avoid |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Young, first episode, weight concern | Aripiprazole, lurasidone, cariprazine | Olanzapine, clozapine early |
| Woman planning pregnancy, menstrual problems | Aripiprazole, quetiapine | Risperidone, amisulpride |
| Prominent EPS history | Quetiapine, aripiprazole, clozapine | Haloperidol, high-dose risperidone |
| Treatment resistance | **Clozapine** | Polypharmacy without clozapine trial |
| Predominant negative symptoms | Cariprazine; consider xanomeline–trospium where available | High-dose D2 blockers |
| Elderly with dementia | Non-drug first; lowest dose, short duration | Long-term antipsychotics |
| Poor adherence | LAI (aripiprazole, paliperidone) | — |

### Management details
- **Weight gain:** metformin 500 mg twice daily titrated to 1 g twice daily (prevents and reverses weight gain; recommended at initiation with high-risk drugs such as olanzapine or clozapine). Consider switching to aripiprazole, or adjunctive aripiprazole. GLP-1 agonists (semaglutide, liraglutide) for BMI ≥30 or ≥27 with risk factors.
- **Hyperprolactinaemia:** reduce dose; switch to aripiprazole; or add aripiprazole 5 mg [2].
- **Akathisia:** reduce dose; propranolol; mirtazapine 15 mg.
- **TD:** VMAT2 inhibitors (valbenazine, deutetrabenazine); switch to clozapine.
- **Xanomeline–trospium practicalities:** 50/20 mg twice daily → 100/20 mg → 125/30 mg twice daily, taken 1 hour before or 2 hours after food. Avoid in urinary retention, moderate–severe hepatic impairment, gastric retention, narrow-angle glaucoma. Monitor heart rate and BP [1].

### Critical appraisal
- **Harm side:**
  - Marketing exaggerated benefits; short trials with high-dose haloperidol comparators created a "straw man".
  - The metabolic epidemic.
  - Expanding off-label use.
  - High costs before generics.
  - Industry funding of trials.
- **Help side:**
  - Clozapine is lifesaving.
  - Less EPS and TD improves adherence and quality of life.
  - Real-world cohorts show lower mortality on treatment than off it.
  - Wider choice allows personalisation.
- **Xanomeline–trospium:**
  - **Promising but early**: only 5-week trials, three studies of mostly Black and male US participants, all industry-funded.
  - No head-to-head comparisons with standard antipsychotics.
  - All-cause discontinuation was not better than placebo.
  - An earlier network meta-analysis found the highest discontinuation risk with xanomeline–trospium [1].
  - Long-term, adjunctive (ARISE trial negative) and real-world data are still limited.
- **The "generation" label is unhelpful**: drugs differ more within classes than between them. Prescribe by receptor profile, not by generation.

### Indian context
- Generic SGAs are cheap in India (olanzapine and risperidone cost a few rupees per day), so cost no longer argues against them, but metabolic monitoring is often neglected.
- South Asians have higher cardiometabolic risk at lower BMI. Use lower BMI thresholds (overweight ≥23, obese ≥25) and a waist threshold of ≥90 cm in men and ≥80 cm in women.
- Olanzapine is widely prescribed first-line in India; this needs reconsideration in young and first-episode patients.
- Clozapine is under-used despite low cost; absolute neutrophil count (ANC) monitoring logistics are a barrier.
- Xanomeline–trospium is **not approved in India** at the time of writing (check current status).
- National List of Essential Medicines (2022): includes haloperidol, chlorpromazine, fluphenazine decanoate, risperidone, olanzapine and clozapine.

### Recent advances
- **Established:** paliperidone 6-monthly LAI; aripiprazole 2-monthly; metformin co-prescription guidelines (2025 Schizophrenia Bulletin guideline: start metformin at initiation for high-risk antipsychotics such as olanzapine and clozapine, or with medium-risk drugs plus a risk factor).
- **Emerging:** xanomeline–trospium (US 2024) [1]; emraclidine (M4 positive allosteric modulator) failed phase 2 in 2024.
- **Investigational:** TAAR1 agonists (ulotaront failed phase 3); GlyT1 inhibitors; cannabidiol; personalised prescribing using pharmacogenomics (CYP2D6, CYP1A2).
