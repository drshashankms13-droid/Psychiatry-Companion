## Exam answer

**Introduction.** **Parkinsonism** is a syndrome of four cardinal deficits [1]:
- **tremor**;
- **rigidity**;
- **bradykinesia**;
- **postural abnormality**.

Idiopathic PD is by far the commonest cause. Many other conditions affecting the basal ganglia cause **akinetic–rigid syndromes**. Misdiagnosis is common: at autopsy, **24% of clinically diagnosed PD** was another condition (PSP, MSA, Alzheimer's, vascular) [1].

### Classification of causes [1, 2]

| Group | Conditions |
| --- | --- |
| **Idiopathic (Lewy body) PD** | Sporadic; rare genetic forms (α-synuclein, parkin, LRRK2) |
| **Drug-induced** | **Neuroleptics** (phenothiazines, butyrophenones), reserpine, methyldopa |
| **Atypical (Parkinson-plus) degenerative** | **Progressive supranuclear palsy**, **multiple system atrophy**, **corticobasal degeneration**, **dementia with Lewy bodies**; also Alzheimer's with parkinsonism |
| **Heredodegenerative** | **Wilson's disease**; **rigid/akinetic forms of Huntington's disease**; **dopa-responsive dystonia** (parkinsonian features) |
| **Vascular** | "Arteriosclerotic"/vascular parkinsonism: about 3% of parkinsonism in elderly Europeans |
| **Post-infectious** | **Postencephalitic** (encephalitis lethargica): young onset, **oculogyric crises**, pupil abnormalities, sleep disturbance; cerebral syphilis |
| **Toxic** | **MPTP**, **carbon monoxide**, **manganese** |
| **Structural or diffuse brain damage** | **Repeated head injury (boxers)**, **anoxia** after cardiac arrest; tumour (strictly unilateral rigidity without tremor) |
| **Mimics (pseudo-parkinsonism)** | **Essential tremor**, **depression** (psychomotor retardation), **myxoedema**, arthritis or osteoporosis in the elderly, **"hysteria"/malingering** (early fluctuations), catatonia or stereotypies in schizophrenia |

### Approach: clinical clues [1, 2]
1. **Drug history first.**
   - Neuroleptic parkinsonism appears **within days to the first month** and is dose-dependent (20–40% of patients).
   - It is **bilateral and symmetrical**, bradykinesia-predominant, with **less tremor**.
   - Late perioral **"rabbit" tremor** can occur.
   - It resolves over weeks (occasionally **up to 18 months**) after stopping.
   - Persistence beyond that suggests underlying idiopathic PD.
   - Severe cases can resemble **catatonia** or akinetic mutism. Differentiate from **NMS**.
2. **Age at onset:**
   - **young onset** (adolescence or early adulthood): exclude **Wilson's disease**, juvenile HD, dopa-responsive dystonia, postencephalitic or genetic PD.
3. **Tempo:**
   - **acute or stepwise**, with pseudobulbar or pyramidal signs and *marche à petit pas*: vascular;
   - **rapid progression**: atypical parkinsonism.
4. **Symmetry and tremor:**
   - PD: asymmetric, rest tremor (presenting feature in 75%);
   - **CBD: marked asymmetry**;
   - drug-induced: symmetrical, little tremor;
   - **essential tremor**: postural or action tremor, head titubation, **improves with alcohol**, family history, no bradykinesia or rigidity, static for decades.
5. **Red-flag features of atypical parkinsonism:**
   - **early falls**;
   - **vertical gaze palsy**;
   - **early autonomic failure**;
   - **cerebellar signs**;
   - **pyramidal signs**;
   - **apraxia, alien limb, cortical sensory loss**;
   - **early dementia, fluctuations, hallucinations**;
   - **poor levodopa response**.
6. **Levodopa response:**
   - about 15% of "PD" patients do not respond: consider PSP or MSA;
   - DLB, CBD and PSP respond poorly or transiently;
   - **dopa-responsive dystonia** responds dramatically.
7. **Investigations:**
   - **caeruloplasmin, 24-hour urinary copper, slit lamp** (Kayser–Fleischer ring) in the young;
   - **thyroid function**;
   - **CT/MRI**: vascular lesions, tumour; **midbrain diameter <17 mm** in PSP; Wilson's basal ganglia signal ("face of the giant panda");
   - **DAT-SPECT/PET**: about 90% sensitivity for PD vs normal or essential tremor;
   - **¹⁸F-dopa PET**: PD has posterior-putamen predominance; PSP has uniform caudate and putamen loss; CBD is asymmetric;
   - **sphincter EMG**: separates PSP or MSA from PD, but not from each other.

### Atypical parkinsonian disorders (p3-eb8f3792) [1, 2]

| Feature | **PSP** (Steele–Richardson–Olszewski) | **MSA** (striatonigral degeneration / OPCA / Shy–Drager) | **CBD** | **DLB** |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Prevalence | 5–6 per 100,000 | 4.4 per 100,000 (up to 10% of parkinsonism?) | Rare | 10–20% of dementias (second or third commonest) |
| Onset and survival | 6th decade; death in **5–10 years** | 6th decade; median survival **9 years** | 7th decade; death in 4–10 years | 60s–70s; about 10 years (variable; shorter than AD) |
| Motor | Axial (trunk/neck) dystonic rigidity; **falls backwards**; pseudobulbar palsy; "growling" dysarthria | Parkinsonian (**MSA-P, 80%**) or cerebellar (**MSA-C, 20%**); pyramidal signs; myoclonus; dyskinesias | **Marked asymmetry**; limb rigidity, **limb apraxia**, **alien limb**, stimulus-sensitive action myoclonus, dystonia, **cortical sensory loss** | Rigidity and gait problems more than tremor; **falls** |
| Eyes | **Supranuclear vertical gaze palsy, especially downgaze**; "Mona Lisa stare"; eventually fixed gaze | Upgaze palsy sometimes | Supranuclear gaze palsy possible | — |
| Autonomic | — | **Central**: orthostatic hypotension (about half faint), **early bladder dysfunction** (detrusor hyperreflexia), impotence, constipation, respiratory dysfunction | — | Orthostatic hypotension, syncope, unexplained loss of consciousness |
| Cognition | **Subcortical dementia** (>80%): bradyphrenia, executive deficits, impaired retrieval; **no aphasia, apraxia or agnosia** | **Dementia not a feature**; mild frontal impairment | Often mild early despite apraxia; parietal deficits; dementia in about one-third | **Fluctuating cognition** (attention, visuoperception, executive); dementia **precedes or comes within 1 year of** parkinsonism |
| Psychiatric | **Apathy and disinhibition** (more than PD); depression about 54%; anxiety; emotional lability; forced laughing or crying; psychosis 18% | **Depression** (39% moderate–severe); emotional lability; sleep disorders and **RBD** | **Depression high**; less apathy; psychosis infrequent; rarely Klüver–Bucy or frontal aggression | **Complex visual hallucinations** (people or animals, small, silent); delusions; depression; **RBD**; **severe neuroleptic sensitivity** |
| Pathology | **4-repeat tauopathy**: straight-filament tangles, glial tau; basal ganglia, brainstem, cerebellar nuclei; cortex spared; H1/H1 haplotype | **α-synucleinopathy**: glial cytoplasmic inclusions (α-synuclein and tau positive); nigra without Lewy bodies | **4-repeat tauopathy**: perirolandic frontoparietal atrophy; achromatic **ballooned neurones**; H1 | **α-synucleinopathy**: cortical Lewy bodies; severe cholinergic deficit |
| Levodopa | May help briefly; usually disappointing | Variable (up to 68%) but wanes; dyskinesias | Usually ineffective; baclofen for rigidity, clonazepam for myoclonus | Not stated in the source |
| Imaging | Midbrain diameter <17 mm; midbrain and red nucleus signal change; uniform striatal dopa loss | — | **Asymmetric** cortical atrophy; asymmetric PET hypometabolism and striatal dopa loss | — |

### Other key differentials [1]
- **Vascular parkinsonism:**
  - historically suspected with acute or stepwise onset and *marche à petit pas*;
  - dementia, pseudobulbar or pyramidal signs;
  - only 1% reclassified in the UK Brain Bank;
  - worse prognosis.
- **Wilson's disease:**
  - more varied movements (chorea, athetosis, dystonia, **wing-beating/flapping tremor**);
  - liver disease, K–F rings, psychiatric change;
  - **must be excluded in anyone under 50 with dystonia or young-onset parkinsonism**.
- **Akinetic-rigid Huntington's disease** can mimic PD.
- **Depression:**
  - psychomotor retardation vs bradykinesia, which **dual-task tests** can separate;
  - depression may also be a PD prodrome.
- **Psychogenic:**
  - early fluctuating PD can be mistaken for "neurosis, hysteria or malingering", especially when function is normal under stress.

**Conclusion.** Approach parkinsonism by:
1. excluding **drugs** first;
2. using **age, tempo, symmetry and red flags** (early falls, gaze palsy, autonomic failure, cerebellar or cortical signs, early dementia or hallucinations, poor levodopa response) to separate **idiopathic PD** from **PSP, MSA, CBD and DLB**;
3. never missing **Wilson's disease** in the young;
4. supporting the diagnosis with DAT/PET imaging, MRI and copper studies.

The distinction matters: prognosis, levodopa response and **neuroleptic sensitivity** (DLB) differ sharply.

## In depth

### Background
- **James Parkinson (1817)** described "paralysis agitans". The **parkinsonian syndrome** is the four-feature clinical picture regardless of cause [1].
- **Steele, Richardson and Olszewski (1964)** described PSP. **Adams et al. (1964)** described striatonigral degeneration. **Rebeiz et al. (1968)** described CBD. **Shy and Drager (1960)** described autonomic failure with parkinsonism, later grouped with olivopontocerebellar atrophy as **MSA** [1, 3].
- **"Arteriosclerotic parkinsonism"** fell into disrepute. Only 1% of the UK Brain Bank series needed reclassifying as vascular parkinsonism [1].
- **Encephalitis lethargica** (1916–1927) produced postencephalitic parkinsonism up to 20 years later, with oculogyric crises and sleep disturbance. Neurofibrillary tangles, not Lewy bodies, were found [1].

### Epidemiology [1, 3]
- **PD** is by far the commonest cause (lifetime risk about 2%).
- **PSP:** 5–6 per 100,000; onset in the 6th decade.
- **MSA:** 4.4 per 100,000; **up to 10% of parkinsonism** (Quinn); men more than women [1, 3].
  - **MSA-P outnumbers MSA-C**, except in **Japan**, where MSA-C predominates [3].
- **CBD:** rarer; onset in the 7th decade.
- **DLB:** 10–20% of dementias at autopsy [2].
- **Vascular parkinsonism:** about 3% of parkinsonism in elderly Europeans [1].
- **Drug-induced parkinsonism** is the commonest cause of secondary parkinsonism and is frequently missed in the elderly [general knowledge].
- **Diagnostic error:**
  - 24 of 100 clinically diagnosed PD cases were misdiagnosed at autopsy (Hughes 1992);
  - in community practice, the diagnosis was correct in 83%, and **19% of those given another diagnosis actually had PD** (Schrag 2002) [1].

### Classification
**By molecular pathology (proteinopathy)** [1; general knowledge for the framework]:
| Class | Disorders | Hallmark |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **α-Synucleinopathies** | PD, DLB, **MSA** | Lewy bodies (neuronal) in PD and DLB; **glial cytoplasmic inclusions** in MSA [1, 3] |
| **Tauopathies** | **PSP, CBD** (both **4-repeat tau**, **H1 haplotype**), FTDP-17, postencephalitic parkinsonism, Guam complex | PSP: straight-filament tangles, glial tau, 4R:3R ratio 3–4:1. CBD: **achromatic ballooned neurones**, astrocytic plaques [1] |
| **Others** | Wilson's (copper), Huntington's (polyglutamine), NBIA/PKAN (iron), neuroacanthocytosis, prion disease | |

**Current clinical criteria** [general knowledge]:
- **PD:** MDS 2015 (supportive criteria, absolute exclusions, **red flags**).
- **PSP:** **MDS-PSP 2017**:
  - four core domains: **ocular motor (O), postural instability (P), akinesia (A), cognitive dysfunction (C)**;
  - phenotypes include **PSP-RS** (Richardson syndrome), **PSP-P** (parkinsonism; levodopa-responsive early, often mistaken for PD), **PSP-PGF** (progressive gait freezing), **PSP-F** (frontal), **PSP-CBS**, **PSP-SL** (speech/language).
- **CBD:** **Armstrong 2013**. The clinical **corticobasal syndrome (CBS)** is distinguished from pathological CBD. CBS can be caused by CBD, **PSP, Alzheimer's disease or FTLD-TDP**.
- **MSA:** **Gilman 2008**, revised as **MDS-MSA 2022** (clinically established vs clinically probable; supportive MRI markers).
- **DLB:** **McKeith 2017**:
  - core features: fluctuating cognition, recurrent visual hallucinations, **RBD**, parkinsonism;
  - indicative biomarkers: reduced DAT uptake, **low cardiac MIBG uptake**, **PSG-confirmed REM sleep without atonia**.

### Mechanism in detail
**Basal ganglia circuitry [1]:**
- In PD, **striatal dopamine loss** increases the "indirect pathway" output. The result is **excess inhibition of the thalamus by GPi/SNr**, giving bradykinesia.
- PSP, MSA and CBD damage the **striatum and its outputs themselves** (postsynaptic). Dopamine replacement therefore has **little to act on**, which explains the **poor levodopa response**.
- **Imaging correlate:** in PD, F-dopa loss is **posterior putamen-predominant**. In PSP the loss is **uniform** across caudate and putamen [1].

**Why the psychiatric profiles differ [1]:**
- **PSP:** frontal–subcortical deafferentation (prefrontal hypometabolism; cortex largely spared). This produces **apathy, disinhibition, bradyphrenia** and **subcortical dementia**.
- **CBD:** asymmetric **perirolandic frontoparietal** cortical disease. This produces apraxia, alien limb, parietal deficits, **depression**, and rarely Klüver–Bucy syndrome.
- **MSA:** brainstem, cerebellar and autonomic disease with relative **cortical sparing**. Hence **no dementia**, but depression, emotional lability and **RBD**.
- **DLB:** **cortical Lewy bodies** plus a **severe cholinergic deficit**. This produces fluctuations, visual hallucinations and extreme neuroleptic sensitivity [2].

**Genetics:**
- **MAPT H1/H1** homozygosity: PSP 89% vs controls 65%; CBD 93% [1].
- **COQ2** mutations have been reported in familial (and some sporadic) MSA, particularly in Japanese cohorts [3; general knowledge for the population].

### Clinical features in detail
**Red flags against idiopathic PD** [1, 3; general knowledge for the list]:
- **early falls** (within 3 years), especially **backwards** → PSP;
- **vertical supranuclear gaze palsy** or slowed vertical saccades → PSP;
- **early, severe autonomic failure** (orthostatic hypotension, early urinary incontinence or retention, erectile dysfunction) → MSA;
- **cerebellar signs** → MSA-C;
- **stridor**, inspiratory sighs, severe **dysphonia**, **disproportionate antecollis** → MSA;
- **pyramidal signs** → MSA, PSP;
- **marked asymmetry with apraxia, alien limb, cortical sensory loss, myoclonus** → CBD/CBS;
- **early dementia or hallucinations** (before or within 1 year of parkinsonism) → DLB;
- **symmetric onset, absent rest tremor, poor or waning levodopa response** → any atypical parkinsonism;
- **rapid progression** (wheelchair within 5 years);
- **pseudobulbar palsy**, emotional incontinence → PSP;
- **young onset (<40)** → exclude Wilson's, juvenile HD, parkin mutations, dopa-responsive dystonia.

**PSP bedside signs [1, 3]:**
- "**Mona Lisa**" / astonished stare;
- downgaze worse than upgaze;
- hypometric vertical saccades; square-wave jerks;
- **preserved oculocephalic responses** early (confirming the gaze palsy is supranuclear), lost later;
- **erect, extended posture** (unlike flexed PD posture);
- axial more than limb rigidity;
- "**rocket sign**" (rising explosively from a chair) [general knowledge];
- **applause sign** (keeps clapping after being asked to clap three times) [3];
- blepharospasm, eyelid-opening apraxia;
- **growling dysarthria**, palilalia.

**MSA [1, 3]:**
- MSA-P about 80%, MSA-C about 20% (Gilman) [1];
- half faint from orthostatic hypotension;
- **bladder dysfunction is early**: detrusor hyperreflexia plus sphincter weakness, unlike PD, where incontinence is late [1];
- respiratory problems: **stridor**, sleep apnoea [1, 3];
- **cold, dusky hands**; Pisa syndrome [general knowledge].

**CBD/CBS [1, 3]:**
- one limb may be incapacitated before the other is affected;
- **alien limb** (medial frontal / supplementary motor area damage);
- limb-kinetic and ideomotor apraxia;
- stimulus-sensitive action myoclonus;
- cortical sensory loss;
- dementia in about one-third;
- **amyotrophy** in a subgroup [3].

### Assessment
| Investigation | Use |
| --- | --- |
| **Levodopa challenge / adequate trial** (up to 1,000 mg/day or more for 1–3 months) | Sustained good response favours PD. About **15% of "PD" never respond**; consider PSP or MSA [1] |
| **MRI** | **PSP:** midbrain axial diameter <17 mm; **"hummingbird"/"penguin" sign** (midsagittal midbrain atrophy); "**mouse ears**" / "**morning glory**" (axial) [1, 3]; raised midbrain-to-pons ratio [general knowledge]. **MSA-C:** pontine and cerebellar atrophy, **"hot cross bun" sign** [3]. **MSA-P:** putaminal T2 hypointensity (iron), **hyperintense putaminal rim** [3; general knowledge for rim]. **CBD:** asymmetric frontoparietal atrophy [1]. **Vascular:** white matter disease, basal ganglia lacunes |
| **DAT-SPECT (ioflupane)** | Abnormal in PD, PSP, MSA, CBD and DLB. **Normal in drug-induced parkinsonism, essential tremor, psychogenic parkinsonism**, mostly normal in vascular parkinsonism [1; general knowledge for the list]. Sensitivity about 90% for early PD vs normal or essential tremor [1] |
| **FDG-PET** | Disease-specific patterns: lentiform hypermetabolism in PD; midbrain and medial frontal hypometabolism in PSP; putamen, pons and cerebellum in MSA; asymmetric frontoparietal in CBD [1; general knowledge] |
| **Cardiac MIBG** | Reduced (postganglionic denervation) in PD and DLB; **normal in MSA** (preganglionic) [general knowledge] |
| **Autonomic testing** | Tilt table; postural BP (a fall of ≥30 systolic or ≥15 diastolic within 3 minutes for MSA criteria); bladder ultrasound for post-void residual |
| **Sphincter EMG** | Denervation separates PSP or MSA from PD, but **not PSP from MSA** [1] |
| **Polysomnography** | RBD (PD, DLB, MSA) |
| **Neuropsychology** | Frontal Assessment Battery (PSP); praxis testing (CBD); fluctuation scales (DLB) [general knowledge] |
| **Wilson screen** | Caeruloplasmin, 24-hour urinary copper, slit-lamp for KF rings in **everyone under 40–50** [1] |
| **Drug history** | Antipsychotics, metoclopramide, prochlorperazine, valproate, flunarizine and cinnarizine, lithium, calcium-channel blockers [1; general knowledge for the non-antipsychotics] |

### Differential diagnosis: key comparisons [3]
**PD vs MSA:**
| Feature | PD | MSA |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Onset | Asymmetric | More symmetric |
| Progression | Slow | Rapid, severe |
| Autonomic | Late, mild (orthostatic hypotension in about 15%, less severe) | Early, severe |
| Tremor | Pill-rolling rest tremor common | Uncommon; jerky postural tremor |
| Levodopa | Good, sustained | Poor or waning; craniocervical dyskinesias |
| Family history | Sometimes | Rarely |

**PD vs PSP:**
| Feature | PD | PSP |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Progression | Slow | Rapid |
| Eyes | Not prominent | Prominent vertical gaze palsy |
| Posture | Stooped, flexed | Rigid, erect |
| Falls | Late, infrequent | Early, frequent, backwards |
| Levodopa | Good | Poor |

**Drug-induced parkinsonism vs PD [1]:**
- Drug-induced parkinsonism is **symmetric**, usually appears within the **first month**, is dose-dependent and may coexist with akathisia or tardive dyskinesia. DAT-SPECT is **normal**.
- Remission may take weeks to months, occasionally **18 months**. If parkinsonism persists, an **unmasked underlying PD** is likely.

**Other mimics [1]:**
- **Essential tremor:** postural or kinetic tremor; family history; improves with alcohol; head titubation; no bradykinesia.
- **Depression:** psychomotor retardation; dual-task testing helps.
- **Catatonia.**
- **Hypothyroidism.**
- **Functional (psychogenic) parkinsonism:** distractible, variable tremor; **normal DAT-SPECT** [general knowledge].

### "Subcortical dementia" (Albert 1974, from PSP) [1]
- **Features:**
  - slowed thinking with prolonged latencies; correct answers come if given time (95% correct with encouragement in Albert's case, with latencies up to 4.5 minutes);
  - **retrieval** rather than storage memory deficit;
  - concrete abstraction; apathy, depression, irritability, euphoria;
  - forced laughing or crying;
  - **no aphasia, agnosia or apraxia**.
- **Mechanism:** impaired **timing and activation**, from disconnection of the reticular activating and subcortical–frontal systems.
- **Also seen in:** PD dementia, Huntington's, Wilson's, NPH, lacunar states.
- The cortical/subcortical dichotomy is now seen as a **spectrum**. Thalamic lesions can mimic Alzheimer's; Alzheimer pathology occurs in PD [1].

### Management details
**General [1, 3]:**
- a **levodopa trial** in all (some benefit in PSP-P, MSA-P);
- **multidisciplinary rehabilitation** (falls, speech, swallowing, PEG decisions);
- **advance care planning** (median survival 5–10 years).

**Disease-specific symptomatic treatment [1, 3; general knowledge]:**
- **PSP:**
  - amantadine;
  - zolpidem (transient benefit reported) [3];
  - **botulinum toxin** for blepharospasm and dystonia [3];
  - SSRIs for depression;
  - emotional incontinence: SSRI or dextromethorphan–quinidine.
- **MSA:**
  - orthostatic hypotension: **fludrocortisone, midodrine, droxidopa**, head-up tilt, salt and fluids, compression stockings;
  - bladder: anticholinergics (cognitive caution), intermittent catheterisation;
  - stridor: CPAP or tracheostomy.
- **CBD:**
  - **baclofen** for rigidity, **clonazepam** or levetiracetam for myoclonus [1];
  - botulinum toxin for dystonia.

**Psychiatric management:**
- **Depression:** SSRIs (sertraline, citalopram). Avoid TCAs because of orthostatic hypotension and anticholinergic effects, especially in MSA [general knowledge; see `parkinsons-disease-psychiatric-aspects`].
- **Apathy and disinhibition (PSP):** behavioural management and carer education. Drug evidence is lacking.
- **Psychosis:**
  - DLB and PD: **avoid typical antipsychotics and risperidone** (severe sensitivity reactions in DLB [2]);
  - cholinesterase inhibitors first in DLB;
  - **quetiapine or clozapine** at low dose.
- **Sleep:** RBD responds to **melatonin** or **clonazepam** (caution in the elderly) [general knowledge].
- **Capacity, driving and end-of-life decisions** become important early in these rapidly progressive disorders.

### Critical appraisal
- **Clinical–pathological mismatch** is the central problem. Up to a quarter of "PD" and many CBS cases have a different pathology at autopsy [1].
- Early disease is hardest. Many signs appear only **after years** (PSP gaze palsy typically after year 1) [3].
- Lishman (2009) predates the phenotype-based PSP criteria and the CBS/CBD distinction. These come from later literature [general knowledge].
- **DAT-SPECT** separates degenerative from non-degenerative parkinsonism, **not PD from Parkinson-plus** [general knowledge].
- Psychiatric phenomenology in rare disorders comes from **small series** [1].

### Indian context [general knowledge]
- **Wilson's disease** is relatively common in India (consanguinity). It must be excluded in young-onset parkinsonism and dystonia [1].
- **Drug-induced parkinsonism** from **flunarizine and cinnarizine** (widely prescribed for vertigo and migraine), metoclopramide and antipsychotics is common in Indian practice.
- **Manganese** exposure (welding, mining) is a regional toxic cause [1].
- Imaging is widely available, but **DAT-SPECT and MIBG** are limited to tertiary centres (NIMHANS, AIIMS, SGPGI).
- Postencephalitic parkinsonism, for example after **Japanese encephalitis** (thalamic and nigral involvement), is a relevant cause in endemic areas.

### Recent advances [general knowledge]
- **α-Synuclein seed amplification assays (RT-QuIC)** in CSF and skin. They distinguish synucleinopathies (PD, DLB, MSA) from tauopathies (PSP, CBD). MSA seeds show distinct kinetics.
- **Tau PET** (second-generation ligands, for example PI-2620) for PSP.
- **Plasma neurofilament light chain** is higher in atypical parkinsonism than in PD.
- **Automated MRI** indices (MR Parkinsonism Index) for PSP.
- Anti-tau antibody trials in PSP (gosuranemab, tilavonemab) were negative.
