## Exam answer

**Introduction.** **Parkinsonism** is a syndrome of four cardinal deficits [1]:
- **tremor**;
- **rigidity**;
- **bradykinesia**;
- **postural abnormality**.

Idiopathic PD is by far the commonest cause. Many other conditions affecting the basal ganglia cause **akinetic–rigid syndromes**. Misdiagnosis is common: at autopsy, **24% of clinically diagnosed PD** was another condition (PSP, MSA, Alzheimer's, vascular) [1].

### Classification of causes [1, 2]

| Group | Conditions |
| --- | --- |
| **Idiopathic (Lewy body) PD** | Sporadic; rare genetic forms (α-synuclein, parkin, LRRK2) |
| **Drug-induced** | **Neuroleptics** (phenothiazines, butyrophenones), reserpine, methyldopa |
| **Atypical (Parkinson-plus) degenerative** | **Progressive supranuclear palsy**, **multiple system atrophy**, **corticobasal degeneration**, **dementia with Lewy bodies**; also Alzheimer's with parkinsonism |
| **Heredodegenerative** | **Wilson's disease**; **rigid/akinetic forms of Huntington's disease**; **dopa-responsive dystonia** (parkinsonian features) |
| **Vascular** | "Arteriosclerotic"/vascular parkinsonism: about 3% of parkinsonism in elderly Europeans |
| **Post-infectious** | **Postencephalitic** (encephalitis lethargica): young onset, **oculogyric crises**, pupil abnormalities, sleep disturbance; cerebral syphilis |
| **Toxic** | **MPTP**, **carbon monoxide**, **manganese** |
| **Structural or diffuse brain damage** | **Repeated head injury (boxers)**, **anoxia** after cardiac arrest; tumour (strictly unilateral rigidity without tremor) |
| **Mimics (pseudo-parkinsonism)** | **Essential tremor**, **depression** (psychomotor retardation), **myxoedema**, arthritis or osteoporosis in the elderly, **"hysteria"/malingering** (early fluctuations), catatonia or stereotypies in schizophrenia |

### Approach: clinical clues [1, 2]
1. **Drug history first.**
   - Neuroleptic parkinsonism appears **within days to the first month** and is dose-dependent (20–40% of patients).
   - It is **bilateral and symmetrical**, bradykinesia-predominant, with **less tremor**.
   - Late perioral **"rabbit" tremor** can occur.
   - It resolves over weeks (occasionally **up to 18 months**) after stopping.
   - Persistence beyond that suggests underlying idiopathic PD.
   - Severe cases can resemble **catatonia** or akinetic mutism. Differentiate from **NMS**.
2. **Age at onset:**
   - **young onset** (adolescence or early adulthood): exclude **Wilson's disease**, juvenile HD, dopa-responsive dystonia, postencephalitic or genetic PD.
3. **Tempo:**
   - **acute or stepwise**, with pseudobulbar or pyramidal signs and *marche à petit pas*: vascular;
   - **rapid progression**: atypical parkinsonism.
4. **Symmetry and tremor:**
   - PD: asymmetric, rest tremor (presenting feature in 75%);
   - **CBD: marked asymmetry**;
   - drug-induced: symmetrical, little tremor;
   - **essential tremor**: postural or action tremor, head titubation, **improves with alcohol**, family history, no bradykinesia or rigidity, static for decades.
5. **Red-flag features of atypical parkinsonism:**
   - **early falls**;
   - **vertical gaze palsy**;
   - **early autonomic failure**;
   - **cerebellar signs**;
   - **pyramidal signs**;
   - **apraxia, alien limb, cortical sensory loss**;
   - **early dementia, fluctuations, hallucinations**;
   - **poor levodopa response**.
6. **Levodopa response:**
   - about 15% of "PD" patients do not respond: consider PSP or MSA;
   - DLB, CBD and PSP respond poorly or transiently;
   - **dopa-responsive dystonia** responds dramatically.
7. **Investigations:**
   - **caeruloplasmin, 24-hour urinary copper, slit lamp** (Kayser–Fleischer ring) in the young;
   - **thyroid function**;
   - **CT/MRI**: vascular lesions, tumour; **midbrain diameter <17 mm** in PSP; Wilson's basal ganglia signal ("face of the giant panda");
   - **DAT-SPECT/PET**: about 90% sensitivity for PD vs normal or essential tremor;
   - **¹⁸F-dopa PET**: PD has posterior-putamen predominance; PSP has uniform caudate and putamen loss; CBD is asymmetric;
   - **sphincter EMG**: separates PSP or MSA from PD, but not from each other.

### Atypical parkinsonian disorders (p3-eb8f3792) [1, 2]

| Feature | **PSP** (Steele–Richardson–Olszewski) | **MSA** (striatonigral degeneration / OPCA / Shy–Drager) | **CBD** | **DLB** |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Prevalence | 5–6 per 100,000 | 4.4 per 100,000 (up to 10% of parkinsonism?) | Rare | 10–20% of dementias (second or third commonest) |
| Onset and survival | 6th decade; death in **5–10 years** | 6th decade; median survival **9 years** | 7th decade; death in 4–10 years | 60s–70s; about 10 years (variable; shorter than AD) |
| Motor | Axial (trunk/neck) dystonic rigidity; **falls backwards**; pseudobulbar palsy; "growling" dysarthria | Parkinsonian (**MSA-P, 80%**) or cerebellar (**MSA-C, 20%**); pyramidal signs; myoclonus; dyskinesias | **Marked asymmetry**; limb rigidity, **limb apraxia**, **alien limb**, stimulus-sensitive action myoclonus, dystonia, **cortical sensory loss** | Rigidity and gait problems more than tremor; **falls** |
| Eyes | **Supranuclear vertical gaze palsy, especially downgaze**; "Mona Lisa stare"; eventually fixed gaze | Upgaze palsy sometimes | Supranuclear gaze palsy possible | — |
| Autonomic | — | **Central**: orthostatic hypotension (about half faint), **early bladder dysfunction** (detrusor hyperreflexia), impotence, constipation, respiratory dysfunction | — | Orthostatic hypotension, syncope, unexplained loss of consciousness |
| Cognition | **Subcortical dementia** (>80%): bradyphrenia, executive deficits, impaired retrieval; **no aphasia, apraxia or agnosia** | **Dementia not a feature**; mild frontal impairment | Often mild early despite apraxia; parietal deficits; dementia in about one-third | **Fluctuating cognition** (attention, visuoperception, executive); dementia **precedes or comes within 1 year of** parkinsonism |
| Psychiatric | **Apathy and disinhibition** (more than PD); depression about 54%; anxiety; emotional lability; forced laughing or crying; psychosis 18% | **Depression** (39% moderate–severe); emotional lability; sleep disorders and **RBD** | **Depression high**; less apathy; psychosis infrequent; rarely Klüver–Bucy or frontal aggression | **Complex visual hallucinations** (people or animals, small, silent); delusions; depression; **RBD**; **severe neuroleptic sensitivity** |
| Pathology | **4-repeat tauopathy**: straight-filament tangles, glial tau; basal ganglia, brainstem, cerebellar nuclei; cortex spared; H1/H1 haplotype | **α-synucleinopathy**: glial cytoplasmic inclusions (α-synuclein and tau positive); nigra without Lewy bodies | **4-repeat tauopathy**: perirolandic frontoparietal atrophy; achromatic **ballooned neurones**; H1 | **α-synucleinopathy**: cortical Lewy bodies; severe cholinergic deficit |
| Levodopa | May help briefly; usually disappointing | Variable (up to 68%) but wanes; dyskinesias | Usually ineffective; baclofen for rigidity, clonazepam for myoclonus | Not stated in the source |
| Imaging | Midbrain diameter <17 mm; midbrain and red nucleus signal change; uniform striatal dopa loss | — | **Asymmetric** cortical atrophy; asymmetric PET hypometabolism and striatal dopa loss | — |

### Other key differentials [1]
- **Vascular parkinsonism:**
  - historically suspected with acute or stepwise onset and *marche à petit pas*;
  - dementia, pseudobulbar or pyramidal signs;
  - only 1% reclassified in the UK Brain Bank;
  - worse prognosis.
- **Wilson's disease:**
  - more varied movements (chorea, athetosis, dystonia, **wing-beating/flapping tremor**);
  - liver disease, K–F rings, psychiatric change;
  - **must be excluded in anyone under 50 with dystonia or young-onset parkinsonism**.
- **Akinetic-rigid Huntington's disease** can mimic PD.
- **Depression:**
  - psychomotor retardation vs bradykinesia, which **dual-task tests** can separate;
  - depression may also be a PD prodrome.
- **Psychogenic:**
  - early fluctuating PD can be mistaken for "neurosis, hysteria or malingering", especially when function is normal under stress.

### Update from Continuum 2022 [3]
**Clinical clues from the MDS-PD criteria** (see the idiopathic PD answer). These point away from PD:
- vertical supranuclear gaze palsy or **selective vertical saccade slowing** (an absolute exclusion for PD);
- cerebellar signs; legs-only parkinsonism for more than 3 years;
- no response to **over 600 mg/day levodopa**;
- cortical sensory loss;
- **normal DaT imaging**.

**DaT-SPECT is abnormal in PD, MSA, PSP and CBS alike**, so it cannot separate them. It may be normal early in CBS.

**Progressive supranuclear palsy (PSP)** [3]:
- **Epidemiology:**
  - prevalence **5–7 per 100,000**; incidence rises with age from about 2 to about 15 per 100,000;
  - **mean onset 65** (early onset is 55 or under; under 40 is very unusual); no sex difference.
- **Pathology:** **4-repeat tau** in **tufted astrocytes** (most specific), oligodendrocytic coils and tangles, on the FTLD spectrum. **MAPT H1/H1** raises the risk.
- **Genetic mimic:** **Niemann–Pick type C** (vertical gaze palsy) is treatable with **miglustat**. Test early-onset or familial cases.
- **Criteria:**
  - **NINDS-SPSP (1996):** probable PSP = vertical gaze palsy plus falls within year 1. Specificity 95–100%, but **sensitivity only 24%**.
  - **MDS-PSP (2017):** **four domains** (ocular, postural instability, akinesia, cognitive), **eight predominance types**, and three certainty levels (probable, possible, suggestive). In an autopsy cohort, **sensitivity 88% and specificity 86%**.
  - The **most specific signs** are **vertical gaze palsy and selective vertical saccade slowing**, with **downward worse than upward**. Optokinetic nystagmus testing helps.

| Certainty | Variant | Definition (Table 7-1) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Probable | **PSP-Richardson syndrome** (about 50%) | Vertical gaze palsy or saccade impairment **plus unprovoked falls or a positive pull test within 3 years** |
| Probable | **PSP-parkinsonism** (second commonest, about 20% at autopsy) | Ocular sign plus parkinsonism (tremor or akinetic-rigid); **may respond to levodopa, but without fluctuations or dyskinesia**; hard to tell from PD for years |
| Probable | PSP-frontal | Ocular sign plus 3 of: apathy, bradyphrenia, dysexecutive syndrome, reduced phonemic fluency, impulsivity, disinhibition or perseveration |
| Probable | PSP-progressive gait freezing | Ocular sign plus levodopa-resistant gait freezing within 3 years |
| Possible | PSP-speech/language | Ocular sign plus non-fluent/agrammatic PPA or **apraxia of speech** |
| Possible | PSP-CBS ("probable 4-repeat tauopathy") | Ocular sign plus cortical signs (apraxia, cortical sensory loss, alien limb) and limb motor signs |
| Suggestive | PSP-ocular motor; PSP-postural instability | Single-domain early features that often evolve into PSP-RS |

- **Other signs:**
  - **retrocollis** (vs anterocollis in MSA); "growling" hypokinetic dysarthria; dysphagia;
  - **pseudobulbar affect**; insomnia (usually without RBD);
  - **procerus sign** (surprised look); eyelid-opening apraxia;
  - **rocket sign**; **applause sign**; grasp reflex.
- **MRI (PSP-RS):**
  - midbrain atrophy, seen as the **hummingbird or penguin** sign (sagittal) and **"Mickey Mouse" or morning-glory** sign (axial);
  - **midbrain:pons ratio under 0.5** on midsagittal T1 is **highly sensitive and specific** for PSP-RS (less reliable for other variants);
  - tau PET is not yet useful for individual diagnosis.
- **Prognosis:**
  - **PSP-RS:** dependence at about **4 years**, death at about **7.5 years** from onset;
  - **PSP-P:** most benign (more than 10 years is not unusual);
  - early falls, dementia and dysphagia predict survival; gaze palsy and levodopa response do not.

**Corticobasal syndrome (CBS) vs corticobasal degeneration (CBD)** [3]:
- **Terms:** CBS is the clinical syndrome; CBD is the **4-repeat tau pathology** (astrocytic **plaques**).
- **Pathology behind CBS:**
  - **CBD in only about 50%**;
  - **PSP 20–25%**, **Alzheimer's 20–25%**;
  - rarely TDP-43, Pick or Lewy pathology.
- **Epidemiology:** incidence under 1 per 100,000; about **5%** of parkinsonism in clinics; **mean onset 64**; sex ratio equal.
- **Armstrong 2013 criteria (Table 7-2):**
  - **Probable CBS:** **asymmetric**, with **2 of** limb rigidity/akinesia, limb dystonia, limb myoclonus **plus 2 of** orobuccal or limb apraxia, cortical sensory deficit, alien limb. **Possible CBS:** may be symmetric, with 1 plus 1.
  - **Other CBD phenotypes:** **frontal behavioural–spatial syndrome**; **non-fluent/agrammatic PPA**; a **PSP-like syndrome** (3 of: axial or symmetric rigidity, falls, incontinence, behavioural change, vertical gaze palsy).
  - The criteria **lack specificity**.
- **Clinical features:**
  - **alien limb in only about 20%** of autopsy-proven CBD;
  - limb apraxia (ideomotor); cortical myoclonus;
  - optic ataxia or Balint syndrome; acalculia, agraphia, finger agnosia, right–left confusion;
  - **memory loss suggests underlying Alzheimer's pathology**.
- **MRI:** asymmetric **frontoparietal** atrophy, though MRI asymmetry correlates poorly with clinical asymmetry.
- **Survival:** about 7 years (range 2.5–12.5); death from aspiration or urosepsis.

**PSP and CBS treatment:**
- **Levodopa trial for all:** build up to **900–1200 mg/day** and hold for **at least 1 month** before tapering.
  - Only about 10% of PSP-RS respond subjectively; PSP-P often responds.
  - **CBS is almost always unresponsive.**
- **Avoid other dopaminergic drugs and anticholinergics.**
- **Psychiatric symptoms:**
  - **pseudobulbar affect:** SSRIs or **dextromethorphan/quinidine**;
  - **depression and anxiety:** SSRIs at standard doses;
  - **apathy:** **modafinil or methylphenidate**.
- **Botulinum toxin:** sialorrhoea, dystonia, pain, eyelid-opening apraxia.
- **Rehabilitation:** physio, OT, speech and swallow therapy, regular swallow assessments.
- **Trials:** anti-tau antibodies (tilavonemab, gosuranemab) have **failed**; others are ongoing.

**Multiple system atrophy (MSA)** [4]:
- **Pathology:** an **α-synucleinopathy** with **glial cytoplasmic inclusions** in oligodendrocytes. Life expectancy is **6–10 years** after diagnosis.
- **MSA-P and MSA-C overlap**, especially over time.
- **Prodrome:**
  - **RBD and autonomic failure**;
  - urinary retention in up to 20% (**Onuf nucleus**);
  - pure autonomic failure that converts to MSA occurs in younger patients with **intact smell** and a heart-rate rise on standing.
- **Red-flag signs:**
  - pyramidal signs, striatal toe;
  - **early falls and rapid progression**;
  - **facial dystonia and levodopa-induced facial dyskinesia**;
  - ataxic dysarthria, dysphagia, **anterocollis**;
  - **cold hands and purple glove**; early severe urinary symptoms;
  - **stridor**; **"coat-hanger" neck pain**; myoclonus.
- **Tests:**
  - orthostatic vitals; tilt table;
  - **normal plasma noradrenaline** (low in pure autonomic failure);
  - **impaired thermoregulatory sweating with a normal QSART** (preganglionic lesion);
  - **cardiac MIBG preserved in MSA but reduced in PD** (heart-to-mediastinum cut-off 1.7);
  - **skin biopsy:** phosphorylated α-synuclein mainly in **somatic** fibres in MSA vs autonomic fibres in PD;
  - CSF neurofilament light (research).
- **MRI:**
  - **hot-cross-bun sign** (pons);
  - **putaminal hyperintense rim** (iron);
  - putaminal, pontine, middle cerebellar peduncle and cerebellar atrophy;
  - **FDG-PET:** cerebellar and lentiform hypometabolism.
- **Treatment:**
  - **levodopa** (a durable response averages **3 years**);
  - **levetiracetam** for cerebellar outflow tremor;
  - **orthostatic hypotension:** midodrine, droxidopa, fludrocortisone, pyridostigmine, low-dose atomoxetine, with **head-up tilt for supine hypertension**;
  - **bladder:** **β3 agonists first**, trospium if antimuscarinic, self-catheterisation;
  - **stridor and sleep apnoea:** CPAP or BiPAP (**sleep problems are a major cause of death**);
  - **RBD:** melatonin or clonazepam (confirm with PSG, because stridor can mimic dream enactment);
  - **mood:** SSRIs for anxiety, depression and lability; palliative care for demoralisation;
  - **UMSARS Part 1** to direct care.
- Rapid decline after STN-DBS for presumed PD may turn out to be MSA.

**DLB:** for the 2017 McKeith criteria, see the dementia classification answer (Continuum 2024).

⚠️ **Discrepancies with Lishman (above), not resolved:**
- **PSP cognition:** Lishman says PSP has "no aphasia, apraxia or agnosia". Continuum describes **prominent apraxia** in PSP, a **speech-language variant** with non-fluent PPA, and frontal-executive impairment.
- **MSA levodopa:** Lishman says the response is variable (up to 68%) but wanes. Continuum says a durable effect lasts **on average 3 years**.
- **PSP survival:** Lishman gives 5–10 years; Continuum gives about **7.5 years** for PSP-RS and longer for PSP-P (consistent, but variant-dependent).
- **PSP imaging:** Lishman gives midbrain diameter under 17 mm; Continuum gives the **midbrain:pons ratio under 0.5**.

**Conclusion.** Approach parkinsonism by:
1. excluding **drugs** first;
2. using **age, tempo, symmetry and red flags** (early falls, gaze palsy, autonomic failure, cerebellar or cortical signs, early dementia or hallucinations, poor levodopa response) to separate **idiopathic PD** from **PSP, MSA, CBD and DLB**;
3. never missing **Wilson's disease** in the young;
4. supporting the diagnosis with DAT/PET imaging, MRI and copper studies.

The distinction matters: prognosis, levodopa response and **neuroleptic sensitivity** (DLB) differ sharply.

## In depth

### "Subcortical dementia" (Albert 1974, from PSP) [1]
- **Features:**
  - slowed thinking with prolonged latencies; correct answers come if given time;
  - **retrieval** rather than storage memory deficit;
  - concrete abstraction; apathy, depression, irritability, euphoria;
  - forced laughing or crying;
  - **no aphasia, agnosia or apraxia**.
- **Mechanism:** disconnection of the reticular activating and subcortical–frontal systems.
- **Also seen in:** PD dementia, Huntington's, Wilson's, NPH, lacunar states.
- The cortical/subcortical dichotomy is now seen as a **spectrum**.

### Not covered by the sources (deliberately left blank)
- Gilman 2008 / MDS 2022 MSA diagnostic criteria text (Continuum cites them but does not list them). MDS-PSP and Armstrong criteria are now covered; DLB 2017 criteria are in the dementia classification answer.
- Drug-induced parkinsonism from non-antipsychotic drugs.
- Normal-pressure hydrocephalus as a cause of gait apraxia/parkinsonism. Lishman mentions it only as a subcortical dementia.
