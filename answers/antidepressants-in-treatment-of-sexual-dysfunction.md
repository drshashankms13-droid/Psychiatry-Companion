## Exam answer

**Definition of the topic.** Antidepressants usually *cause* sexual dysfunction, but several are used to *treat* sexual problems. Uses include:
- **premature (early) ejaculation (PE):** SSRIs, clomipramine, dapoxetine;
- **psychogenic erectile dysfunction**, especially with depression: trazodone;
- **antidepressant-induced or hypoactive desire:** bupropion;
- **paraphilic disorders and compulsive sexual behaviour:** SSRIs;
- **sexual dysfunction secondary to depression or anxiety:** an antidepressant chosen for a low sexual side-effect profile;
- **dhat syndrome** with comorbid depression or anxiety [1,2].

### Mechanism
- **Serotonin (5-HT2C/5-HT1B)** stimulation in the brainstem (nucleus paragigantocellularis) and spinal ejaculation generator → **delays ejaculation** (raises intravaginal ejaculatory latency time, IELT).
- **Trazodone:** α1-adrenergic antagonism → reduced detumescence, raised penile blood retention (also causes priapism); 5-HT2 antagonism.
- **Bupropion:** dopamine and noradrenaline reuptake inhibition → increased desire and arousal.
- **SSRIs in paraphilias:** reduced sexual drive and compulsivity, impulse control.
- **Treating depression or anxiety** removes the psychological cause of low desire or performance anxiety [1,2].

### Indications and agents

### Assessment before prescribing
Diagnose the specific sexual dysfunction (DSM-5-TR/ICD-11): lifelong vs acquired, generalised vs situational. For PE: IELT (lifelong usually <1 minute; acquired ≤3 minutes), lack of control, distress. Assess psychiatric comorbidity, relationship, medical causes (prostatitis, thyroid for PE; vascular for ED), drug interactions, and contraindications (cardiac disease for TCAs; BP for dapoxetine) [1].

### Management principles
Psychoeducation and sex education → behavioural techniques (**stop–start**, **squeeze**, sensate focus) with 60–95% reported success → pharmacotherapy (daily SSRI, on-demand dapoxetine or clomipramine, topical anaesthetics) → **combined** behavioural and drug treatment is best, because drug effects wear off after stopping → treat comorbid ED (PDE-5) and psychiatric disorders [1].

### Key evidence
- **Waldinger 2004 meta-analysis:** daily paroxetine gives the largest IELT increase (about 8.8-fold) vs fluoxetine, clomipramine and sertraline.
- **Pryor 2006 (Lancet, n=2,614):** dapoxetine 30/60 mg increased IELT from ~0.9 to 2.8–3.3 minutes vs 1.9 with placebo.
- **Trazodone systematic review (Fink 2003):** benefit in psychogenic ED [2].
- **Clayton 2004:** bupropion SR improved desire in premenopausal women with HSDD.

**Conclusion.** Antidepressants have a genuine therapeutic role in sexual medicine: serotonergic drugs for premature ejaculation and paraphilic urges, trazodone for psychogenic ED with depression, and bupropion for low desire. They work best combined with behavioural sex therapy and careful choice of agent when depression and sexual dysfunction coexist.

## In depth

### Background
The ejaculation-delaying effect of clomipramine was noticed in the 1970s (Eaton 1973). SSRIs were used off-label from the 1990s. Dapoxetine, a short-acting SSRI, was developed specifically for PE and approved in Europe and India (2009–2012), though not in the US. Trazodone's pro-erectile effect was noticed through priapism cases. Bupropion's pro-sexual profile was recognised in the 1990s.

### Epidemiology
- PE is the commonest male sexual dysfunction (20–30% self-reported; lifelong PE by strict criteria ~2–5%).
- In Indian clinics, PE and dhat syndrome are common presenting complaints in young men [1].
- HSDD in women affects about 10%.
- Paraphilic disorders are rare clinically; compulsive sexual behaviour disorder (ICD-11) affects about 3–6%.

### Classification and criteria
- **DSM-5-TR premature (early) ejaculation:** ejaculation within ~1 minute of vaginal penetration and before the person wishes, on 75–100% of occasions for ≥6 months, with distress. Lifelong vs acquired; generalised vs situational; mild (30–60 s), moderate (15–30 s), severe (<15 s).
- **ICD-11 HA03.0 male early ejaculation.**
- **ISSM definition:** lifelong PE within ~1 minute; acquired ≤3 minutes; inability to delay; negative personal consequences.
- ICD-11 paraphilic disorders (6D3x); compulsive sexual behaviour disorder (6C72).

### Mechanism in detail
- **Ejaculation neurobiology:**
  - Emission (sympathetic T10–L2) and expulsion (somatic S2–S4, bulbospongiosus) are coordinated by the spinal ejaculation generator.
  - Supraspinal: medial preoptic area (dopamine, pro-ejaculatory) and paragigantocellular nucleus (serotonergic, inhibitory).
  - The **5-HT hypothesis of lifelong PE (Waldinger):** hyposensitive 5-HT2C or hypersensitive 5-HT1A receptors; 5-HTTLPR variants.
- **Acquired PE:** prostatitis, hyperthyroidism, ED (rushing to ejaculate), psychological factors.
- **On-demand vs daily SSRIs:** daily dosing causes 5-HT1A autoreceptor desensitisation and gives greater delay (needs 1–2 weeks). Dapoxetine's short half-life allows on-demand use [1].

### Clinical features in detail
PE presents with a short IELT, poor control, distress and avoidance; partner dissatisfaction; secondary ED; and anxiety. In India, it is often associated with dhat beliefs and fears about masturbation [1].

### Assessment in detail
- **Tools:** Premature Ejaculation Diagnostic Tool (PEDT), Index of Premature Ejaculation, stopwatch IELT (research), IIEF (ED).
- **Investigations** (acquired PE): TSH, urine examination (prostatitis), testosterone and prolactin if indicated.
- Assess relationship, sexual knowledge and myths [1].

### Differential diagnosis
- Normal variation ("subjective PE"): normal IELT but perceived as short.
- PE secondary to ED.
- Substance withdrawal (opioid withdrawal causes PE).
- Prostatitis, hyperthyroidism.
- Anxiety disorders, dhat syndrome [1].

### Management details
- **Behavioural:**
  - Stop–start (Semans): stimulate to near-ejaculation, stop until the urge subsides, repeat.
  - Squeeze (Masters & Johnson): partner squeezes the glans at the coronal ridge.
  - These are introduced during genital sensate focus; reported success 60–95% [1].
  - Pelvic floor exercises.
- **Pharmacological:** see the table. Combination treatment with dapoxetine + behavioural therapy gives better long-term results. Avoid dapoxetine with MAOIs, thioridazine, potent CYP3A4 inhibitors, and in significant cardiac disease [1].
- **Safety:** SSRIs can cause their own sexual side effects (low libido, anorgasmia) and discontinuation symptoms. TCAs carry cardiotoxicity in overdose. Trazodone carries priapism risk (counsel to seek urgent care if an erection lasts >4 hours).
- **Paraphilias:** SSRIs for mild cases; GnRH agonists (leuprolide) or cyproterone for high-risk cases; plus CBT and relapse prevention.

### Critical appraisal
- PE definitions changed (IELT-based); many trials used heterogeneous criteria.
- SSRIs for PE are off-label except dapoxetine; relapse after stopping is common.
- Trazodone evidence for ED is modest and mainly in psychogenic ED.
- Paraphilia trials are mostly uncontrolled.
- Patient preference matters: some prefer on-demand over daily treatment; side effects limit long-term use.

### Indian context
- The **IPS CPG** recommends: for depression with PE, choose SSRIs or TCAs; for depression with ED, choose trazodone. It also gives treatment tables for PE with doses [1].
- Dapoxetine is approved and widely available in India (including dapoxetine–sildenafil fixed-dose combinations: caution about syncope) [1].
- Dhat syndrome algorithm: treat comorbid depression and anxiety with antidepressants, give sex education, treat comorbid PE or ED [1].
- Over-the-counter "timing" sprays and pills are common; counsel patients.

### Recent advances
- **Established:** on-demand dapoxetine; topical eutectic lidocaine–prilocaine spray (Fortacin).
- **Emerging:** modafinil, silodosin (α1A blocker) for PE; oxytocin antagonists (investigational).
- **Investigational:** selective 5-HT1A antagonists; botulinum toxin to the bulbospongiosus.
