## Exam answer

**Definition.** Interpersonal psychotherapy (IPT) is a **time-limited, manualised, non-psychodynamic** psychotherapy developed in the 1970s by **Gerald Klerman, Myrna Weissman** and colleagues for non-bipolar, non-psychotic depressed outpatients [1]. It aims to reduce depressive symptoms by improving the quality of the patient's **current interpersonal relationships and social functioning** [1].

### Theoretical basis [1]

- **Roots:** Harry Stack Sullivan's interpersonal school (1920s–1940s) held that personality and psychopathology arise from interactions with others; Adolf Meyer's psychobiological approach and Bowlby's attachment theory also contributed.
- **Assumptions:** IPT makes no claim about the cause of depression. It assumes that depression **develops and is maintained in a social and interpersonal context**, and that onset, response and outcome are influenced by relationships with significant others.
- **Focus:** here and now; current relationships rather than childhood origins; transference is not interpreted.

### The four problem areas [1]

| Problem area | Description | Goals | Strategies |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Grief** | Onset linked to death of a loved one (or loss of a relationship or identity) | Facilitate mourning; help find new activities and relationships | Reconstruct the relationship with the deceased, positive and negative; explore feelings; counter idealisation |
| **Interpersonal role disputes** | Conflict with a significant other from non-reciprocal expectations | Identify the dispute; modify expectations and faulty communication; resolve or dissolve | Determine stage: **renegotiation** (calm down), **impasse** (reopen negotiation), **dissolution** (assist mourning) |
| **Role transitions** | Change in life status: marriage, divorce, childbirth, job loss, retirement, migration, medical illness | Mourn the old role; see positives and negatives of both; build mastery in the new role | Explore feelings about what is lost; develop new skills and social support |
| **Interpersonal deficits** | Social isolation or chronically unfulfilling relationships | Reduce isolation; improve existing and form new relationships | Review past relationships for repetitive patterns; use the therapeutic relationship as a model |

One, or at most two, problem areas are chosen; the most soluble is tackled first [1].

### Structure: 12–20 weekly sessions over 4–5 months [1]

**Initial phase (sessions 1–5)**
- Diagnose depression and **give the syndrome a name**; psychoeducation on its prevalence, treatability and good prognosis.
- **Assign the sick role:** gives permission and responsibility to recover, and temporarily exempts the patient from some obligations.
- **Interpersonal inventory:** for each important person, frequency of contact, shared activities, satisfying and unsatisfying aspects, and desired changes; chronological link between life events and mood.
- **Interpersonal formulation** linking symptoms to the problem area; the patient must agree before work begins.
- Collaborative, specific goals, often given in writing as a treatment contract.

**Intermediate phase (sessions 6–15)**
- Apply strategies specific to the problem area.
- Repeatedly link **mood changes to interpersonal events** of the week.
- Sessions open with "How have things been since we last met?" to elicit recent interpersonal events and symptoms; unfocused complaining is redirected.

**Termination phase (sessions 16–20)**
- Discuss ending explicitly as a potential time of **grief**.
- Review progress and credit the patient's own work.
- Identify early warning signs and plan for future difficulties; depression framed as a vulnerability that may recur.

### Techniques [1]

| Technique | Use |
| --- | --- |
| Exploratory questions | Open, then progressively specific |
| Encouragement of affect | Accept painful affect; use it to change relationships; bring out suppressed affect |
| Clarification | Point out contradictions between words and affect |
| **Communication analysis** | Detailed reconstruction of a recent argument to identify faulty communication |
| Decision analysis and role play | Generate and rehearse alternatives |
| Use of the therapeutic relationship | Mainly in interpersonal deficits and role disputes |

**Therapeutic stance:** warm, supportive, active and an **advocate** for the patient; the relationship is neither a transference object nor a friendship [1].

### How IPT differs from other therapies [1]

| Feature | IPT | CBT | Psychodynamic |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Target | Interpersonal relationships | Cognitions and behaviour | Unconscious conflict |
| Time frame | Present | Present | Past and present |
| Transference | Not interpreted | Not used | Central |
| Homework | Informal interpersonal tasks | Structured | None |
| Personality | Recognised, not targeted | Schemas targeted in later work | Central |

Cognitions and behaviours are addressed in IPT only insofar as they bear on relationships [1].

### Indications and evidence [1]

- **Efficacy established:** acute non-psychotic major depression, **recurrent depression (maintenance)**, dysthymia, **postpartum and antenatal depression**, bipolar disorder (as **IPSRT**), bulimia nervosa and binge-eating disorder.
- APA practice guideline: useful in depression when there is **conflict with significant others or difficulty adjusting to a life transition**.
- **NIMH Treatment of Depression Collaborative Research Program (TDCRP):** IPT comparable to imipramine and better than placebo in severe depression; CBT comparable.
- **Pittsburgh maintenance study (Frank 1990):** monthly maintenance IPT delayed recurrence compared with placebo; medication was more effective.
- Group IPT for depression in rural **Uganda** (Bolton 2003) showed that IPT can be delivered by lay workers.

### Adaptations [1]

IPT-A (adolescents), IPT for older adults, **IPSRT** (interpersonal and social rhythm therapy for bipolar disorder), group IPT (20 sessions, pre-, mid- and post-group individual meetings), conjoint marital IPT, telephone IPT, IPC (interpersonal counselling for primary care).

### Limitations and contraindications [1]

- Not developed for **psychotic depression** or schizophrenia; not effective for substance use disorders.
- Evidence in anxiety disorders is still evolving.
- Few complications; non-responders after a full course should receive an alternative evidence-based treatment.
- Ethical risk: the therapist may impose values (for example pushing a patient to leave a relationship).

**Conclusion.** IPT is a brief, structured, evidence-based treatment that treats depression as a medical illness occurring in an interpersonal context. It works through four problem areas (grief, role disputes, role transitions, interpersonal deficits) across three phases and is particularly suited to depression triggered by loss, conflict or life change, including perinatal depression.

## In depth

### Historical development

- **Sullivan** proposed two basic drives: physical satisfaction and interpersonal security (self-esteem, competence, freedom from anxiety). He described social rather than psychosexual stages of development [1].
- **Insight-oriented interpersonal therapies:** Frieda Fromm-Reichmann, Harold Searles, Karen Horney and Hilda Bruch blended Sullivan with psychoanalysis; Donald Kiesler developed a dyadic model in which the therapist monitors **"impact messages"** (feelings, action tendencies, attributions, fantasies) evoked by the patient, with four phases: hooking, uncovering, resolving and termination. These lack rigorous trials [1].
- Klerman's time-limited IPT was written for the research setting; its main advantage over earlier interpersonal therapies is that it can be tested in trials [1].

### Sick role in detail

Drawing on Parsons, the sick role is temporary. It legitimises recovery as the main task, which helps patients who habitually put others' needs first (for example a mother caring for the extended family while depressed). The therapist redirects this energy toward recovery [1].

### Interpersonal inventory: what to record [1]

| Item | Example question |
| --- | --- |
| Frequency of contact | "How often do you see your mother-in-law?" |
| Shared activities | "What do you do together?" |
| Satisfying aspects | "What do you value in this relationship?" |
| Unsatisfying aspects | "What would you change?" |
| Changes around onset | Death, job change, marital discord, losing a friend |

### Role dispute stages in practice

| Stage | Feature | Therapist action |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Renegotiation | Both parties aware and trying to resolve | Calm the participants and facilitate negotiation |
| Impasse | Discussion has stopped; resentment smoulders | Bring the dispute back into the open to reopen negotiation |
| Dissolution | Relationship irretrievable | Help end it and mourn the loss |

### Communication analysis in practice

The patient reconstructs an argument line by line: what was said, what was meant, how it was received and the outcome. Typical faults include indirect, non-verbal or ambiguous messages, incorrect assumptions that the other person knows one's needs, silence, and closing off communication [1].

### Group IPT [1]

- 6–9 members, one or two leaders, 20 sessions over 5 months.
- **Pre-group meeting (2 hours):** sick role, inventory, problem area and written goals.
- **Mid-treatment meeting** between sessions 10 and 11: review and recontract.
- **Post-group meeting** within one week of ending: individual plan.
- Weekly written **group summaries** (Wilfley) or self-help manuals maintain individual focus.
- Phases are linked to group development stages: engagement, differentiation, work, termination.

### IPT in perinatal depression

The transition to parenthood maps onto **role transitions**; marital conflict onto **role disputes**. O'Hara and colleagues (2000) showed IPT superior to wait-list for postpartum depression, and Spinelli (2003) adapted it for antenatal depression [1]. For women who wish to avoid medication during pregnancy or breastfeeding, IPT is a first-line option for mild to moderate depression.

### Mechanisms (proposed)

Mechanisms are little studied [1]. Suggested pathways include increased social support, better communication and conflict resolution, mastery of new roles, and reduction of the stress that maintains depression. Attachment theory provides one model: interpersonal distress activates insecure attachment, which IPT addresses by strengthening current attachments.

### Critical appraisal

- **Strengths:** brief, manualised, teachable to non-specialists, culturally adaptable, acceptable to patients, with evidence from high-income countries and LMICs.
- **Weaknesses:** limited dissemination outside research settings; few trained therapists; mechanisms unclear; in TDCRP, differences between active treatments were small, raising the question of common factors.
- **Choice between IPT and CBT:** largely equivalent for depression overall; patient preference and the presence of a clear interpersonal trigger favour IPT.

### Indian context

- Family-centred living makes **role disputes** (with spouse or in-laws) and **role transitions** (marriage, migration, childbirth, retirement) common precipitants, which suits the IPT model.
- Involving family members, with consent, may help resolve disputes.
- WHO's **Group Interpersonal Therapy (IPT-G) manual** (2016) and lay-counsellor delivery models offer a route to scale in primary care.
- Values about marriage and gender roles require the therapist to avoid imposing personal views on whether a relationship should continue.

### Recent advances

- IPT delivered by lay health workers in LMICs.
- Internet-based and telephone IPT.
- IPT for adolescents (IPT-A) and IPT-AST for prevention of adolescent depression.
