## Exam answer

**Definition.** Interpersonal psychotherapy (IPT) is a **time-limited, structured, non-psychodynamic psychotherapy** developed in the **1970s by Gerald Klerman, Myrna Weissman and colleagues** for non-bipolar, non-psychotic depressed outpatients [1]. It focuses on the **current interpersonal context** in which depression begins and persists, and aims to relieve symptoms by improving relationships and social functioning [1].

### Theoretical basis [1]

- Roots in **Harry Stack Sullivan's** interpersonal psychiatry (1920s–40s) and **Adolf Meyer's** psychobiology; attachment theory (Bowlby) also informs it (Meyer and Bowlby from author's knowledge).
- Makes **no assumption about cause** — only that depression develops and is maintained in a social context, and that relationships influence onset, response and outcome.
- Differs from CBT: thoughts and behaviours are addressed only as they relate to relationships; the aim is to change **relationship patterns**, not cognitions. Differs from psychodynamic therapy: **here and now**, no transference interpretation, little interest in origins.

### The four interpersonal problem areas [1]

| Problem area | Description | Goals | Strategies |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Grief** | Complicated bereavement after death (or loss of a relationship or identity) | Facilitate mourning; re-engage with activities and relationships | Reconstruct the relationship with the deceased — positive and negative; explore feelings |
| **Interpersonal role disputes** | Conflict with a significant other from non-reciprocal expectations | Identify the dispute; modify expectations and communication; resolve or dissolve | Determine **stage**: renegotiation, **impasse**, dissolution |
| **Role transitions** | Change in life status — marriage, divorce, childbirth, job change, retirement, migration, illness | Mourn the old role; accept the new; regain mastery and self-esteem | Review pros and cons of old and new roles; build skills and support |
| **Interpersonal deficits** | Social isolation or chronically unfulfilling relationships | Reduce isolation; improve existing and build new relationships | Review past relationships, recurrent patterns; use therapist relationship as a model |

### Structure — 12–20 weekly sessions over 4–5 months [1]

**Initial phase (sessions 1–5)**
- Diagnose depression and give it a name; psychoeducation; **assign the sick role** (permission and responsibility to recover).
- **Interpersonal inventory** — review each important relationship.
- **Interpersonal formulation** — link symptoms to one (at most two) problem areas; agree goals (treatment contract).

**Intermediate phase (sessions 6–15)**
- Problem-area strategies; link mood changes to interpersonal events each week; manage painful affects.

**Termination phase (sessions 16–20)**
- Discuss ending as a potential grief; review progress; identify warning signs; plan future work.

**Techniques:** exploratory questioning, encouragement of affect, clarification, **communication analysis**, decision analysis, role play, use of the therapeutic relationship. Therapist stance: warm, active, supportive advocate [1] (technique list partly author's knowledge).

### Evidence and indications [1]

- Efficacious in **major depression**, recurrent depression, **dysthymia**, **postpartum depression**, bipolar disorder (as IPSRT), bulimia nervosa and **binge-eating disorder**.
- Recommended by the **APA practice guideline** for patients with conflicts with significant others or difficulty adjusting to life transitions.
- **NIMH Treatment of Depression Collaborative Research Program (1989):** IPT comparable to imipramine and better than placebo in mild-to-moderate depression; maintenance IPT (monthly) delays recurrence (Frank et al., 1990) (author's knowledge).
- Adapted for adolescents (IPT-A), older adults, HIV, cancer, primary care, telephone delivery and **group IPT**.

**Not indicated:** psychotic depression and schizophrenia; not effective for substance use disorders [1].

**Conclusion.** IPT is a focused, manualised therapy that treats depression by working on one of four interpersonal problem areas — grief, role disputes, role transitions or interpersonal deficits — over 12–20 sessions. It has strong evidence in acute and maintenance treatment of depression, including postpartum depression, and is well suited to task-sharing and group delivery in low-resource settings.

## In depth

### Background

- Sullivan proposed two basic drives — physical satisfaction and interpersonal security — and that self-esteem and coping are learned in relationships [1].
- Insight-oriented interpersonal therapies (IO-IPT) exist, but the time-limited IPT is distinct and researchable [1].
- Klerman and Weissman's manual (1984) and later *Comprehensive Guide to IPT* (Weissman, Markowitz, Klerman) define the model (author's knowledge for dates).

### Mechanism

Interpersonal stress (loss, conflict, change, isolation) ↔ depressive symptoms (withdrawal, irritability, poor communication) → worsened relationships → maintenance of depression. Improving relationships and social support reverses the cycle. The specific mechanisms of change are not established [1].

### Assessment in detail — the interpersonal inventory [1]

For each important person: frequency of contact, shared activities, satisfying and unsatisfying aspects, how the patient would like the relationship to change. Plus a chronological history linking life events, mood and self-esteem.

### Role-dispute stages [1]

| Stage | Description | Therapist action |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Renegotiation | Parties actively trying to resolve | Calm participants, facilitate resolution |
| Impasse | Discussion has stopped; silent resentment | Increase disharmony to reopen negotiation |
| Dissolution | Relationship irretrievably broken | Assist mourning and adaptation |

### Group IPT [1]

- About 20 sessions over 5 months, preceded by individual pre-group meetings.
- Phases map onto group development: engagement (sessions 1–2), differentiation (3–5), work (6–15), termination (16–20).
- Evidence in depression, postpartum depression and binge-eating disorder.

### Adaptations [1]

| Adaptation | Population |
| --- | --- |
| IPT-A | Depressed adolescents |
| IPSRT (interpersonal and social rhythm therapy) | Bipolar disorder (author's knowledge) |
| IPT for postpartum depression | Role transition to motherhood |
| IPT-G | Group format, including in low-income countries |
| Telephone IPT | Remote delivery |

### Evidence table

| Study | Finding |
| --- | --- |
| NIMH TDCRP (Elkin et al., 1989) | IPT ≈ imipramine > placebo for mild–moderate depression (author's knowledge) |
| Frank et al., 1990 | Monthly maintenance IPT delayed recurrence (author's knowledge) |
| Bolton et al., 2003 (Uganda) | Group IPT by lay workers reduced depression (author's knowledge) |
| Multicentre bulimia trial (Agras et al.) | CBT faster, IPT caught up at follow-up [1] |

### Critical appraisal

- Strong efficacy evidence, but mechanisms unclear and dissemination among clinicians poor [1].
- Trials largely in mild-to-moderate depression; severe and psychotic depression need medication or ECT.
- Fewer trained IPT therapists than CBT therapists in most countries, including India.

### Indian context

- Interpersonal stressors — marital conflict, joint family disputes, role transitions (marriage, migration, childbirth) — are common precipitants of depression in India, making IPT culturally relevant.
- Group IPT and IPT delivered by trained lay health workers (as in Uganda) support **task-sharing** within the District Mental Health Programme (author's knowledge).
- Family involvement may be adapted, e.g. joint sessions in role disputes.

### Recent advances

- WHO's **Group Interpersonal Therapy (IPT) for depression** manual (2016) for low-resource settings (author's knowledge).
- Digital and telephone IPT; IPT in perinatal and adolescent prevention programmes [1].
