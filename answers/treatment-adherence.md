## Exam answer

**Definitions [1].**
- **WHO:** adherence is "the extent to which a person's behaviour — taking medication, following a diet and/or executing lifestyle changes — corresponds with agreed recommendations from a healthcare provider".
- **NICE:** "the extent to which the patient's action matches the agreed recommendations".
- **"Compliance"** is seen as **patronising**, because it denies the patient's agency.
- **"Concordance"** means agreement between patient and doctor: part of informed consent and a "prescribing partnership". But agreement does not guarantee action.
- The first description of non-adherence comes from **Hippocrates**.

**Magnitude [1]:**
- About **50%** of people with mental health problems do not take medication as prescribed (2020 meta-analysis).
- Most patients are **partially** adherent; a few are fully adherent; a few never take any.
- **Schizophrenia after discharge:** up to **25%** non-adherent at 10 days, **50% at 1 year, 75% at 2 years**.
- **By diagnosis:** schizophrenia 66%, affective disorders 47%, others 41% (Austrian study).
- **Self-report overstates adherence:** 76% reported adhering vs **44%** on electronic monitoring.
- **Hidden from the prescriber:**
  - prescribers identified only half of non-adherent patients;
  - **35%** of patients referred for "refractory" schizophrenia had sub-therapeutic or **zero** plasma levels.
- **Psychotherapy too:** 34% did not attend the first assessment session.

**Impact [1, 2]:**
- **Relapse** in schizophrenia, bipolar disorder and depression.
- Antipsychotic use lowered **suicide and overall mortality** (20-year cohort of 62,250).
- Depressed patients not taking antidepressants had **20% more MIs**.
- Providers with a **policy on non-adherence had 20% fewer suicides** (National Confidential Inquiry).
- **Abrupt unmonitored stopping** worsens prognosis.
- **Medical illness:**
  - depression and anxiety bring a **threefold** higher rate of non-adherence to medical regimens [2, §27.6];
  - in transplant, non-adherence drives **late rejection and graft loss** [2, §27.13].
- **Depots:** pharmacologically the same as oral drugs, yet they give **lower readmission**, which shows that adherence matters.

### Factors affecting adherence (Table 14.1) [1]
| Domain | Factors |
|---|---|
| Illness-related | Lack of motivation, **poor insight**, grandiose delusions, cognitive deficit, thought disorder, forgetfulness, disorganisation |
| Treatment-related | **Adverse effects**, inappropriate preparation or packaging, **complex dosing schedules** |
| Clinician-related | **Poor therapeutic alliance**, lack of follow-up, limited time, poor information |
| Patient-related | Denial, poor insight, comorbidity, physical impairment, poor (health) literacy, poor understanding of options |
| Environmental | Disorganised environment, **family, religious and health beliefs** |

- Patients' **explanatory models** may differ: only about a third of white English patients saw schizophrenia as substantially biological.
- **Only about 10%** of non-adherent patients say they simply forgot.

### Assessing adherence (Table 14.2) [1]
| Method | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|
| Plasma levels | Direct, accurate | Only a zero level is definitive; invasive; short timeframe |
| Pill count | Simple | Underestimates non-adherence |
| Pharmacy records (medication possession ratio) | Cheap, accessible | Shows collection, not ingestion |
| Self-report scales (MARS) | Easy | **Massively overestimates** adherence |
| Electronic monitoring (MEMS) | Among the most accurate; shows patterns | Costly; shows container opening only; feels like surveillance |

- **NICE:** ask **non-judgementally** about missed doses over a specific period, such as the last week.
- **Attitude scales:**
  - **Drug Attitude Inventory** (30 or 10 items), which predicts adherence over time;
  - ROMI; the Beliefs about Medicines Questionnaire; MARS.
- **Capacity and autonomy:** respect a capable patient's right to refuse, and **record** the reasons and concerns.

### Interventions (Table 14.3) [1]
**Intentional non-adherence:**
- **Psychoeducation:** verbal and written. It is the foundation, but not very effective alone.
- **Motivational interviewing** for goal setting.
- **Adherence ("compliance") therapy:**
  - active listening, CBT techniques, MI and information;
  - the Maudsley pilot improved adherence and reduced admissions over 6 months;
  - a replication without training or supervision failed.
- **CBT** for residual symptoms and dysfunctional beliefs.
- **Cognitive remediation**; mindfulness.
- **Regular adverse-effect monitoring.**
- A **non-judgemental therapeutic alliance**.
- **Family intervention.**

**Unintentional non-adherence:**
- **Simplify the regimen** (fewer drugs, less frequent dosing).
- **Medication-taking aids** (dosette boxes):
  - for motivated but disorganised or cognitively impaired patients;
  - not a substitute for insight;
  - error-prone; some formulations are unstable outside the blister pack.
- **Pairing doses with daily routines**; SMS, email and phone **reminders**; pharmacy help for physical impairment. Evidence for electronic monitoring is weak.

**Long-acting injectables:**
- Relapse risk is **30% lower in relative terms, 10% in absolute terms**, than with oral drugs.
- **NICE:** an option for known non-adherence or by patient preference.
- **But** they do not fix the underlying reasons: more than 50% discontinue SGA depots.
- They prevent **sudden cessation** and make adherence **certain and known**.
- **Underused:** fewer than 1 in 5 recently non-adherent US patients were prescribed one.

**Other approaches:**
- long-acting oral **penfluridol** (weekly);
- **Abilify MyCite** (sensor tablet; slim evidence);
- **financial incentives**: improve adherence during the payments, but the effect does not persist (complete adherence in only 28%). Controversial.

**Overall evidence [1]:**
- **Patient-specific** interventions work better.
- NICE: **no single intervention suits all**.
- Cochrane: only 11 of 182 studies had the lowest risk of bias.
- Use an **intervention-mapping** approach, linking each barrier to a matched intervention.

**Conclusion.** Adherence is a **biopsychosocial** behaviour, mostly partial and often hidden. Good practice:
- identify barriers;
- measure objectively where possible;
- tailor interventions: psychoeducation plus MI or adherence therapy, regimen simplification, LAIs where appropriate.

Maudsley's suggestion: after every first prescription, ask and record, "**What have I done to help this patient take this medication?**"

## In depth

### Adherence in medical comorbidity [2]
- **Diabetes:** depressed patients had poorer diet, less exercise, more smoking and **more lapses in refilling** oral hypoglycaemic, lipid-lowering and antihypertensive drugs.
- **Transplant:**
  - **28%** of kidney recipients report immunosuppressant non-adherence; about 23 per 100 recipients per year across organs;
  - best predictors: **past non-adherence, substance use, poor support**;
  - no single intervention is superior; combine pharmacist education, CBT and booster sessions.
