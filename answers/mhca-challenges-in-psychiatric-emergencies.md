## Exam answer

**Definition.** A **psychiatric emergency** is an acute disturbance of thought, mood, behaviour or social relationships that needs immediate intervention to prevent harm to the patient or others. Examples:
- violent agitation;
- suicide attempt or high suicide risk;
- severe self-neglect or catatonia;
- acute psychosis or mania with risk;
- delirium;
- substance intoxication or withdrawal;
- neuroleptic malignant syndrome.

**MHCA 2017** regulates emergency care through **Section 94 (emergency treatment)**, together with the rules on restraint (Section 97), prohibited treatments (Section 95), police duties (Section 100), supported admission (Sections 89–90), ambulance services (Section 21) and attempted suicide (Section 115) [1, 2].

### What the Act provides
- **Section 94, emergency treatment:**
  - **Any registered medical practitioner** may give **any medical treatment, including for mental illness**, in a health establishment **or in the community**.
  - Conditions: it is **immediately necessary** to prevent death or irreversible harm to health, serious harm to self or others, or serious damage to property; **and** the behaviour is believed to flow directly from the mental illness.
  - **Informed consent of the nominated representative (NR)** where available.
  - Duration: up to **72 hours** or until the person is assessed at a mental health establishment (MHE), whichever is earlier. It may extend to **7 days** in disaster or emergency situations as notified.
  - **ECT is excluded** as emergency treatment.
  - Emergency treatment is **not an admission** [1].
- **Section 97, restraint:**
  - Physical restraint only when it is the **only means** to prevent imminent harm.
  - Authorised by the psychiatrist in charge; for the minimum time; monitored and recorded.
  - **NR informed within 24 h.**
  - **Seclusion and solitary confinement are prohibited**; chaining is banned [1].
- **Section 100, police:** take a person believed to be at risk to a public health establishment **within 24 h**; no judicial order needed [1].
- **Section 21(4):** ambulance services for persons with mental illness on the same basis as for physical illness [2].
- **Section 115:** a person who attempts suicide is presumed to have **severe stress**, is not to be tried or punished, and the government must provide care and rehabilitation [1, 2].

### Challenges

| Area | Challenge |
| --- | --- |
| **Capacity and consent** | Agitated or psychotic patients cannot be assessed calmly. Section 4's "or" wording means many acutely ill patients may legally "have capacity" and refuse [2]. Consent from an NR may be unavailable at 3 am |
| **72-hour limit** | Too short to stabilise acute psychosis or mania. Converting to supported admission (Section 89) needs **two assessments and forms**, which are hard in district hospitals without psychiatrists [1, 2] |
| **Who can treat** | Any registered medical practitioner can give emergency treatment, but **emergency physicians and general practitioners fear litigation** and are often unaware of Section 94, so they refuse or refer, which delays care [2] |
| **Medication** | Rapid tranquillisation (e.g., haloperidol + promethazine, lorazepam, olanzapine) must be justified and documented. There is no clear national protocol, which raises legal anxiety |
| **Restraint** | Shortage of staff to monitor; documentation burden; seclusion ban removes one de-escalation option. Families may resort to illegal chaining at home [1] |
| **ECT** | **No ECT in emergencies** and **no ECT for minors** without guardian consent and MHRB permission, even in life-threatening catatonia, NMS or suicidality [2] |
| **Mental Health Review Boards** | Not constituted or not functioning in many districts. Appeals and permissions are delayed [2] |
| **Registration of MHEs** | General hospitals may avoid psychiatric emergencies, fearing registration or penalty requirements [2] |
| **Police and ambulance** | Untrained police; no psychiatric transport; ambulances refusing agitated patients; homeless patients without NRs [1, 2] |
| **Family role** | The NR framework may sideline families who usually bring the patient. Conflicts arise over who consents [2] |
| **Suicide attempts** | Section 115 shifts the response from legal to care. Medico-legal (MLC) procedures, police intimation practices and insurance still create confusion |
| **Substance-related emergencies** | Substance use is within "mental illness", but de-addiction emergencies (withdrawal seizures, delirium) often present to non-psychiatric facilities |
| **Penalties** | Harsh penalties (Sections 107–108) without a test of intent lead to **defensive practice** [2] |
| **Minors** | Minor admission needs NR consent, a separate ward and MHRB intimation; child emergency facilities are scarce |
| **Documentation** | Multiple forms take time from clinical care; rural centres lack staff |

### Practical approach for the emergency psychiatrist
1. **Safety first:** de-escalation, safe environment, medical screen (delirium, hypoglycaemia, intoxication, head injury).
2. **Invoke Section 94** where criteria are met. Document the risk, the link to mental illness, the NR's consent if available, the drugs given, and the time.
3. **Restraint per Section 97:** psychiatrist authorisation, monitoring chart, NR informed within 24 h, release as early as possible.
4. **Within 72 h:** assess capacity → independent admission (Section 86) if the patient agrees with capacity, **or** supported admission (Section 89) with NR application and two assessments, **or** discharge with follow-up.
5. **Suicide attempt:** treat medically, assess risk, offer care (Section 115), avoid punitive framing, involve family, arrange follow-up.
6. **No ECT** within Section 94. If ECT is urgently needed (catatonia, NMS), complete supported admission and consent procedures; for minors, seek MHRB permission urgently.

### Suggested reforms (Math et al. 2019) [2]
- Amend Section 4 ("and" in place of "or").
- **Hospital-level review boards** and an **MHRB decision limit under 72 h**.
- A family-accompanied admission category.
- Allow ECT in emergencies and in minors under expert safeguards.
- Exempt general hospital psychiatry units from MHE registration hurdles.
- Training for police, emergency physicians and primary care doctors.

**Conclusion.** MHCA 2017 gives a clear legal basis for emergency treatment (Section 94) and humane limits on restraint and coercion. In practice, though, short time limits, capacity-assessment ambiguity, the ban on emergency ECT, non-functioning MHRBs, fear of litigation and poor police and ambulance readiness make emergency psychiatric care harder, especially outside tertiary centres. Clinicians should know and document Section 94 and 97 procedures carefully. The system needs targeted amendments, training and investment to fulfil the Act's rights-based aims without delaying life-saving care.

## In depth

### Background
- **MHA 1987:** emergency care relied on "admission under special circumstances" (72 h) and magistrate reception orders. Police could produce persons before magistrates.
- **MHCA 2017** shifted to a clinical decision with rights safeguards and placed emergency treatment explicitly in the community as well [1].

### Epidemiology
- **Who presents:** psychiatric emergencies make up about 5–10% of general emergency department visits in many settings. Agitation, suicide attempts and substance-related problems dominate.
- **Indian suicide burden:** NCRB records about 1.7 lakh suicides a year (2022: about 1.71 lakh; rate about 12.4 per lakh).
- **Where they present:** most people who attempt suicide reach general hospitals first.
- **Specialist capacity:** NMHS 2015–16 found 0.2–1.2 psychiatrists per lakh population in surveyed states. Most emergency first contacts are with non-psychiatrists [3].

### Classification and criteria
| Emergency | Typical MHCA provision |
| --- | --- |
| Violent agitation | Section 94; Section 97 restraint |
| Suicide attempt | Section 115; Section 94 |
| Catatonia / NMS | Section 94 (medical treatment); ECT only after admission and consent |
| Homeless wandering person | Section 100 |
| Minor in crisis | Section 87, NR; MHRB |
| Intoxication / withdrawal | Section 94 (SUD within "mental illness") |

### Mechanism in detail: the legal logic
The rights-based design starts from a **presumption of capacity** and **least restriction**. Emergency powers are a narrow, time-limited exception to consent. Coercion beyond the exception must pass through supported admission and the review architecture (NR, MHRB). The friction comes from the gap between this architecture and the resources that exist [2].

### Clinical features in detail: common dilemmas
1. **Psychotic patient brought by family at night, refusing medication.** Section 94 rapid tranquillisation is permissible if the risk criteria are met. Document, then arrange a supported admission the next day.
2. **Catatonic patient not eating for 5 days.** This is medically urgent: IV fluids and lorazepam under Section 94. ECT needs supported admission and NR consent, and cannot be done as "emergency treatment".
3. **Adolescent with suicidal catatonia.** ECT is prohibited without guardian consent and MHRB permission, so delays are likely [2].
4. **Patient with capacity refusing admission despite risk.** If capacity is preserved, the law respects the refusal. This is ethically troubling for high suicide risk with "capacity".

### Assessment in detail
- **Capacity:** document understanding, appreciation and communication. Use the MacCAT-T where possible.
- **Risk:** structured risk assessment (violence: HCR-20 or brief tools; suicide: C-SSRS).
- **Medical exclusion:** glucose, oxygen saturation, vitals, CT if head injury, toxicology.
- **Records:** the Section 94 justification, restraint chart, NR communication log.

### Differential: medical vs psychiatric emergency
Delirium, hypoglycaemia, hypoxia, encephalitis, intoxication or withdrawal, post-ictal state and head injury must be excluded. Section 94 also covers any medical treatment, so delirium management is included.

### Management details: rapid tranquillisation (typical options)
- **Oral first:** lorazepam 1–2 mg, or olanzapine 10 mg, or risperidone 2 mg.
- **IM:** lorazepam 1–2 mg; haloperidol 5 mg + promethazine 25–50 mg (TREC trials); olanzapine 10 mg IM (do not combine with IM benzodiazepine within 1 hour).
- **Monitoring:** vitals every 15 minutes for 1 hour, then hourly; ECG for haloperidol.
- **Avoid:** using restraint as punishment; seclusion (prohibited under MHCA).

### Critical appraisal
- **Rights vs access trade-off:** strict safeguards protect against abuse, but delayed or refused care is also a harm, especially for the poor and the homeless.
- **Evidence gap:** there are few systematic Indian data on whether MHCA has changed emergency outcomes (e.g., time to treatment, restraint rates, refusals).
- **Western model** imported into a family-centred, low-resource system [2].
- **Balance:** the Act prohibits chaining and seclusion, which are clear gains. Implementation should fix delays rather than reduce rights.

### Indian context
- **Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita 2023:** attempted suicide is no longer an offence (no IPC 309 equivalent). This complements Section 115. Attempts to compel or deter public servants are separately criminalised.
- **Police training** under the MHCA Rules; Tele-MANAS (14416) for crisis calls; DMHP emergency services; 108 ambulance services.
- **NCRB and the National Suicide Prevention Strategy (2022)**: target a 10% reduction in suicide mortality by 2030.

### Recent advances
- **Emerging:**
  - Crisis-resolution teams and helplines (Tele-MANAS).
  - MHCA-compliant emergency SOPs in some states and institutions.
  - Digital MHRB filing.
  - Proposals to amend the MHCA (emergency ECT, family admission, CTOs) [2].
