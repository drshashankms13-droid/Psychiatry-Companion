## Exam answer

**Definition.** Cognitive side effects of ECT are adverse changes in cognition during and after a course of ECT. They include:
- **acute post-ictal disorientation and, sometimes, post-ictal delirium;**
- **anterograde amnesia:** reduced new learning, short-term;
- **retrograde amnesia,** especially for **autobiographical memory** (personal episodic and semantic memories), most marked for the months before the course.

Other cognitive domains (attention, executive function, processing speed) are impaired only briefly, and often improve above baseline as depression lifts [1].

### Mechanism
Electrical stimulus → generalised seizure + post-ictal suppression → transient disruption of hippocampal and medial temporal consolidation (new learning) and of retrieval and reconsolidation of recent memories → retrograde amnesia. Contributors: hyperaemia and blood–brain barrier changes, NMDA-mediated effects, and stimulus charge in excess of seizure threshold. Effects scale with **electrode placement** (bitemporal > bifrontal > RUL), **pulse width** (sine wave >> brief pulse > ultra-brief), **dose above threshold**, **frequency** and **number** of treatments [1].

### Nature and time course (Semkovska meta-analyses)

| Domain | 0–3 days post-ECT | 4–14 days | >14 days to 2 years |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Orientation | Post-ictal disorientation; longer than 30 minutes predicts autobiographical loss | — | — |
| Processing speed, executive function | Impaired | Back to baseline or improved | No impairment |
| Attention | Not impaired | — | — |
| Anterograde memory (new learning) | Impaired | Returns to baseline | No impairment (group means) |
| **Retrograde autobiographical memory** | Impaired | Impaired | **May persist ≥1 year; some memories never return**; worst for the ~3 months before ECT, may extend to 1 year or more |

Source: [1].

**Subjective complaints:** about 60% report memory problems; 40% say these lasted weeks to years (mostly bilateral ECT). Complex subjective questionnaires track mood rather than objective deficits, but a simple question ("has ECT helped or hurt your memory?") correlates with objective autobiographical loss [1].

### Risk factors

| Treatment factors | Patient factors |
| --- | --- |
| Bilateral (especially bitemporal) placement | Older age |
| Brief pulse > ultra-brief; **sine wave** (much worse; reaction time affected at 6 months) | Pre-existing cognitive impairment or low baseline 3MS score |
| High dose above seizure threshold | Brain injury, neurological disease, intellectual disability |
| Thrice-weekly vs twice-weekly | Female sex (severe autobiographical loss) |
| More than 12 treatments; frequent maintenance (<3 weeks apart) | **Lithium** co-prescription (delirium) |
| Long post-ictal reorientation time | Anaesthetic and benzodiazepine factors |

Source: [1].

### Assessment and monitoring
- **Minimal (any setting):** record time to reorientation (report if >30 minutes); educate carers about inter-ictal delirium; record salient life events from the past year at baseline and re-test every 3 treatments; ask patient, family and staff about memory; rate "helped vs hurt memory" on a 1–7 scale [1].
- **If resources allow:** 3MS/MoCA/ACE-III at baseline and every 2–3 treatments; CUAMI-SF (30 items, 10–20 minutes); Hopkins or Rey Verbal Learning Test; repeat 3–4 days after the last treatment [1].

### Management / minimisation
Use RUL ultrabrief (0.25–0.3 ms) at 6× seizure threshold where clinically appropriate → bilateral only when speed or severity requires (psychotic, suicidal, catatonic) → twice-weekly schedule → avoid courses over 12 treatments unless necessary → dose titration by threshold → stop or reduce lithium (or hold the dose before ECT) and avoid excess benzodiazepines → if significant cognitive decline: switch bilateral → RUL, reduce frequency, lengthen intervals, or pause → advise on driving and work for about 2 weeks after the course → give written information to patient and family [1].

### Key evidence
- **Semkovska & McLoughlin 2010** meta-analysis (84 studies, 2,981 patients): deficits in 0–3 days resolve by 15 days; some improvement above baseline thereafter.
- **Sackeim 2007** prospective cohort (n=347): bilateral and sine-wave ECT gave persistent retrograde deficits at 6 months; women and older patients were more affected.
- **Sackeim 2008 RCT:** ultrabrief RUL markedly reduced cognitive effects vs brief pulse, with slightly lower efficacy.
- **Sobin 1995:** longer reorientation time predicts retrograde amnesia [1].

**Conclusion.** Modern ECT causes **transient** anterograde and executive impairment that recovers within about 2 weeks, but it can cause **lasting autobiographical memory loss** in some patients, especially with bilateral, high-dose, frequent or sine-wave treatment. These effects can be minimised through technique (ultrabrief RUL, twice weekly, threshold-based dosing), simple systematic monitoring and honest consent. The risk has to be weighed against ECT's often life-saving benefit in severe depression.

## In depth

### Background
Cerletti and Bini introduced ECT in 1938, using sine-wave current without anaesthesia, which caused substantial confusion and amnesia. Anaesthesia and muscle relaxants (1950s), unilateral placement (Lancaster 1958; d'Elia 1970), brief-pulse machines (1980s), and ultrabrief pulse (2000s) progressively reduced cognitive effects. The consumer and anti-ECT movement highlighted memory loss. Studies by Squire (1980s) and Sackeim (2000s) characterised retrograde amnesia. The Porter et al. 2020 ANZ guideline gives practical monitoring and information advice [1].

### Epidemiology
- About 60% subjectively report memory problems, and 40% report problems lasting weeks to years (mostly bilateral ECT) [1].
- Objective persistent autobiographical memory loss beyond normal forgetting is greatest with bitemporal ECT [1].
- A typical course is 8–10 treatments (slightly more with UB-RUL) [1].
- Sine-wave devices are still used in some LMICs, including some Indian centres [1].

### Classification of cognitive effects
1. **Acute:** post-ictal confusion (minutes to an hour); emergence delirium/agitation.
2. **Inter-ictal delirium:** fluctuating disorientation between sessions. More likely in the elderly, with lithium, and with frequent bilateral ECT.
3. **Anterograde amnesia:** reduced encoding of new information during the course and up to about 2 weeks after.
4. **Retrograde amnesia:** autobiographical and impersonal (public events). It is temporally graded (recent > remote) and may be persistent.
5. **Subjective cognitive complaints:** influenced by mood, expectation and media [1].

### Mechanism in detail
- **Hippocampus:** the seizure disrupts long-term potentiation (LTP saturation hypothesis). Paradoxically, ECT increases hippocampal volume and neurogenesis, which may partly explain later improvement.
- **Electrode placement:** bitemporal current traverses the temporal lobes (memory) bilaterally. RUL spares the dominant (left) hemisphere's verbal memory. Bifrontal is intermediate.
- **Pulse width:** shorter pulses depolarise neurons more efficiently with less total charge, so less excess current spreads.
- **Dose relative to threshold:** higher multiples cause more cognitive effects. RUL needs about 6× threshold for efficacy, bitemporal about 1.5×.
- **Post-ictal suppression** and reorientation time index the cognitive burden of each treatment [1].

### Clinical features in detail
Patients may forget events around the course (hospital stay, visitors), recent holidays, weddings, work tasks, and passwords; they may have difficulty following conversations or reading during the course. After about 2 weeks, learning recovers. Some autobiographical gaps persist. Severe depression itself impairs memory and attention, so baseline testing is important for attribution [1].

### Assessment in detail

| Tool | Domain | Practicality |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Time to reorientation | Acute | Very easy; >30 minutes is a red flag [1] |
| 3MS / MoCA / ACE-III | Global | 10–15 minutes; sensitive to placement and frequency [1] |
| HVLT-R / RAVLT | Anterograde verbal learning | 10–20 minutes [1] |
| CUAMI-SF | Retrograde autobiographical | 30 questions, 10–20 minutes [1] |
| Personalised event recall | Retrograde (low-resource alternative) | Easy; family can help [1] |
| "Helped or hurt memory" 1–7 Likert | Subjective | Very easy; correlates with objective autobiographical loss [1] |
| CAM / delirium screen | Inter-ictal delirium | Nurses and carers |

### Differential — cognitive decline during or after ECT

| Cause | Clue |
| --- | --- |
| ECT-related amnesia | Temporal link; autobiographical gaps |
| Persistent depression ("pseudodementia") | Low mood, poor effort, improves with remission |
| Delirium from other causes | Infection, electrolytes, drugs (lithium toxicity, anticholinergics) |
| Underlying neurodegenerative disease | Progressive decline, focal deficits; ECT may unmask it in the elderly |
| Benzodiazepine or anaesthetic effects | Sedation |
| Non-convulsive status | Prolonged confusion; EEG |

### Management details
- **Technique:** RUL ultrabrief at 6× ST for most patients; switch to brief-pulse RUL or bilateral if response is inadequate [1]. Bifrontal is an option.
- **Frequency:** twice a week where speed is not critical; thrice-weekly bilateral gives faster relief but more side effects [1].
- **Number:** review at 6; beyond 12, re-weigh benefit [1].
- **Maintenance ECT:** monthly intervals preferred; monitor every 3–12 months [1].
- **Medications:** hold or reduce lithium (raised delirium risk) [1]; minimise benzodiazepines (they raise threshold, leading to higher doses); avoid anticholinergics.
- **Anaesthesia:** ketamine anaesthesia has been studied for cognitive protection, with mixed results. Ensure adequate oxygenation.
- **Pharmacological protection (investigational):** donepezil, memantine, thyroid hormone (T3) and others have weak or no evidence.
- **Counselling:** what to tell patients [1]:
  1. ECT may cause memory loss, which some find distressing.
  2. Loss is worse for about 3 months before the course and sometimes extends back a year or more.
  3. It usually improves by 6 months, but some memories may not return.
  4. New learning is reduced for up to about 2 weeks after the course.
  5. Concentration and planning may improve as depression lifts.
  6. Depression itself impairs memory.

  Emphasise these points more for bilateral ECT, long courses, frequent maintenance, the elderly and those with cognitive vulnerability. Give written information to family too.
- **After the course:** delay driving and complex work decisions for about 2 weeks; plan graded return to roles [1].

### Critical appraisal
- **Group means hide individuals:** "no mean decline" may conceal a subgroup with significant loss [1].
- **Baseline problem:** depression lowers baseline cognition, so "return to baseline" may still be below the pre-illness level [1].
- **Measurement:** the CUAMI is not fully standardised; items can be culturally inappropriate (e.g., New Year events) [1].
- **Subjective vs objective:** complex questionnaires reflect mood; simple ratings may be more valid [1].
- **Evidence gaps:** optimal monitoring schedules and thresholds for concern are not established [1].
- **Efficacy–cognition trade-off:** ultrabrief RUL is cognitively sparing but may need more sessions [1].

### Indian context
- **MHCA 2017, Section 95:** ECT only with anaesthesia and muscle relaxant (unmodified ECT banned); ECT in minors needs guardian consent and Mental Health Review Board permission. Informed consent should include cognitive effects.
- Some older Indian centres still use **sine-wave machines**, which carry high cognitive risk and should be upgraded or at least monitored closely [1].
- Bitemporal ECT is commonly used in India; consider RUL more often where appropriate.
- Low-resource monitoring (reorientation time, personalised event recall, family report) is feasible [1].
- The IPS CPG on ECT recommends brief-pulse machines and cognitive monitoring.

### Recent advances
- **Established:** ultrabrief RUL ECT.
- **Emerging:** individualised low-amplitude ECT; frontoMedial ECT; focal electrically administered seizure therapy (FEAST); **magnetic seizure therapy (MST)**, with ECT-like efficacy and fewer cognitive effects in early trials; computerised cognitive batteries for monitoring [1].
- **Investigational:** ketamine anaesthesia; neuroprotective adjuncts; EEG markers of post-ictal suppression as predictors of efficacy and cognition.
