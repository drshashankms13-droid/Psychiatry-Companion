## Exam answer

**Introduction.** Wilson's disease (hepatolenticular degeneration; Wilson, 1912) is a rare **autosomal recessive** disorder of copper metabolism affecting the **liver and the brain** (especially the lenticular nuclei) [1].
- **Prevalence:** about **1 in 40,000**.
- **Importance for psychiatrists:**
  - it often **presents with psychiatric symptoms**;
  - it is **treatable**;
  - **delayed diagnosis causes irreversible damage**.

### Aetiopathogenesis [1]
- **Gene:** ***ATP7B*** on the **long arm of chromosome 13**; over 200 mutations.
- **Protein:** a membrane-bound copper-transporting ATPase, mainly hepatic.
- **Function:** it moves copper into the secretory pathway for:
  - incorporation into **apocaeruloplasmin**;
  - **biliary excretion**.
- **Defect:** impaired biliary excretion leads to copper accumulation in the **liver**, then **brain** (basal ganglia most), **cornea**, **kidney**, joints and heart.
- **Toxic pool:** "free" copper (not bound to caeruloplasmin) is raised. Caeruloplasmin normally binds 90% of serum copper.
- **Pathology:**
  - **Brain:** shrunken, brick-red **putamen**, sometimes **cavitated**; neuronal loss in caudate and putamen; astrocytes with enlarged vesicular **"Alzheimer nuclei"**; **Opalski cells**; spongy change in the frontal cortex; dentate degeneration.
  - **Liver:** **multilobular (coarse nodular) cirrhosis**; enlarged spleen.

### Clinical features [1]
- **Onset:**
  - usually childhood or adolescence (about half are symptomatic by 15), but **up to the 5th decade**;
  - liver disease predominates in childhood presentations.
- **Initial presentation (Bearn):**
  - about **40% hepatic**;
  - **40% neurological**;
  - **20% psychiatric or behavioural**;
  - one-quarter have more than one system involved.

| System | Features |
| --- | --- |
| **Hepatic** | Almost invariable (may be subclinical): hepatitis, cirrhosis, jaundice, hepatosplenomegaly, **fulminant hepatic failure**, ascites, variceal bleed |
| **Neurological** (motor only; facio-bulbar emphasis) | Rigidity, tremor (including **"wing-beating"** at the shoulders, **flapping** tremor), **dystonia**, athetosis, chorea; fixed **"rigid silent smile"** with open mouth; **dysarthria and dysphagia**; seizures (10 times the general rate; Jacksonian); hemiplegia; coma |
| | **Children and adolescents:** dystonia and spastic rigidity (**"lenticular"** form; rapid course). **Adults:** tremor and dysarthria (**"pseudosclerosis"** form; slower course) |
| **Ocular** | **Kayser–Fleischer ring**: brown or grey-green copper in Descemet's membrane; use a **slit lamp**. **Absent K–F ring makes Wilson's improbable when neuropsychiatric features are present** (it may be absent in purely hepatic cases; not pathognomonic, also seen in PBC and autoimmune hepatitis). **Sunflower cataract** |
| **Renal** | Azotaemia (20%), tubular dysfunction, **aminoaciduria**, nephrocalcinosis |
| **Others** | Arthritis and premature joint degeneration; bone fragmentation; cardiomyopathy and conduction defects; **haemolytic anaemia** |

Early motor symptoms can be **transient and emotionally sensitive**, which is **mistaken for conversion disorder** [1].

### Psychiatric manifestations (p3-2f8050c6) [1]
Wilson emphasised psychiatric features as fundamental. **About 20% saw a psychiatrist before diagnosis** (Dening & Berrios, n = 195).

Reasons for referral: declining school or work performance, behavioural outbursts, odd movements.

| Domain | Findings (Dening & Berrios; Akil & Brewer; Oder) |
| --- | --- |
| **Personality change** | About **25%**: altered lifestyle, relationships, behaviour; **impulsivity, irresponsibility, aggression** |
| **"Incongruous behaviour"** | About **25%**: **disinhibited, bizarre, reckless**, often leading to **forensic problems** |
| **Irritability / aggression** | 18% / 14% |
| **Depression** | **21–27%**; linked to hepatic dysfunction and family problems more than neurology |
| **Cognitive impairment** | About 25%, usually **mild** (subcortical); some only *look* impaired because of dysarthria and facial rigidity |
| **Psychosis** | **Rare (<2%)**: no convincing link with schizophrenia |
| **Disorientation** | 7%; linked to **liver failure** (encephalopathy) |

- **Organic basis:** personality and behaviour change correlate with **neurological severity, especially dysarthria**, bradykinesia and rigidity.
- **Serotonergic deficit** (reduced SERT) has also been shown.
- **Course with treatment:** cognition and incongruous behaviour **improve**; **depression and irritability often persist**.
- **Iatrogenic:** penicillamine can cause seizures and psychosis. Tetrathiomolybdate appears free of this.
- **Classic pitfall (case):** a 17-year-old with lability and school decline was given chlorpromazine. Tremor worsened, LFTs and EPS were blamed on the drug, she was labelled schizoaffective, and diagnosis was delayed 22 months.

**When should a psychiatrist screen (caeruloplasmin)?** [1]
- **Personality change**, especially disinhibited, bizarre or reckless behaviour;
- **neurological signs not explained by medication** (EPS out of proportion; **dysarthria or other bulbar signs** under middle age);
- unexplained **liver disease**;
- falling **school or work performance** in the young;
- **all new behavioural abnormalities under 40** (Brewer);
- **any dystonia with onset under 50**.

### Investigations [1]
- **Serum caeruloplasmin:**
  - low in >75%;
  - **normal in about 5%**;
  - low in 10–20% of heterozygotes, and in protein deficiency or liver disease.
- **Serum copper:** total low; **free copper high**.
- **24-hour urinary copper:** **>100 µg**.
- **Slit-lamp examination** for the K–F ring.
- **⁶⁴Cu incorporation test** if caeruloplasmin is normal but suspicion high.
- **Liver biopsy:** **raised hepatic copper**; nodular cirrhosis (definitive).
- **Imaging:**
  - **CT:** ventricular and cisternal dilatation, cortical atrophy, **hypodense basal ganglia**;
  - **MRI:** symmetrical T2 hyperintensity of the **putamen and pallidum**, thalamus, caudate, dentate and brainstem; **"face of the giant panda"** in the midbrain; changes can reverse with treatment;
  - **PET:** reduced lenticular metabolism.
- **Family screening:** all **siblings** (25% affected, 50% carriers).

### Treatment (p3-0b62b688) [1]
**Aims:**
- remove excess copper;
- prevent reaccumulation;
- **start immediately and continue for life**.

| Agent | Mechanism and use | Key adverse effects |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Dimercaprol (BAL) | First chelator (1951); historical | Painful IM injections; toxicity |
| **D-penicillamine** | Chelator; long the treatment of choice | Acute hypersensitivity. **About 50% of neurological presenters worsen** (half never recover). Late: skin, renal, **SLE, myasthenia**, optic neuritis, marrow suppression, loss of taste. **Psychiatric: seizures, psychosis** |
| **Trientine** | Chelator; **considered by many the drug of choice** (fewer adverse effects than penicillamine) | **Sideroblastic anaemia** |
| **Tetrathiomolybdate** | Complexes copper with food protein and blocks absorption. Suggested **first line for neurological or psychiatric presentations** (Brewer); well tolerated | Unlicensed (2009) |
| **Zinc** | Induces enterocyte **metallothionein**, blocking copper absorption (including salivary and gastric copper). **Treatment of choice for lifelong maintenance** | Gastric discomfort (10%); essentially non-toxic |
| **Liver transplantation** | Occasional patients (e.g., progressive disease despite penicillamine); **cures the metabolic defect** (restores copper excretion); can reverse severe neurological disease | Surgical |

**Other measures:**
- **low-copper diet no longer needed**;
- treat presymptomatic siblings.

**Psychiatric management:**
- treat depression and behavioural disturbance alongside chelation;
- **be wary of neuroleptics**: in the Cartwright case, chlorpromazine worsened tremor and its side effects masked the diagnosis;
- monitor for **penicillamine-induced psychosis**.

**Outcome:**
- **Most patients recover well**: tremor, rigidity, dystonia, dysarthria and dysphagia improve, often more completely the **earlier treatment starts**.
- **Dysarthria and dysphagia are the most persistent.**
- A few deteriorate despite treatment.
- Untreated: progressive crippling, liver failure, death (children formerly survived less than 4 years).

### Update from Continuum 2022 [2]
- **Genetics:** **autosomal recessive**; ***ATP7B*** on **chromosome 13q14.3** [2].
- **Age pattern:**
  - **isolated hepatic disease at about 8–12 years**;
  - **neurological symptoms are typically absent before 7–10 years**.
- **Neurological picture:**
  - **generalised rigidity**; **faciolinguopharyngeal rigidity**;
  - gross postural or intention tremor;
  - behavioural and cognitive change;
  - chorea and dystonia.
- **MRI:** the **"face of the giant panda"** on T2.
- **Diagnosis:** **low caeruloplasmin and serum copper**, **excess urinary copper**, **K–F rings**, ***ATP7B* mutation**.
- **When to test:**
  - Continuum's dystonia work-up includes **copper, caeruloplasmin and 24-hour urinary copper** to exclude Wilson's;
  - Wilson's is the main **treatable exception** among genetic choreas and should be screened early whenever suspected.
- **Treatment:**
  - **chelation or substrate reduction:** **D-penicillamine, trientine, zinc**;
  - **avoid hepatotoxic agents**;
  - the disease **can remain stable with chelation or be cured by liver transplantation**.

⚠️ **Still missing:** Continuum gives **no doses**, monitoring targets or the Leipzig score.

**Conclusion.** Wilson's disease:
- is an autosomal recessive *ATP7B* copper-transport defect causing hepatic cirrhosis and lenticular degeneration;
- often presents to psychiatrists with personality change, disinhibited or reckless behaviour, depression or school decline. Psychosis is rare.

Diagnosis rests on **low caeruloplasmin, high urinary copper, K–F rings**, MRI basal ganglia changes and liver copper. Treat lifelong with chelation (trientine/penicillamine or tetrathiomolybdate for neurological cases), then **zinc** maintenance, or liver transplantation. **Screen every young patient with unexplained neuropsychiatric change and movement disorder or liver disease.**

## In depth

### Neuropsychiatric differentials in which Wilson's must be excluded [1]
- Young-onset parkinsonism.
- **Any dystonia with onset under 50.**
- Dystonia musculorum deformans.
- Oromandibular dystonia.
- Choreoathetosis in the young.
- Apparent "conversion" movement disorder.
- Antipsychotic-induced EPS that is atypical or out of proportion.
