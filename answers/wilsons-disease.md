## Exam answer

**Introduction.** Wilson's disease (hepatolenticular degeneration; Wilson, 1912) is a rare **autosomal recessive** disorder of copper metabolism affecting the **liver and the brain** (especially the lenticular nuclei) [1].
- **Prevalence:** about **1 in 40,000**.
- **Importance for psychiatrists:**
  - it often **presents with psychiatric symptoms**;
  - it is **treatable**;
  - **delayed diagnosis causes irreversible damage**.

### Aetiopathogenesis [1]
- **Gene:** ***ATP7B*** on the **long arm of chromosome 13**; over 200 mutations.
- **Protein:** a membrane-bound copper-transporting ATPase, mainly hepatic.
- **Function:** it moves copper into the secretory pathway for:
  - incorporation into **apocaeruloplasmin**;
  - **biliary excretion**.
- **Defect:** impaired biliary excretion leads to copper accumulation in the **liver**, then **brain** (basal ganglia most), **cornea**, **kidney**, joints and heart.
- **Toxic pool:** "free" copper (not bound to caeruloplasmin) is raised. Caeruloplasmin normally binds 90% of serum copper.
- **Pathology:**
  - **Brain:** shrunken, brick-red **putamen**, sometimes **cavitated**; neuronal loss in caudate and putamen; astrocytes with enlarged vesicular **"Alzheimer nuclei"**; **Opalski cells**; spongy change in the frontal cortex; dentate degeneration.
  - **Liver:** **multilobular (coarse nodular) cirrhosis**; enlarged spleen.

### Clinical features [1]
- **Onset:**
  - usually childhood or adolescence (about half are symptomatic by 15), but **up to the 5th decade**;
  - liver disease predominates in childhood presentations.
- **Initial presentation (Bearn):**
  - about **40% hepatic**;
  - **40% neurological**;
  - **20% psychiatric or behavioural**;
  - one-quarter have more than one system involved.

| System | Features |
| --- | --- |
| **Hepatic** | Almost invariable (may be subclinical): hepatitis, cirrhosis, jaundice, hepatosplenomegaly, **fulminant hepatic failure**, ascites, variceal bleed |
| **Neurological** (motor only; facio-bulbar emphasis) | Rigidity, tremor (including **"wing-beating"** at the shoulders, **flapping** tremor), **dystonia**, athetosis, chorea; fixed **"rigid silent smile"** with open mouth; **dysarthria and dysphagia**; seizures (10 times the general rate; Jacksonian); hemiplegia; coma |
| | **Children and adolescents:** dystonia and spastic rigidity (**"lenticular"** form; rapid course). **Adults:** tremor and dysarthria (**"pseudosclerosis"** form; slower course) |
| **Ocular** | **Kayser–Fleischer ring**: brown or grey-green copper in Descemet's membrane; use a **slit lamp**. **Absent K–F ring makes Wilson's improbable when neuropsychiatric features are present** (it may be absent in purely hepatic cases; not pathognomonic, also seen in PBC and autoimmune hepatitis). **Sunflower cataract** |
| **Renal** | Azotaemia (20%), tubular dysfunction, **aminoaciduria**, nephrocalcinosis |
| **Others** | Arthritis and premature joint degeneration; bone fragmentation; cardiomyopathy and conduction defects; **haemolytic anaemia** |

Early motor symptoms can be **transient and emotionally sensitive**, which is **mistaken for conversion disorder** [1].

### Psychiatric manifestations [1]
Wilson emphasised psychiatric features as fundamental. **About 20% saw a psychiatrist before diagnosis** (Dening & Berrios, n = 195).

Reasons for referral: declining school or work performance, behavioural outbursts, odd movements.

| Domain | Findings (Dening & Berrios; Akil & Brewer; Oder) |
| --- | --- |
| **Personality change** | About **25%**: altered lifestyle, relationships, behaviour; **impulsivity, irresponsibility, aggression** |
| **"Incongruous behaviour"** | About **25%**: **disinhibited, bizarre, reckless**, often leading to **forensic problems** |
| **Irritability / aggression** | 18% / 14% |
| **Depression** | **21–27%**; linked to hepatic dysfunction and family problems more than neurology |
| **Cognitive impairment** | About 25%, usually **mild** (subcortical); some only *look* impaired because of dysarthria and facial rigidity |
| **Psychosis** | **Rare (<2%)**: no convincing link with schizophrenia |
| **Disorientation** | 7%; linked to **liver failure** (encephalopathy) |

- **Organic basis:** personality and behaviour change correlate with **neurological severity, especially dysarthria**, bradykinesia and rigidity.
- **Serotonergic deficit** (reduced SERT) has also been shown.
- **Course with treatment:** cognition and incongruous behaviour **improve**; **depression and irritability often persist**.
- **Iatrogenic:** penicillamine can cause seizures and psychosis. Tetrathiomolybdate appears free of this.
- **Classic pitfall (case):** a 17-year-old with lability and school decline was given chlorpromazine. Tremor worsened, LFTs and EPS were blamed on the drug, she was labelled schizoaffective, and diagnosis was delayed 22 months.

**When should a psychiatrist screen (caeruloplasmin)?** [1]
- **Personality change**, especially disinhibited, bizarre or reckless behaviour;
- **neurological signs not explained by medication** (EPS out of proportion; **dysarthria or other bulbar signs** under middle age);
- unexplained **liver disease**;
- falling **school or work performance** in the young;
- **all new behavioural abnormalities under 40** (Brewer);
- **any dystonia with onset under 50**.

### Investigations [1]
- **Serum caeruloplasmin:**
  - low in >75%;
  - **normal in about 5%**;
  - low in 10–20% of heterozygotes, and in protein deficiency or liver disease.
- **Serum copper:** total low; **free copper high**.
- **24-hour urinary copper:** **>100 µg**.
- **Slit-lamp examination** for the K–F ring.
- **⁶⁴Cu incorporation test** if caeruloplasmin is normal but suspicion high.
- **Liver biopsy:** **raised hepatic copper**; nodular cirrhosis (definitive).
- **Imaging:**
  - **CT:** ventricular and cisternal dilatation, cortical atrophy, **hypodense basal ganglia**;
  - **MRI:** symmetrical T2 hyperintensity of the **putamen and pallidum**, thalamus, caudate, dentate and brainstem; **"face of the giant panda"** in the midbrain; changes can reverse with treatment;
  - **PET:** reduced lenticular metabolism.
- **Family screening:** all **siblings** (25% affected, 50% carriers).

### Treatment [1]
**Aims:**
- remove excess copper;
- prevent reaccumulation;
- **start immediately and continue for life**.

| Agent | Mechanism and use | Key adverse effects |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Dimercaprol (BAL) | First chelator (1951); historical | Painful IM injections; toxicity |
| **D-penicillamine** | Chelator; long the treatment of choice | Acute hypersensitivity. **About 50% of neurological presenters worsen** (half never recover). Late: skin, renal, **SLE, myasthenia**, optic neuritis, marrow suppression, loss of taste. **Psychiatric: seizures, psychosis** |
| **Trientine** | Chelator; **considered by many the drug of choice** (fewer adverse effects than penicillamine) | **Sideroblastic anaemia** |
| **Tetrathiomolybdate** | Complexes copper with food protein and blocks absorption. Suggested **first line for neurological or psychiatric presentations** (Brewer); well tolerated | Unlicensed (2009) |
| **Zinc** | Induces enterocyte **metallothionein**, blocking copper absorption (including salivary and gastric copper). **Treatment of choice for lifelong maintenance** | Gastric discomfort (10%); essentially non-toxic |
| **Liver transplantation** | Occasional patients (e.g., progressive disease despite penicillamine); **cures the metabolic defect** (restores copper excretion); can reverse severe neurological disease | Surgical |

**Other measures:**
- **low-copper diet no longer needed**;
- treat presymptomatic siblings.

**Psychiatric management:**
- treat depression and behavioural disturbance alongside chelation;
- **be wary of neuroleptics**: in the Cartwright case, chlorpromazine worsened tremor and its side effects masked the diagnosis;
- monitor for **penicillamine-induced psychosis**.

**Outcome:**
- **Most patients recover well**: tremor, rigidity, dystonia, dysarthria and dysphagia improve, often more completely the **earlier treatment starts**.
- **Dysarthria and dysphagia are the most persistent.**
- A few deteriorate despite treatment.
- Untreated: progressive crippling, liver failure, death (children formerly survived less than 4 years).

**Conclusion.** Wilson's disease:
- is an autosomal recessive *ATP7B* copper-transport defect causing hepatic cirrhosis and lenticular degeneration;
- often presents to psychiatrists with personality change, disinhibited or reckless behaviour, depression or school decline. Psychosis is rare.

Diagnosis rests on **low caeruloplasmin, high urinary copper, K–F rings**, MRI basal ganglia changes and liver copper. Treat lifelong with chelation (trientine/penicillamine or tetrathiomolybdate for neurological cases), then **zinc** maintenance, or liver transplantation. **Screen every young patient with unexplained neuropsychiatric change and movement disorder or liver disease.**

## In depth

### Background
- **Samuel Alexander Kinnier Wilson (1912)** described "progressive lenticular degeneration": familial, with cirrhosis and lenticular necrosis. He **stressed psychiatric symptoms** (psychotic and "hysterical", later affective change and disordered behaviour). He noted that mental impairment was often "**more apparent than real**", suggested by the appearance and dysarthria [1].
- **Westphal and Strümpell's "pseudosclerosis"** (tremor-dysarthria form) was later recognised as the same disease [1].
- **Kayser (1902) and Fleischer (1903)** described the corneal ring.
- **Treatment history:**
  - dimercaprol (Denny-Brown and Porter, 1951);
  - **penicillamine** (Walshe, 1956);
  - trientine (Walshe, 1969);
  - zinc (Schouwink 1961; Brewer, 1980s);
  - tetrathiomolybdate (Brewer, 1990s) [1; general knowledge for dates].
- **The Cambridge (Walshe) cohort**, studied by **Dening and Berrios**, is the largest UK series with operationally defined psychiatric assessment [1].

### Epidemiology
- About **1 in 40,000** [1]. A carrier frequency of about 1 in 90 is commonly quoted. Genetic data suggest prevalence may be **1 in 7,000** or higher (gene-frequency studies) [general knowledge].
- **Onset:** usually childhood or adolescence, up to the **5th decade** (rarely the 70s). About **half are symptomatic by 15** [1].
- **First presentations [1]:**
  - about **40% hepatic**, **40% neurological**, **20% psychiatric or behavioural** (Bearn 1957);
  - one-quarter have more than one system involved at presentation;
  - **childhood onset favours a hepatic presentation**.
- **Psychiatric contact before diagnosis:** **20%** had seen a psychiatrist before diagnosis (Dening and Berrios) [1].
- **Diagnostic delay:** in the Cartwright case, 22 months; mean delays of 1–3 years in neuropsychiatric presentations [1; general knowledge for mean delays].

### Classification
**By presentation [1; general knowledge for the Leipzig/EASL categories]:**
| Form | Features |
| --- | --- |
| **Hepatic** | Asymptomatic transaminitis; acute hepatitis; **fulminant hepatic failure with Coombs-negative haemolysis**; chronic hepatitis; cirrhosis |
| **Neurological** | **Dystonic / "lenticular"** (younger: dystonia, rigidity, rapid course); **"pseudosclerotic"** (adults: tremor, wing-beating, dysarthria, slower course); **parkinsonian**; ataxic; choreic [1] |
| **Psychiatric** | Personality change, incongruous behaviour, mood disorder, cognitive decline; psychosis rare [1] |
| **Presymptomatic** | Identified by sibling screening; must be treated [1] |
| **Other** | Renal (aminoaciduria, nephrocalcinosis), skeletal (arthropathy, osteopenia), cardiac (cardiomyopathy, arrhythmia), haemolytic anaemia, amenorrhoea and miscarriage [1; general knowledge for gynaecological] |

**Leipzig scoring system (2001)** [general knowledge]:
- items: **KF rings** (2), **neuropsychiatric symptoms or typical MRI** (2), **Coombs-negative haemolysis** (1), **caeruloplasmin** (<0.1 g/L: 2; 0.1–0.2: 1), **urinary copper** (>2× ULN: 2), **liver copper** (>250 µg/g: 2), **ATP7B mutations** (2 mutations: 4; 1 mutation: 1);
- **score ≥4: diagnosis established**; 3: probable; ≤2: unlikely.

### Mechanism in detail
**Molecular [1]:**
- ***ATP7B*** on **13q14.3** (over 200 mutations then; now more than 900) [1; general knowledge for the current number].
- It is a **copper-transporting P-type ATPase** in the **hepatocyte trans-Golgi**. It loads copper onto **apocaeruloplasmin** and excretes copper into **bile**.
- **Failure** leads to hepatic copper accumulation, then **spill-over of non-caeruloplasmin-bound ("free") copper** into blood and tissues: brain, cornea, kidney, joints, heart [1].
- Low caeruloplasmin reflects unstable copper-free apocaeruloplasmin. **Caeruloplasmin itself is not causal** [1; general knowledge].
- **Common mutations:** **H1069Q** in Europe. In **India**, mutations are heterogeneous (for example C271X, G1061E and others; regional founder variants) [general knowledge].

**Brain injury [1; general knowledge for mechanisms]:**
- Free copper produces **oxidative stress** (Fenton chemistry) and mitochondrial dysfunction. Astrocytes buffer copper and become **Alzheimer type II astrocytes**, which are also seen in hepatic encephalopathy [1].
- **Predilection for the lenticular nuclei** is unexplained. The putamen often **cavitates**; **Opalski cells** appear; pericapillary copper concretions; frontal cortical degeneration and status spongiosus [1].
- **Psychiatric mechanisms:**
  - **frontal–subcortical circuit disruption** (putamen, caudate, thalamus) produces disinhibition and impulsivity. This explains why behaviour correlates with **dysarthria, bradykinesia and rigidity** [1].
  - **Reduced central serotonergic activity and SERT binding** (Hesse 2003). This questions whether psychiatric features come from copper directly [1].
  - Depression also relates to **hepatic dysfunction** and **psychosocial stress** (long admissions, family problems) [1].

### Clinical features in detail
**Neurological signs [1]:**
- **Facial and bulbar emphasis:** fixed, open-mouthed "**vacuous smile**" (risus sardonicus); drooling; **spastic dysarthria**; dysphagia.
- **"Wing-beating" tremor** of the abducted arms; postural or flapping tremor.
- Dystonia (limb, oromandibular); athetosis; chorea; rigidity.
- Jacksonian seizures (10 times the population rate); hemiplegia; episodes of coma.
- **Early fluctuation and emotional sensitivity** of signs, which may be mistaken for **conversion disorder** [1].

**Psychiatric phenomenology (Dening and Berrios) [1]:**
| Feature | Frequency | Correlate |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Any psychiatric disturbance at index admission | About 50% | |
| Personality change | About 25% | Neurological severity |
| **Incongruous behaviour** (disinhibited, bizarre, reckless; forensic problems) | About 25% | **Dysarthria** and neurological severity |
| Irritability | 18% | Neurological |
| Aggression | 14% | Neurological |
| Depression | 21% (27% in Oder 1991) | Hepatic dysfunction, family problems, long admission (not neurological) |
| Cognitive impairment (mild) | About 25% | Less tied to neurology; some "appear" impaired but test well |
| Delusions or hallucinations | **<2%** | Psychosis is **not** significantly associated |
| Disorientation | 7% | **Liver failure** |
| Prospective sample (n = 31) | More than half abnormal on the Personality Assessment Schedule (impulsive, irresponsible, aggressive); one-third GHQ "cases"; **no psychosis** | |

**Other reported features** [general knowledge]:
- bipolar-like mood instability;
- catatonia (reported);
- **OCD**;
- eating disorders;
- school and work decline as the earliest sign in adolescents.

**Iatrogenic psychiatric syndromes [1]:**
- **penicillamine-induced psychosis and seizures**, even in previously asymptomatic patients;
- **paradoxical neurological (and psychiatric) worsening** after starting chelators. About 50% of neurological presenters worsened on penicillamine in older series; about 10–20% on modern regimens [1; general knowledge for modern rates].

### Assessment: who to screen and how
**Screen with serum caeruloplasmin** [1]:
- **personality change** (especially disinhibited, bizarre or reckless behaviour);
- **neurological signs not explained by medication**;
- **unexplained liver disease** in a psychiatric patient;
- **any new behavioural abnormality under 40** (Brewer);
- dysarthria or bulbar signs below middle age;
- school or work decline in the young;
- **dystonia with onset under 50**; young-onset parkinsonism [1].

**Diagnostic steps** [1; general knowledge for thresholds]:
1. Caeruloplasmin (<0.2 g/L suggestive; <0.1 strongly so). Low in >75% [1].
2. **24-hour urinary copper** >100 µg (>1.6 µmol) [1]. A threshold of >40 µg is used in children. The **penicillamine challenge test** (500 mg twice daily, >1,600 µg/24 hours) is now rarely used.
3. **Slit lamp for KF rings:**
   - almost always present (95%) in **neurological** cases; about 50% in hepatic cases [1; general knowledge for percentages];
   - **absence makes neuropsychiatric Wilson's improbable** [1];
   - not pathognomonic (primary biliary cholangitis, autoimmune hepatitis) [1];
   - **sunflower cataract** [1].
4. **MRI:**
   - symmetrical T2 hyperintensity in the putamen (often a **hypointense rim**), globus pallidus, thalamus, caudate, dentate and brainstem;
   - **"face of the giant panda"** (midbrain);
   - "face of the miniature panda" (pons) [general knowledge];
   - white-matter lesions;
   - changes may reverse with treatment [1].
5. **Hepatic copper** >250 µg/g dry weight on biopsy [1; general knowledge for the threshold].
6. **ATP7B genetic testing**: increasingly first line, and for siblings [general knowledge].
7. **Exchangeable copper / relative exchangeable copper (REC)**: emerging marker [general knowledge].

**Psychiatric work-up:** mental state examination; cognitive testing (MoCA; frontal tests); mood and behaviour scales; **capacity assessment** (treatment adherence is lifelong).

### Differential diagnosis
- **Young-onset psychosis or mania with neurological soft signs or EPS out of proportion to antipsychotic dose** [1].
- **Drug-induced EPS** (as in the Cartwright case, where it masked the diagnosis) [1].
- **Huntington's disease** (juvenile, Westphal variant) [1].
- **Neurodegeneration with brain iron accumulation (PKAN):** "eye-of-the-tiger" MRI sign [general knowledge].
- **Acquired hepatocerebral degeneration** (pallidal T1 hyperintensity from manganese) [general knowledge].
- **Hepatic encephalopathy.**
- **Conversion disorder** (fluctuating early signs) [1].
- **Adolescent adjustment or conduct disorder; drug misuse** [1].
- **Menkes disease** and **aceruloplasminaemia** (iron accumulation, diabetes, retinal degeneration) [general knowledge].

### Management details [1; general knowledge for doses and monitoring]
**Chelation and copper-absorption blockade:**
| Drug | Adult dose (typical) | Monitoring and cautions |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **D-penicillamine** | Start 250–500 mg/day; increase to 750–1,500 mg/day in 2–4 divided doses before meals; **pyridoxine 25 mg/day** | FBC and urine protein; hypersensitivity; nephrotic syndrome; lupus; myasthenia; elastosis perforans serpiginosa; **neurological worsening**; psychosis [1] |
| **Trientine** | 900–2,700 mg/day (maintenance 900–1,500) in divided doses | Sideroblastic anaemia [1]; less neurological worsening; requires refrigeration (older salt) |
| **Zinc** (acetate or sulfate) | **50 mg elemental zinc three times daily** (adults), away from food | Gastric irritation [1]; pancreatic enzyme rise; slow onset (months), so it is **maintenance or presymptomatic therapy**, and first line in some neurological cases |
| **Tetrathiomolybdate** | Investigational | Bone-marrow suppression, transaminitis [general knowledge] |
| **Liver transplant** | Fulminant failure or decompensated cirrhosis; consider for severe refractory neurological disease | Cures the metabolic defect [1] |

**Monitoring targets** [general knowledge]:
- **24-hour urinary copper:** 200–500 µg/day on chelators; **<75 µg/day on zinc**.
- **Non-caeruloplasmin-bound copper** 50–150 µg/L.
- LFTs; FBC; urine protein.
- Repeat **MRI and KF ring** assessment over years.

**Paradoxical worsening (neurological and psychiatric)** [general knowledge]:
- start low and go slow;
- consider **trientine or zinc** for neurological presentations;
- monitor closely in the first 6 months.

**Psychotropic prescribing [1; general knowledge for choices]:**
- **Avoid high-potency typical antipsychotics.** Patients are very sensitive to EPS, and EPS mask disease progression [1].
- If an antipsychotic is needed, use **low-dose quetiapine or clozapine**.
- **Hepatic impairment:**
  - prefer drugs with less hepatic metabolism, or adjust doses (for example, lower-dose sertraline or escitalopram; avoid duloxetine and valproate in liver disease);
  - **avoid valproate** (hepatotoxic) and carbamazepine (hepatic enzyme effects);
  - lithium is possible with renal monitoring (tubular disease caution).
- **Depression:** SSRIs. Address psychosocial stressors [1].
- **Disinhibition and impulsivity:** treat the underlying disease (behaviour improves with chelation); behavioural management; SSRIs.
- **Mania:** reports of response to low-dose antipsychotics or benzodiazepines; ECT in refractory cases.
- **ECT:** case reports of benefit in catatonia and psychotic depression.

**Response of psychiatric symptoms [1]:**
- **cognition and incongruous behaviour improve** (most gains early);
- **depression and irritability do not** (Dening and Berrios, 129 patients over about 10 years);
- tetrathiomolybdate: psychiatric symptoms respond without adverse effects (early reports).

**Adherence:** lifelong therapy. Non-adherence is a major cause of late deterioration and fulminant failure. Psychiatric illness and personality change undermine adherence, so **psychiatric care improves medical outcome** [general knowledge].

**Pregnancy** [general knowledge]:
- **continue treatment** (stopping risks fulminant failure);
- **reduce chelator dose by 25–50%** in the third trimester (wound healing);
- zinc is safe.

**Family:**
- screen all **siblings** (25% affected, 50% carriers) [1];
- offspring (carrier frequency is high in consanguineous communities);
- genetic counselling.

### Critical appraisal
- Psychiatric data come mainly from **referral-centre series** (Cambridge, Michigan). There is **selection bias** towards severe or neurological cases [1].
- Older studies used **retrospective case-note review** [1].
- **Psychosis may be under-recognised** if patients with psychiatric presentations are never referred to Wilson's centres. Case reports of psychosis persist despite the <2% figure [1; general knowledge].
- **Chelator comparisons** are mostly observational; there are few RCTs [general knowledge].
- **Lishman (2009) predates** current EASL (2012; updated 2025) and AASLD (2022) guidelines, and current tetrathiomolybdate status [general knowledge].

### Indian context [general knowledge]
- Wilson's disease is **relatively common in India**. Consanguinity is common in South India.
- The **NIMHANS series** (Sinha, Taly and colleagues) describe large cohorts with **psychiatric presentations in about 20–30%** and **high rates of KF rings**.
- Typical presentation: an **adolescent with school decline, dysarthria and behavioural change**, often after years of psychiatric treatment.
- **Diagnostic barriers:** limited availability of 24-hour urinary copper and genetic testing outside tertiary centres; **slit-lamp examination is essential and cheap**.
- **Penicillamine and zinc are available and affordable.** Trientine is costly or limited.
- Liver transplantation is available in major centres.
- Psychiatrists in India should screen young patients with atypical psychiatric presentations plus neurological or hepatic signs.

### Recent advances [general knowledge]
- **Trientine tetrahydrochloride** (room-temperature-stable salt; non-inferior to penicillamine in the CHELATE trial, 2022).
- **Bis-choline tetrathiomolybdate (ALXN1840):** the phase 3 programme was discontinued in 2023.
- **Exchangeable copper / REC** for diagnosis and monitoring.
- **AAV gene therapy** (VTX-801, UX701) in early trials.
- **EASL–ERN 2025** clinical practice guidelines.
