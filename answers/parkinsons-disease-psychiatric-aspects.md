## Exam skeleton

**Definition.** Parkinson's disease (PD) is a progressive α-synucleinopathy in which neuropsychiatric syndromes — depression, anxiety, apathy, psychosis, impulse-control disorders, cognitive impairment and sleep disorders — are common, often precede motor signs, and drive disability more than the motor syndrome itself.

### Why it matters
- Non-motor symptoms are the main determinant of quality of life, caregiver burden and nursing-home placement.
- Many are **prodromal**: depression, anxiety, RBD, hyposmia and constipation can precede motor onset by years.

### Mechanism in one line
Braak staging: α-synuclein spreads brainstem (stage 1–2: dorsal vagal nucleus, olfactory bulb, **locus coeruleus, raphe** → mood, anxiety, RBD) → substantia nigra (stage 3: motor) → limbic/mesocortex (4) → neocortex (5–6: dementia). Dopaminergic (mesolimbic/mesocortical), noradrenergic, serotonergic and **cholinergic** deficits plus medication effects explain the syndromes.

### Syndromes, frequency and first-line management

| Syndrome | Frequency | Key features | Management |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Depression | MDD ~17%; clinically significant depressive symptoms ~35% (Reijnders 2008) | Anhedonia, pessimism; less guilt; overlaps with motor symptoms | Optimise dopaminergic therapy; SSRI/SNRI (**SAD-PD**: paroxetine, venlafaxine effective); nortriptyline; pramipexole; CBT; ECT/rTMS for severe |
| Anxiety | ~31% (Broen 2016) | GAD, panic, social anxiety; **OFF-period anxiety** | Treat motor fluctuations; SSRIs; CBT |
| Apathy | ~40% | Loss of motivation without sadness; worse after DA withdrawal or STN-DBS | Optimise dopamine; rivastigmine; activity scheduling |
| Psychosis | Hallucinations in up to 50% over course | Minor phenomena (presence, passage, illusions) → well-formed visual hallucinations → delusions | Exclude delirium → reduce drugs (anticholinergics → amantadine → MAO-B → DA agonists → COMT, levodopa last) → **pimavanserin / clozapine**; rivastigmine if dementia |
| ICDs / DDS / punding | ICD ~14% (DOMINION) | Gambling, hypersexuality, buying, binge eating; linked to **dopamine agonists** | Taper DA agonist (watch **DAWS**); CBT; caregiver involvement |
| Cognitive impairment | PD-MCI ~25%; dementia ~80% at 20 years (Sydney study) | Executive, attentional, visuospatial | Rivastigmine (**EXPRESS**), donepezil; review anticholinergics |
| Sleep | RBD in ~40–50% | Dream enactment; insomnia, EDS | Melatonin, clonazepam (caution), bed safety |

### Assessment
History from patient + caregiver → map to ON/OFF and drug changes → MDS-UPDRS Part I, GDS-15/HADS, Parkinson Anxiety Scale, Starkstein Apathy Scale, QUIP-RS for ICDs, MoCA, SCOPA-PC → screen for delirium, infection, drugs, sleep.

### Pitfalls
- Hypomimia, bradyphrenia and fatigue mimic depression; apathy ≠ depression.
- **Never** use haloperidol, risperidone or olanzapine for PD psychosis.
- STN-DBS can be followed by depression, apathy, impulsivity and **suicide**.

**Conclusion.** Psychiatric syndromes in PD are intrinsic to the disease and its treatment; management depends on linking each symptom to disease stage, motor fluctuations and medication, then using motor-sparing psychiatric treatment.

## Full answer

### 1. Introduction

James Parkinson's 1817 *Essay on the Shaking Palsy* stated that "the senses and intellects" were uninjured — a view now known to be wrong. PD is a multisystem α-synucleinopathy in which non-motor and neuropsychiatric features are present from the prodromal phase onward. They contribute more to quality of life, caregiver strain and institutional care than tremor or rigidity, and they are frequently under-recognised because patients, families and neurologists attribute them to the "normal" course of the illness or to motor disability.

Psychiatric manifestations in PD arise from three overlapping sources:

1. **The disease itself** — degeneration of dopaminergic, noradrenergic, serotonergic and cholinergic systems.
2. **The treatment** — dopaminergic and anticholinergic drugs, and deep-brain stimulation.
3. **The psychological response** to a progressive, disabling, visible illness.

### 2. Epidemiology

- PD affects roughly 1% of people over 60 and over 6 million people worldwide (Global Burden of Disease 2016), with numbers rising with population ageing.
- In India, community studies give a wide range of prevalence estimates; the highest reported was among the Parsi community of Mumbai (Bharucha et al., 1988), and most estimates are lower than Western figures, partly because of methodology and under-ascertainment.
- Approximate frequencies of neuropsychiatric syndromes:

| Syndrome | Approximate frequency | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Major depressive disorder | ~17% | Reijnders et al., 2008 (systematic review) |
| Clinically significant depressive symptoms | ~35% | Reijnders et al., 2008 |
| Anxiety disorders | ~31% | Broen et al., 2016 (meta-analysis) |
| Apathy | ~40% | den Brok et al., 2015 (meta-analysis) |
| Psychosis (hallucinations) | 20–40% cross-sectionally; up to ~60% over long follow-up | Forsaa et al., 2010 |
| Impulse-control disorders | ~14% | DOMINION study, Weintraub et al., 2010 |
| PD-mild cognitive impairment | ~25% | Aarsland et al., 2010 (pooled) |
| PD dementia | ~25–30% cross-sectionally; ~80% after 20 years | Aarsland 2005; Hely et al., 2008 |
| REM sleep behaviour disorder | ~40–50% | Multiple cohorts |

### 3. Classification and concepts

- **ICD-11** codes the psychiatric syndromes as *secondary mental or behavioural syndromes* (e.g. secondary mood syndrome, secondary psychotic syndrome) associated with a disease classified elsewhere, alongside the PD code; **DSM-5-TR** uses "depressive disorder due to another medical condition", "psychotic disorder due to another medical condition", and "major or mild neurocognitive disorder due to Parkinson's disease".
- **PD psychosis** has specific diagnostic criteria (NINDS/NIMH workgroup, Ravina et al., 2007): illusions, false sense of presence, hallucinations or delusions, occurring after PD onset, recurrent or continuous for at least one month, not better explained by delirium, schizophrenia, DLB or other causes.
- **PD dementia vs dementia with Lewy bodies** — the "one-year rule": dementia beginning more than one year after established motor parkinsonism is PDD; dementia before or within one year of parkinsonism is DLB. The distinction is clinical convention; both are Lewy body diseases.
- **MDS criteria** exist for PD-MCI (Litvan et al., 2012) and PDD (Emre et al., 2007).

### 4. Aetiology and mechanism

**Braak staging (2003)** gives a framework for the timing of symptoms:

Stage 1–2: dorsal motor nucleus of vagus, olfactory bulb, **locus coeruleus (noradrenaline), raphe nuclei (serotonin)** → hyposmia, constipation, RBD, depression, anxiety (prodromal phase)
→ Stage 3–4: **substantia nigra**, basal forebrain, amygdala → motor syndrome, early cognitive and emotional changes
→ Stage 5–6: limbic and neocortical spread → dementia, psychosis.

Neurotransmitter contributions:

| System | Lesion | Psychiatric consequence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Dopamine | Nigrostriatal, mesolimbic, mesocortical loss | Apathy, anhedonia, executive dysfunction, OFF-period dysphoria |
| Dopamine (iatrogenic) | Relatively intact ventral striatum overstimulated by D2/D3 agonists | Impulse-control disorders, punding, psychosis |
| Noradrenaline | Locus coeruleus | Anxiety, depression, attention deficits |
| Serotonin | Raphe nuclei | Depression, anxiety |
| Acetylcholine | Nucleus basalis of Meynert, pedunculopontine nucleus | Dementia, visual hallucinations, falls |

**Visual hallucinations** are explained by a combination of impaired visual processing (retinal dopamine loss, occipital/ventral stream dysfunction), impaired attention (cholinergic deficit) and intrusion of REM-type dream imagery into wakefulness — integrated in the Perception and Attention Deficit model (Collerton et al., 2005). Dopaminergic drugs lower the threshold but are not the sole cause.

**Impulse-control disorders** reflect the "overdose hypothesis": doses of dopaminergic drugs needed to replace dorsal striatal dopamine over-stimulate the relatively preserved ventral striatum. Agonists with high D3 affinity (pramipexole, ropinirole) carry the greatest risk; risk factors include younger age at onset, male sex (gambling, hypersexuality), personal or family history of addiction, novelty-seeking traits and depression.

**Psychological factors** — loss of role, visible disability, uncertainty and stigma — add to biological vulnerability.

### 5. Clinical features

#### 5.1 Depression

- Often begins before motor diagnosis and may be the first presentation.
- Features: low mood, anhedonia, pessimism, irritability, suicidal ideation; less pronounced guilt and self-blame than in primary depression.
- Overlap problem: psychomotor slowing, hypomimia, hypophonia, fatigue, sleep and appetite change occur in PD without depression. Diagnosis rests more on mood, anhedonia and negative cognitions.
- **Non-motor fluctuations**: dysphoria, anxiety and tearfulness can appear in OFF periods and resolve when the next levodopa dose works.
- Suicidal ideation is common; completed suicide is not consistently raised overall but is increased after STN-DBS.

#### 5.2 Anxiety

- Generalised anxiety, panic attacks, social anxiety (embarrassment about tremor) and specific phobias (fear of falling).
- **OFF-period panic** with autonomic symptoms is characteristic and may be mistaken for cardiac or respiratory emergencies.

#### 5.3 Apathy

- Reduced motivation, initiative and emotional responsiveness **without sadness or distress**.
- Occurs alone or with depression or cognitive impairment; worsens after dopamine-agonist withdrawal or STN-DBS ("dopamine withdrawal apathy").

#### 5.4 Psychosis

- A **spectrum**:
  - *Minor phenomena* — sense of presence, passage hallucinations (fleeting figures in peripheral vision), visual illusions (pareidolia).
  - *Well-formed visual hallucinations* — people, children, animals, often silent, initially with insight.
  - *Loss of insight and delusions* — commonly persecutory or **delusional jealousy** (Othello syndrome).
- Auditory, tactile and olfactory hallucinations occur less often.
- Psychosis is a strong predictor of dementia, nursing-home placement and mortality.

#### 5.5 Impulse-control and related disorders

| Behaviour | Description |
| --- | --- |
| Pathological gambling | Including online gambling and lotteries |
| Hypersexuality | Increased demands, pornography, paraphilic behaviour, infidelity |
| Compulsive buying | Debt, hidden purchases |
| Binge eating | Weight gain, nocturnal eating |
| **Punding** | Repetitive, purposeless fascination with objects — sorting, dismantling, collecting |
| Hobbyism, walkabout | Excessive hobbies; aimless wandering |
| **Dopamine dysregulation syndrome** | Compulsive overuse of levodopa or apomorphine beyond motor need, with hypomania, irritability and denial |

Patients often hide these behaviours; spouses usually report them first.

#### 5.6 Cognitive impairment and dementia

- **PD-MCI**: executive dysfunction (planning, set-shifting), attention, processing speed, visuospatial deficits; memory retrieval more than storage.
- **PDD**: progression of these deficits with functional decline; fluctuations, hallucinations and apathy common.
- Posterior cortical deficits (semantic fluency, figure copying) predict progression to dementia better than frontal deficits (CamPaIGN cohort).

#### 5.7 Sleep disorders

- **REM sleep behaviour disorder** — dream enactment; isolated RBD is a strong prodromal marker, converting to a synucleinopathy at about 6% per year and in over 70% by 12 years (Postuma et al., 2019).
- Insomnia, sleep fragmentation, excessive daytime sleepiness and "sleep attacks" with dopamine agonists, restless legs.

#### 5.8 Iatrogenic syndromes

- **Dopamine agonist withdrawal syndrome (DAWS)** — anxiety, panic, dysphoria, fatigue, pain, craving and orthostatic symptoms after tapering an agonist; unresponsive to levodopa; occurs in roughly 15–20% of those tapered.
- Delirium with anticholinergics or amantadine, especially in older patients.
- Psychiatric effects of **STN-DBS**: transient hypomania or impulsivity after programming, later apathy and depression as dopaminergic drugs are reduced, and increased suicide in the first postoperative years.

### 6. Assessment

**History** — from patient and caregiver; timeline relative to motor onset, drug changes and ON/OFF cycles; screen every visit for ICDs (patients rarely volunteer them); suicidal ideation; substance use; previous psychiatric history; family history of addiction.

**Examination** — motor state at the time of interview (ON or OFF); orthostatic BP; sensory impairment (vision); signs of infection or dehydration.

**Rating instruments**

| Domain | Instrument |
| --- | --- |
| Global non-motor | MDS-UPDRS Part I; Non-Motor Symptoms Scale/Questionnaire |
| Depression | GDS-15, HADS, BDI, HAM-D (validated in PD with adjusted cut-offs) |
| Anxiety | Parkinson Anxiety Scale (PAS), HADS |
| Apathy | Starkstein Apathy Scale, Lille Apathy Rating Scale |
| Psychosis | SAPS-PD, SCOPA-PC, NPI |
| ICDs | QUIP / QUIP-RS |
| Cognition | **MoCA** (more sensitive than MMSE for PD-MCI), SCOPA-COG, ACE-III |
| Sleep | RBD Screening Questionnaire, Epworth Sleepiness Scale |

**Investigations** — guided by context: CBC, electrolytes (especially sodium with SSRIs), renal and liver function, TSH, B12, glucose, urine examination for infection; neuroimaging if cognitive decline is rapid or atypical; EEG if fluctuating consciousness suggests seizures or delirium; polysomnography for RBD.

### 7. Differential diagnosis

| Presentation | Consider | Distinguishing point |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Low mood/slowing | Apathy; hypomimia/bradyphrenia; hypothyroidism | Apathy lacks sadness; motor signs without negative cognition |
| Fluctuating confusion + hallucinations | **Delirium** (infection, drugs, dehydration) | Acute onset, inattention, identifiable precipitant |
| Early dementia + hallucinations | **Dementia with Lewy bodies** | Dementia before or within 1 year of parkinsonism |
| Parkinsonism + early falls, gaze palsy, autonomic failure | PSP, MSA | Poor levodopa response, red flags |
| Parkinsonism + psychosis in a patient on antipsychotics | **Drug-induced parkinsonism** | Symmetric onset; temporal link to D2 blocker; normal DAT-SPECT |
| Episodic anxiety/dysphoria | OFF-period non-motor fluctuation | Linked to dosing timetable |
| Excessive spending, hypersexuality | Mania, FTD | Temporal link to agonist; resolves on withdrawal |

### 8. Management

#### 8.1 General principles

1. **Treat the underlying driver first** — optimise motor fluctuations; review and rationalise drugs; treat infection, pain, constipation, sleep.
2. Work with the neurologist; a single coordinated prescriber reduces interactions.
3. Psychoeducation for patient and family; caregiver support.
4. Choose psychotropics that do not worsen parkinsonism, orthostasis, falls or cognition.

#### 8.2 Depression

| Option | Evidence / notes |
| --- | --- |
| SSRIs (sertraline, escitalopram, paroxetine) | **SAD-PD trial (Richard et al., 2012)**: paroxetine and venlafaxine XR superior to placebo without worsening motor function |
| SNRI (venlafaxine) | As above; monitor BP |
| Nortriptyline | Superior to placebo (Menza et al., 2009); anticholinergic burden limits use in elderly or cognitively impaired |
| Pramipexole | Antidepressant effect partly independent of motor benefit (Barone et al., 2010) — weigh against ICD risk |
| Mirtazapine | Helps insomnia and appetite; sedation, weight |
| CBT | Effective in RCTs (Dobkin et al., 2011), including with caregiver involvement and telehealth |
| ECT | Effective for severe/psychotic/suicidal depression; often transiently improves motor symptoms; risk of post-ECT delirium |
| rTMS | Modest antidepressant effect |

Cautions: serotonin syndrome with SSRIs plus MAO-B inhibitors is rare at usual doses, but monitor; SSRIs can occasionally worsen tremor or restless legs; hyponatraemia in the elderly; bupropion may help apathy and fatigue but can cause agitation.

#### 8.3 Anxiety

- Treat OFF-period anxiety by smoothing dopaminergic delivery (fractionated levodopa, COMT/MAO-B inhibitors, longer-acting agonists).
- SSRIs/SNRIs; CBT including exposure for fear of falling and social anxiety.
- Avoid long-term benzodiazepines (falls, cognitive impairment); use only short term.

#### 8.4 Apathy

- Exclude depression and dementia.
- Optimise dopaminergic therapy; avoid abrupt agonist withdrawal.
- Rivastigmine improved apathy in non-demented, non-depressed patients (Devos et al., 2014).
- Behavioural activation, structured routine, exercise, caregiver education.

#### 8.5 Psychosis — stepwise approach

Step 1: Identify and treat **delirium** precipitants (infection, dehydration, hyponatraemia, new drugs)
→ Step 2: Simplify antiparkinsonian drugs in this order, as tolerated: anticholinergics → amantadine → MAO-B inhibitors → dopamine agonists → COMT inhibitors → reduce levodopa last
→ Step 3: If psychosis persists and is distressing or dangerous, add a motor-sparing antipsychotic
→ Step 4: If cognitive impairment is present, add a cholinesterase inhibitor (rivastigmine).

| Antipsychotic | Evidence and use |
| --- | --- |
| **Clozapine** 6.25–50 mg/day | Effective without motor worsening (Parkinson Study Group, NEJM 1999; French Clozapine Study Group, 1999); requires blood-count monitoring; sedation, orthostasis, sialorrhoea |
| **Pimavanserin** 34 mg/day | Selective 5-HT2A inverse agonist; no D2 blockade; effective in a phase 3 RCT (Cummings et al., 2014); FDA approved 2016; not widely available in India |
| Quetiapine 12.5–150 mg/day | Widely used because it needs no monitoring; RCT evidence of efficacy is weak and inconsistent; sedation, orthostasis |
| **Avoid** | Haloperidol and other typicals, risperidone, olanzapine, aripiprazole (motor worsening); in Lewy body disease, severe neuroleptic sensitivity reactions can be fatal |

Cholinesterase inhibitors (rivastigmine, donepezil) reduce hallucinations, particularly with coexisting dementia.

#### 8.6 Impulse-control disorders, DDS and punding

- Screen at every visit when an agonist is prescribed; warn patients and families before starting.
- **Reduce or stop the dopamine agonist** — the most effective intervention — tapering slowly; compensate with levodopa if motor symptoms worsen.
- Monitor for **DAWS** during the taper; it may require slower reduction.
- DDS: restrict and supervise levodopa supply.
- Adjuncts: CBT for ICDs (Okai et al., 2013), financial safeguards, involve spouse; amantadine has conflicting evidence and may even be associated with ICDs; naltrexone not proven.
- STN-DBS may allow agonist reduction but can unmask apathy and depression.

#### 8.7 Cognitive impairment

- **Rivastigmine** — EXPRESS trial (Emre et al., 2004): modest benefit in PDD on cognition and global function; licensed for PDD. Can transiently worsen tremor; nausea.
- Donepezil also used; memantine has smaller, inconsistent effects.
- Withdraw anticholinergic drugs; treat depression, sleep and vascular risk factors; exercise and cognitive stimulation.
- Capacity assessments for financial, driving and treatment decisions as disease progresses.

#### 8.8 Sleep

- RBD: bedroom safety; **melatonin** (3–12 mg) first; clonazepam 0.25–1 mg if needed (caution: falls, sleep apnoea, cognition).
- Insomnia: treat nocturnal akinesia, nocturia, pain; CBT-I.
- Excessive daytime sleepiness: reduce agonists; screen for sleep apnoea.

#### 8.9 Deep-brain stimulation

- Pre-operative psychiatric assessment: exclude active major depression, psychosis, significant cognitive impairment, ICD and unrealistic expectations.
- Post-operative surveillance for mood change, apathy, impulsivity and suicidality, especially in the first year.

#### 8.10 Palliative and caregiver issues

- Advance care planning while capacity is intact.
- Caregiver strain is high when psychosis, ICDs or dementia are present; respite and support groups help.

### 9. Evidence summary

| Trial / study | Design | Main finding |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Reijnders et al., 2008 | Systematic review | MDD ~17%, clinically significant depressive symptoms ~35% |
| SAD-PD (Richard et al., 2012) | RCT, n = 115 | Paroxetine and venlafaxine XR improved depression vs placebo without motor worsening |
| Menza et al., 2009 | RCT, n = 52 | Nortriptyline superior to placebo; paroxetine CR not |
| Barone et al., 2010 | RCT, n = 323 | Pramipexole improved depressive symptoms vs placebo |
| Dobkin et al., 2011 | RCT, n = 80 | CBT superior to clinical monitoring for depression |
| Parkinson Study Group, 1999 | RCT, n = 60 | Low-dose clozapine improved psychosis without motor worsening |
| Cummings et al., 2014 | Phase 3 RCT, n = 199 | Pimavanserin improved PD psychosis |
| EXPRESS (Emre et al., 2004) | RCT, n = 541 | Rivastigmine modestly improved cognition in PDD |
| DOMINION (Weintraub et al., 2010) | Cross-sectional, n = 3,090 | ICDs in 13.6%; dopamine-agonist use raised odds 2–3.5-fold |
| Hely et al., 2008 (Sydney) | 20-year cohort | ~80% of survivors developed dementia |
| Postuma et al., 2019 | Multicentre cohort, n = 1,280 | Isolated RBD converted to neurodegenerative disease at ~6% per year |

### 10. Critical appraisal

- **Under-recognition**: neuropsychiatric symptoms are missed in a large proportion of routine neurology consultations; structured screening changes detection substantially.
- **Diagnostic overlap**: standard depression scales include somatic items that inflate scores in PD; using PD-validated cut-offs or focusing on core mood symptoms is essential.
- **Small, short trials**: the antidepressant evidence base rests on a handful of modest RCTs; placebo response is high.
- **Quetiapine paradox**: the most widely used antipsychotic for PD psychosis has the weakest RCT support, while clozapine, the best supported, is underused because of monitoring burden.
- **Pimavanserin**: a single positive phase 3 trial supported approval; questions remain about effect size, QT prolongation and a mortality signal in elderly patients with dementia, as with all antipsychotics.
- **ICDs reveal a trade-off**: dopamine agonists delay levodopa motor complications but at a behavioural cost that often falls on families; informed consent before prescribing is a clinical and ethical duty.
- **DBS**: excellent motor results can coexist with poorer psychosocial adjustment, highlighting the need for psychiatric input in selection and follow-up.

### 11. Indian context

- Most patients are managed by neurologists or physicians; psychiatric syndromes are often not screened for, and families may attribute behaviour change to ageing, medication "weakness" or character.
- Cost and access: clozapine monitoring requires reliable laboratory follow-up; pimavanserin is not widely available; generic SSRIs, rivastigmine and levodopa are affordable.
- ICD consequences (gambling, debt, sexual behaviour) carry particular stigma and marital consequences; sensitive enquiry with the spouse is important.
- **Mental Healthcare Act 2017**: capacity is decision-specific — a patient with PDD may retain capacity for some decisions; advance directives and nominated representatives are useful before cognition declines. Admission for severe psychosis or suicidality follows MHCA provisions.
- **RPwD Act 2016**: Parkinson's disease is listed under chronic neurological conditions for disability certification and benefits.
- DBS is available at a limited number of tertiary centres; psychiatric screening should be part of these programmes.

### 12. Recent advances

- **α-synuclein seed amplification assays** (CSF, skin) can detect pathological synuclein with high accuracy, including in prodromal disease (Siderowf et al., 2023), leading to proposed **biological staging systems** (2024) — implications for identifying prodromal depression/anxiety and RBD.
- **Pimavanserin** as a non-dopaminergic antipsychotic for PD psychosis (established in the US; limited availability elsewhere).
- **Telehealth CBT** and caregiver-inclusive CBT for depression and anxiety.
- rTMS and transcranial stimulation for depression in PD (investigational to modest evidence).
- Continuous dopaminergic delivery (levodopa–carbidopa intestinal gel, subcutaneous foslevodopa–foscarbidopa, apomorphine infusion) smooths fluctuations and may reduce OFF-period anxiety and dysphoria.
- Prodromal cohorts testing neuroprotective agents in isolated RBD.

### 13. Conclusion

Psychiatric manifestations are a core part of Parkinson's disease, not complications at its margins. They follow the spread of α-synuclein pathology and are shaped by dopaminergic treatment. Good management means routine structured screening, linking each symptom to disease stage, motor fluctuations and medication, rationalising drugs before adding new ones, and using motor-sparing psychiatric treatments — clozapine or pimavanserin for psychosis, SSRIs/SNRIs and CBT for depression, agonist reduction for impulse-control disorders and cholinesterase inhibitors for dementia — with families involved throughout.
