## Exam answer

**Definition.** **Sleep hygiene** is a set of behavioural and environmental practices that promote good-quality, consolidated sleep and daytime alertness, and limit habits that harm sleep. The term was introduced by **Peter Hauri (1977)**.

It is a component of **CBT for insomnia (CBT-I)**, alongside stimulus control, sleep restriction, relaxation and cognitive therapy. **On its own it is not an effective treatment for chronic insomnia** [1, 2].

"**Inadequate sleep hygiene**" is also a clinical subtype of insomnia described in ICSD: insomnia caused or maintained by such practices [1].

### Mechanism: why it works
Each practice targets one of the processes regulating sleep [1]:
- **Homeostatic drive (process S, adenosine):** avoid daytime naps; daytime physical activity builds sleep pressure. Caffeine blocks adenosine receptors.
- **Circadian rhythm (process C, SCN–melatonin):** a regular wake time, morning bright light and evening dim light keep the clock entrained. Evening light (including blue light from screens) suppresses melatonin.
- **Arousal:** caffeine, nicotine, late exercise, worry and stimulating activities increase sympathetic and cortical arousal.
- **Sleep continuity:** alcohol sedates initially but causes REM rebound, awakenings and nocturia in the second half of the night. Noise, light and heat cause arousals.
- **Conditioning:** using the bed only for sleep strengthens the link between bed and sleepiness.

### Components

| Domain | Recommendations |
| --- | --- |
| **Schedule** | Regular wake-up time **7 days a week** (the most important single rule); regular bedtime; avoid long or late naps (if needed, under 30 minutes before about 3 pm) [1] |
| **Substances** | **No caffeine after noon** (half-life 5–6 h); avoid nicotine near bedtime and at night; **limit alcohol**, never as a sleep aid; avoid heavy meals and large fluid intake late at night [1] |
| **Activity** | Regular exercise, ideally in the daytime; avoid vigorous exercise within 2–3 h of bed [1] |
| **Light** | Morning daylight exposure; dim lights and avoid screens 30–60 min before bed |
| **Environment** | Dark, quiet, cool, comfortable bedroom; manage noise (earplugs), pets, children, snoring bed partner [1] |
| **Pre-sleep routine** | A 30–60-minute wind-down (bath, reading, relaxation); write down worries and the next day's plans earlier in the evening ("worry time") |
| **Bed use** | Bed for sleep (and sex) only; do not work, eat or watch TV in bed; **do not watch the clock**; if not asleep in about 20 minutes, get up (stimulus control) [1] |

### Assessment of sleep hygiene
- Sleep history and a **2-week sleep diary**, recording caffeine (C), medication (M), alcohol (A), exercise (E), naps, bed and wake times [1].
- **Sleep Hygiene Index** (13 items) or Sleep Hygiene Awareness and Practice Scale.
- Identify which practices are relevant to this patient. Generic leaflets are less effective than personalised advice.

### Clinical use
- **Primary prevention and psychoeducation:** students, shift workers, elderly people, psychiatric patients.
- **First step in all sleep complaints,** including insomnia, before hypnotics.
- **Component of CBT-I.** It is weak as monotherapy: in trials it is often used as the **control condition** because its effect on chronic insomnia is small [2].
- **Particular groups:**
  - **Bipolar disorder:** regular rhythms are protective (interpersonal and social rhythm therapy).
  - **Depression, anxiety, ADHD, autism, dementia:** a structured day, daylight and activity.
  - **Substance use:** sleep problems during withdrawal and early abstinence predict relapse [1].
  - **Children:** consistent bedtime routines; screens out of the bedroom.

### Key evidence
- **Meta-analyses** show sleep hygiene education alone has small effects on insomnia, smaller than full CBT-I (Irish et al. 2015; Chung et al. 2018) [2].
- **Individual components** have experimental support: caffeine timing (Drake 2013, 6 hours before bed still reduced sleep by about 1 hour), evening alcohol, light exposure, exercise.
- The **AASM (2021)** advises **against sleep hygiene as a single-component therapy** for chronic insomnia; it should be part of CBT-I [2].

**Conclusion.** Sleep hygiene is the foundation of sleep health: regular timing, light and activity, avoidance of stimulants and alcohol, and a calm sleep environment. It is cheap, safe and useful for prevention and for mild or situational insomnia. Clinicians should not mistake it for treatment of chronic insomnia, which needs **full CBT-I** (especially stimulus control and sleep restriction), with medication only as a short-term adjunct.

## In depth

### Background
- **Hauri (1977)** coined "sleep hygiene" in *The Sleep Disorders*. The rules grew out of clinical experience rather than trials.
- **Rule lists** have varied widely between sources, and they overlap with stimulus control instructions (Bootzin 1972).
- **ICSD-2 (2005):** "inadequate sleep hygiene" was a diagnosis. In ICSD-3 it became a descriptive subtype of chronic insomnia [1].

### Epidemiology
- **Students:** poor sleep hygiene is common in students and young adults; screen use in bed is near-universal.
- **India:** late dinners, evening tea and coffee, shared bedrooms, heat and noise in urban housing, night-shift work and late-night screen use are common barriers. Indian surveys of medical students show high rates of poor sleep quality (PSQI >5 in more than half in several studies; check the specific study before quoting).

### Mechanism in detail
| Practice | Physiology |
| --- | --- |
| Caffeine | Adenosine A1/A2A antagonist; half-life 3–7 h, longer in pregnancy, liver disease and with fluvoxamine (CYP1A2 inhibition) |
| Alcohol | GABA-A agonism → faster onset; metabolised within about 4 h → rebound arousal, REM rebound, sympathetic activation, worsened OSA |
| Nicotine | Stimulant; withdrawal at night causes awakenings |
| Evening light | Melanopsin retinal ganglion cells → SCN → melatonin suppression → phase delay |
| Morning light | Phase advance; strengthens rhythm amplitude |
| Exercise | ↑ slow-wave sleep and homeostatic drive; body-temperature fall afterwards aids sleep onset |
| Bedroom temperature | Sleep onset needs a fall in core temperature; heat fragments sleep |
| Naps | Discharge homeostatic pressure; late naps delay sleep onset |

### Clinical features in detail: typical poor-hygiene patterns
- Irregular schedule with weekend "catch-up" sleep ("social jet lag").
- Lying in bed scrolling the phone.
- Evening tea or coffee "to relax".
- Alcohol as a nightcap.
- Long afternoon naps.
- Sleeping late on holidays, followed by Sunday-night insomnia.

### Assessment in detail
| Tool | Description |
| --- | --- |
| Sleep diary (2 weeks) | Timing, latency, awakenings, naps, caffeine, alcohol, exercise, medication [1] |
| Sleep Hygiene Index | 13 items, 5-point scale; higher score = poorer hygiene |
| Sleep Hygiene Awareness and Practice Scale | Knowledge and practice |
| PSQI / ISI | Outcome measures [1] |
| Actigraphy | Objective timing and regularity [1] |

### Differential: when sleep hygiene advice is not enough
| Problem | Needed instead |
| --- | --- |
| Chronic insomnia disorder | CBT-I (stimulus control, sleep restriction, cognitive therapy) |
| OSA | PSG, CPAP |
| RLS | Iron, dopamine agonists or α2δ ligands |
| Circadian phase disorders | Timed light and melatonin |
| Depression or mania | Treat the mood disorder |
| Narcolepsy | Specialist management |

### Management details: delivering advice effectively
- **Personalise:** pick the 2–3 rules relevant to this patient instead of a 15-item leaflet.
- **Explain the rationale** for each rule (adenosine, light, conditioning). This improves adherence.
- **Pair with stimulus control** and a fixed wake time.
- **Review with a sleep diary** after 2 weeks.
- **Avoid "orthosomnia":** obsessive pursuit of perfect sleep, sometimes driven by wearable trackers, can worsen insomnia.
- **Special populations:**
  - Elderly: daytime activity and light; avoid long time in bed; manage nocturia.
  - Shift workers: anchor sleep, blackout curtains, strategic napping and caffeine, light management.
  - Adolescents: later natural phase; screen curfews.
  - Inpatients: reduce night-time ward noise and light; daytime activity programmes.

### Evidence table
| Study | Design | Result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Irish et al. 2015 (Sleep Med Rev) | Review | Individual components have support; sleep hygiene as a standalone insomnia treatment has limited efficacy |
| Chung et al. 2018 (Sleep Med Rev) | Meta-analysis | Sleep hygiene education inferior to CBT-I; small effect alone |
| Drake et al. 2013 (J Clin Sleep Med) | RCT crossover | 400 mg caffeine even 6 h before bed reduced total sleep time by about 1 h |
| Edinger et al. 2021 (AASM guideline) | Guideline | Recommends against sleep hygiene as a single-component therapy |

### Critical appraisal
- **Rules lack standardisation**, and some are based on expert opinion; for example, the evidence on avoiding evening exercise is weak (moderate exercise may not harm sleep).
- **Knowledge is not behaviour change:** many people with insomnia already practise good hygiene. The perpetuating factors are conditioned arousal and time in bed, which need CBT-I.
- **Risk of blame:** framing insomnia as poor "hygiene" can feel moralising. Explain the physiology instead.
- **Cultural fit:** advice must suit Indian homes (shared rooms, heat, late meals, power cuts).

### Indian context
- **IPS sleep CPG** (Gupta et al. 2017) includes sleep hygiene as part of non-pharmacological management of insomnia [1].
- **Yoga and lifestyle:** yoga, pranayama and yoga nidra are culturally acceptable add-ons. Ayurvedic "dinacharya" (daily routine) overlaps with circadian hygiene.
- **Public health:** school and college sleep education; the Tele-MANAS helpline for sleep and stress complaints.

### Recent advances
- **Established:** sleep hygiene incorporated into digital CBT-I (Sleepio) [1].
- **Emerging:**
  - Digital "nudges" (night mode, screen curfews).
  - Light hygiene (circadian lighting).
  - "Social jet lag" as a public-health target.
  - Wearables: useful for feedback but with orthosomnia risk.
