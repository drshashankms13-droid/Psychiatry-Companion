## Exam answer

### Definition [1, 2]
- **DSM-5-TR:** a tic is a **sudden, rapid, recurrent, nonrhythmic motor movement or vocalisation** [2].
- **Abdo's keyword is "stereotyped"** [1]. Tics are:
  - preceded by a **rising urge or discomfort** (the "sensory tic") that is **relieved** by the movement (the "itch and scratch" analogy);
  - **suppressible** for short periods, at the cost of mounting inner tension and a **rebound** afterwards;
  - easily mimicked.

### Phenomenology [1, 2]

| | Motor | Vocal (phonic) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Simple** | Eye blinking (often the first tic), grimacing, shoulder shrug | Throat clearing, sniffing, grunting, barking |
| **Complex** | Combined movements, touching or smelling objects, jumping, **echopraxia**, **copropraxia** | Words or phrases, **palilalia**, **echolalia**, **coprolalia** (an abrupt bark-like utterance without normal prosody) |

- **Distribution:** mainly face, upper arms and neck [1].
- **Dystonic tics:** abrupt onset followed by a slow or sustained posture. They are recognised as tics by their suppressibility and stereotypy [1].
- **Modifiers:** tics wax and wane. They are **worse with anxiety, excitement, exhaustion and stress**, and **better during calm, focused activity**. Talking about tics can trigger them [2, 3].
- **In clinic:** tics are often **absent in the examination room** because anxiety suppresses them. **Video recording at home** helps [1].

### Classification: DSM-5-TR (hierarchical) [2]

| Disorder | Tics | Duration | Onset |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Tourette's disorder** (F95.2) | **Multiple motor plus at least one vocal** (not necessarily concurrent) | **More than 1 year** since first tic | **Before 18** |
| **Persistent (chronic) motor or vocal tic disorder** (F95.1) | Motor **or** vocal, not both | More than 1 year | Before 18 |
| **Provisional tic disorder** (F95.0) | Motor and/or vocal | **Less than 1 year** | Before 18 |
| Other specified / unspecified (F95.8/9) | Atypical (e.g., onset after 18) or known cause | — | — |

All require that the tics are not due to a substance (e.g., cocaine) or a medical condition (e.g., Huntington's disease, post-viral encephalitis). A higher diagnosis in the hierarchy excludes the lower ones.

### Epidemiology and course [2, 3]
- **Prevalence:**
  - transient tics in **5–20% of children** [3];
  - tics in up to 21% of school-age children and adolescents [1];
  - **Tourette's syndrome about 0.7%** [3]; 3–9 per 1,000 school-age children in Canada [2];
  - **males 2–4 times more than females** [2, 3].
- **Course:**
  - onset at **4–6 years**; eye blinking is a characteristic first symptom;
  - **peak severity at 10–12 years**, declining in adolescence;
  - **up to 65% have no or only mild tics by adulthood** [3].
- **First onset in adulthood is very rare.** It suggests substances (cocaine), a CNS insult or a **functional disorder** [2].
- **Aetiology** [2]:
  - **heritability 70–85%**; shared genetic variance with OCD, ADHD and ASD;
  - perinatal adversity and advanced paternal age;
  - autoimmune links (Hashimoto's; Sydenham's chorea); **PANDAS/PANS** in a subset [2, 3].

### Approach to a person with tics [1, 2, 3, 4]

**Step 1: confirm the movement is a tic** (Abdo's four questions [1]):
1. Which types of movement disorder are present?
2. Which one is dominant?
3. What are the associated neurological and non-neurological features?
4. What is the differential diagnosis?

**Differentiating tics from other movements** [1, 2]:

| Movement | Distinguishing features |
| --- | --- |
| **Tic** | Stereotyped; premonitory urge, relief after; suppressible; rebound |
| Myoclonus | Shock-like, faster; **not suppressible**, no urge; may occur in sleep |
| Chorea | **Random, flowing**, non-stereotyped jerks; motor impersistence |
| Dystonia | Sustained co-contraction producing abnormal posture; triggered by voluntary action; absent in sleep |
| **Motor stereotypies** | Earlier onset (often under 3), longer, **rhythmic**, no urge, **stop with distraction**; seen in autism, intellectual disability, Rett syndrome, congenital blindness or deafness |
| Paroxysmal dyskinesias | Episodic dystonic or choreoathetoid movements precipitated by sudden voluntary movement |
| **OCD compulsions** | Anxiety-driven, in response to obsessions. Tic-related repetition aims at a **"just right"** feeling |
| BFRBs | More goal-directed and complex |
| **Functional tic-like behaviours** | See below |

**Step 2: look for a secondary cause** [2, 3]:
- substances (cocaine, stimulants);
- medical conditions (Huntington's, post-encephalitic);
- infection-related onset (PANDAS/PANS).

**Step 3: distinguish functional tic-like behaviours** [2, 4]:
- **Profile:** a rapid rise during COVID-19 ("TikTok tics"), mostly in **females aged 15–25**, with large-amplitude complex arm movements, self-injury and coprolalia.
- **ESSTS major criteria:**
  - **onset at 12 or older**;
  - **rapid evolution**;
  - **specific phenomenology** (at least one specific complex motor and one complex phonic tic; complex outnumbering simple tics).
- **Other pointers:** movements not fully stereotyped; interference with speech or action; no clear premonitory urge; inability to suppress; "**tic attacks**" lasting 15 minutes to hours [2, 4].
- **Diagnostic difficulty:** they are challenging to diagnose because primary tics also show sudden onset, suggestibility and fluctuation. Functional tics are rare, about 2% of FMD [4].

**Step 4: screen for comorbidity, often the main source of impairment** [2, 3]:
- **ADHD**;
- **OCD** (early onset; symmetry and exactness; taboo thoughts);
- ASD, learning disorder, disruptive behaviour;
- **anxiety, depression, substance use** (adolescents and adults);
- separation anxiety (children).

**Step 5: assess impairment and risk** [2]:
- distress, school or work impact, peer victimisation;
- physical injury (eye injury, cervical disc disease from forceful movements);
- **suicide:** in Tourette's or chronic tic disorder, OR **3.86 for attempts and 4.39 for deaths**. The strongest predictors are tic persistence beyond young adulthood and a previous attempt.

### Management of chronic tic disorders [3]
**Principle:** most people with tics **do not need medication**. Treat comorbid conditions **first**, then assess disability from the tics themselves.

**1. Education** for the patient, family and school is crucial [3]. Authority figures may misread tics as purposeful [2].

**2. Behavioural treatment** (first line for distressing or disabling tics):
- **Habit reversal training**;
- **CBIT** (comprehensive behavioural intervention for tics);
- **exposure and response prevention**.

Effect sizes are similar to antipsychotics [3].

**3. Pharmacotherapy** (Maudsley Figure 5.2: education/behavioural → α2-agonist → antipsychotic → other):

| Step | Drug | Evidence and notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **α2-agonists (try first)** | **Clonidine 3–5 µg/kg** (built up gradually; patch available); **guanfacine** | Clonidine efficacy confirmed in a 2023 network meta-analysis (guanfacine less so). Sedation, postural hypotension, depression; **do not stop clonidine suddenly (rebound hypertension)**. Useful when stimulants worsen tics in ADHD |
| **Antipsychotics** | **Aripiprazole** (5–20 mg by weight), **risperidone**, pimozide, haloperidol, sulpiride, tiapride, ziprasidone, olanzapine | Possibly more effective than α2-agonists but more adverse effects. **Aripiprazole RCTs:** 69–74% much improved vs 38% placebo. **Pimozide:** robust Cochrane efficacy, **ECG essential** (also for haloperidol); haloperidol often poorly tolerated. Antipsychotics probably do not differ in efficacy. Weight and metabolic effects are common, even with aripiprazole |
| **Other / experimental** | **Ecopipam** (D1 antagonist; positive RCTs); tetrabenazine (add-on); topiramate, baclofen, nicotine, metoclopramide (limited) | Deutetrabenazine, valbenazine, ondansetron and pergolide are probably **not** effective. Cannabinoids: little evidence |
| **Focal painful tics** | **Botulinum toxin** | Low-quality evidence |
| **PANDAS/PANS** | Antibiotics, immunomodulation (trials) | More research needed |

- **Evidence problems:** variable and fluctuating severity, small trials, comorbidity confounding, and widespread complementary therapy use [3].
- **In practice:** it is "extremely rare" to need treatments beyond antipsychotics [3].

**Conclusion.** In a person with tics:
1. **Confirm the phenomenology:** stereotyped, suppressible, with an urge and relief.
2. **Classify by DSM-5-TR:** type, duration over 1 year, onset before 18.
3. **Exclude secondary and functional causes**, especially adult or late-adolescent onset in females with rapid evolution.
4. **Identify and treat comorbid ADHD, OCD, anxiety and depression**, which often drive impairment.
5. **Treat in steps:** education, habit reversal/CBIT, then clonidine or guanfacine, then aripiprazole or risperidone.
6. **Remember suicide risk.**

## In depth

### Lishman: Tourette's syndrome in more depth [5]
- **Phenomenology:**
  - motor tics begin about age 7, vocal tics about 4 years later, coprolalia around puberty;
  - simple tics can be clonic (blink), tonic (arm stretch) or dystonic (grimace).
- **Coprolalia:**
  - about one-third of clinic samples, but **only about 10% of all sufferers**; less common than echolalia or palilalia;
  - "mental coprolalia"; **copropraxia** (obscene gestures);
  - non-obscene socially inappropriate behaviour.
- **Modulation:**
  - **worse** with anxiety, excitement, anger, boredom, self-consciousness, and **"releasing tics"** on relaxing after stress;
  - **better** with concentration, alcohol and sexual arousal;
  - tics can persist in sleep, and even increase in REM sleep.
- **Echophenomena:** fewer than one-third.
- **Cognition:**
  - IQ usually normal;
  - **visuopractic deficits** (verbal > performance IQ);
  - **attentional deficits** (sustaining, focusing, shifting);
  - more common in autism spectrum disorder.
- **Comorbidity:**

  | Condition | Frequency or feature |
  | --- | --- |
  | **ADHD** | 31–91%; often the reason for referral and a major contributor to psychosocial impairment |
  | **OCD/OCB** | 32% up to 89%; **"evening up"**/symmetry, touching, counting, "just right"; shared genetic liability; OCD in relatives |
  | **Self-injurious behaviour** | 14–60% (definition-dependent); severe form about 4%, linked to impulse-control problems |
  | **Depression / anxiety** | About 20% / 18% |
  | **Sleep problems** | Nightmares, sleepwalking, night terrors (up to one-third) |
  | Conduct problems, sexual disinhibition | Claimed, but without controlled data |
  | Psychosis | No special relationship |

- **Course:**
  - waxing and waning; tics change over time;
  - **about half tic-free by 18**; 30–40% remit completely by late adolescence;
  - adult symptoms are more stable;
  - **quality of life** worsened by employment status, tic severity, OCB, anxiety and depression.
- **Aetiology** (from older to current views):
  - **psychoanalytic:** repressed aggressive or sexual impulses; coprolalia seemed to support this, but susceptibility to emotion does not imply causation;
  - **learning theory:** a conditioned anxiety-reducing response;
  - **developmental:** immature motor control; male excess, onset at 6–8, remission at adolescence;
  - **organic:** an inherited developmental disorder of synaptic transmission that **disinhibits cortico–striato–thalamo–cortical circuits**.
    - **Dopamine hypersensitivity:** neuroleptic response; tardive Tourettism; raised blink rate; more striatal DAT.
    - **Imaging:** caudate and basal ganglia asymmetry; prefrontal/orbitofrontal change; hypoperfusion; reduced D2.
    - **Physiology:** **no Bereitschaftspotential before tics** (but normal before mimicked tics), implying a subcortical origin.
    - **Perinatal risk:** maternal stress, smoking, caffeine or alcohol; low birthweight; hypoxia.
  - **Genetics:** MZ concordance 53% vs DZ 8% (77% vs 23% for any tic). The **unaffected MZ co-twin had the higher birthweight**. Possibly a major autosomal gene with incomplete penetrance; spectrum with chronic tics and OCD; linkage to chromosomes 11q, 4, 8, 2.
  - **PANDAS** (streptococcal; anti-basal ganglia antibodies): accounts for **only a small proportion**; prospective studies found no clear link.
- **Older treatment evidence (Lishman):**
  - **Aim:** reduce tics enough to allow normal function, not abolish them.
  - **Behavioural:** massed practice (mixed results); **incompatible-response training**; operant reward for tic-free periods.
  - **Haloperidol:** over 80% improve, but 13% stop because of dysphoria or sedation; start 0.25–0.5 mg, often 2–3 mg/day suffices.
  - **Pimozide:** less sedating, possibly preferable. **Sulpiride:** fewer EPS.
  - **Risperidone:** two RCTs.
  - **Clonidine/guanfacine:** RCTs.
  - Others: clonazepam, clomipramine, tetrabenazine, presynaptic dopamine agonists (pergolide, ropinirole).
  - **Botulinum toxin to the vocal cords** for vocal tics or coprolalia.
  - **Surgery** (thalamic or dentate lesions; **thalamic high-frequency stimulation**) for occasional refractory cases.
  - **Treat comorbid OCD (SSRI/clomipramine; fluoxetine did not worsen tics) and ADHD (stimulants do not worsen tics) first** (Leckman).
  - Supportive psychotherapy, group counselling and Tourette associations.
- **Differential:** **latah**, myriachit and the "jumping Frenchmen of Maine" are **startle-induced culture-bound reactions**, not tics: echolalia, echopraxia and automatic obedience, always provoked.

### Why tics are hard to see in clinic [1]
Anxiety during examination suppresses tics. Partners or parents often notice them more than patients. Home video is a useful substitute.
