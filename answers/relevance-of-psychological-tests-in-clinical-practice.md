## Exam answer

**Introduction.**
- Psychological (psychometric) tests are **standardised procedures**, usually **numerically scored**, validated on large groups so that **norms** and individual variation are known [1].
- Their clinical value "has been much debated". Over-enthusiastic claims have had to be tempered, and psychiatrists often **expect unrealistic exactitude** from them [1].
- Used properly, in **collaboration with clinical psychologists** (especially neuropsychologists), they add valuable evidence to diagnosis and management [1].

### Advantages over clinical assessment [1]
1. **Standardised and scored:** results can be compared with norms, so one can speak in **probabilities**, for example of brain damage.
2. **Repeatable:** a **baseline** can be set and change tracked accurately, highlighting decline.
3. **Detailed:** can pursue a suspected area (such as minor memory or verbal deficits) much more thoroughly than an interview.
4. **Structure of the deficit:** can be explored, if the tester is flexible. The *nature* of failure matters, not just the score (Piercy).

### Clinical applications
**1. Diagnosis of organic brain disorder and dementia [1]:**
- **Bedside screening:**
  - **MMSE:** 5–10 minutes; out of 30; below 24 usually indicates impairment; tracks change. Weak on memory, general knowledge and **frontal-executive** items.
  - **Addenbrooke's Cognitive Examination:** separates "functional" from "organic" disorders reasonably well; **screening cut-off of 88**.
- **Intelligence:** **WAIS-III**:
  - verbal and performance IQ;
  - indices of verbal comprehension, perceptual organisation, working memory, processing speed;
  - mean 100, SD 15; below 70 is in the ID range;
  - "**hold**" (vocabulary, information) vs "**don't hold**" (digit symbol, block design) tests;
  - comparison with premorbid IQ gives prima facie evidence of **decline**.
- **Premorbid IQ:** **NART** (reading irregular words), resistant to dementia. It may underestimate in moderate dementia and is unsuitable for non-native speakers or poor readers.
- **Memory:** **WMS-III**: immediate, general (delayed) and working-memory indices. The immediate vs delayed comparison separates **encoding from retention** deficits.
- **Specific domains:**
  - perception and attention (Bender–Gestalt, VOSP, TEA);
  - language (Boston Naming, Token Test);
  - memory (Rey AVLT, Rivermead, Benton);
  - **executive** (Wisconsin Card Sorting, Stroop, verbal fluency, Trail Making, Tower of London, BADS, Hayling, Brixton);
  - vigilance (PASAT, CPT);
  - batteries (Halstead–Reitan, CANTAB);
  - dementia scales (CAMCOG, Blessed).

**2. Intellectual disability [2]:**
- **Confirms diagnosis and severity.** ICD-10 IQ bands: mild 50–69, moderate 35–49, severe 20–34, profound below 20. DSM-5 uses 65–75 (70 ± 5).
- **Indian IQ tests:** Binet–Kamat Test (BKT), MISIC, WISC; developmental scales (Developmental Screening Test; Developmental Assessment Scales for Indian Infants) below 3 years.
- **Adaptive behaviour:** **VSMS** is the only standardised Indian measure. It gives a **social quotient (SQ)** across 8 domains. **When IQ and SQ disagree, decide in favour of SQ.**
- **Disability certification** uses VSMS bands: 0–20 profound 100%; 21–35 severe 90%; 36–54 moderate 75%; 55–69 mild 50%; 70–84 borderline 25%.
- **Caution:** Indian norms are often old (**Flynn effect**), so IQ scores should not be interpreted rigidly. Choose tests to suit sensory or motor impairments.
- **Psychopathology scales** (Psychiatric Assessment Schedule for Adults with Developmental Disability, Reiss Screen, Developmental Behavior Checklist) should only **complement** clinical observation.

**3. Specific learning disorder [3]:**
- Confirms diagnosis and plans intervention: **IQ** (BKT, MISIC, WISC-IV) plus **academic tests** (NIMHANS Index for SLD, WRAT, WIAT, Woodcock–Johnson).
- Patterns:
  - **performance IQ higher than verbal**;
  - "**ACID**" profile (low Arithmetic, Coding, Information, Digit span);
  - WISC-IV weaknesses in verbal comprehension, working memory and processing speed.
- The psychiatrist should take a full history and examine the child **before** psychological testing.

**4. Rehabilitation [1]:**
- Identifies **intact areas** and targets for re-education.
- Monitors progress and sets realistic goals.
- **Aptitude tests** (such as the Vineland Social Maturity Scale) guide resettlement.

**5. Medicolegal and effort assessment [1]:**
- In compensation settings, **forced-choice recognition tests** detect poor effort. Scoring **below chance** suggests the person knows the right answer but chooses the wrong one.
- Testing is **not truly objective**: it needs the subject's cooperation, and a patient may wish to influence the result.

**6. Pre-surgical and medical settings [4]:**
- Pre-bariatric surgery evaluation typically includes **psychological testing** and a psychosocial interview.

**7. Personality assessment [4]:**
- Projective (**Rorschach**) and objective (**MMPI**) instruments have been used in psychosomatic research.

### Limitations [1]
1. **Sensitivity and specificity:** no single test separates brain-damaged from other patients without overlap. Tests are seldom validated in borderline cases or in large normal samples.
2. **Attention and motivation:** **depression** disrupts memory tests, and **thought disorder** lowers abstract-thinking scores. Tests used for organic vs functional differentiation must be standardised on psychiatric patients.
3. **Theoretical basis:** "global brain-damage" tests ignore regional organisation. For example, verbal tests miss non-dominant visuospatial deficits.
4. **Cultural and educational bias:** the WAIS is sensitive to education, language and culture [1]. Indian norms are limited [2, 3].
5. **Subtest scatter** can reflect education or occupation, not pathology. **Verbal–performance discrepancy is not a reliable guide to laterality** [1].
6. **"Untestable" patients:** "patients are never untestable, although the clinician may be incapable of testing them". Bedside improvisation still yields information [1].

### Using tests well [1]
- **Ask realistic questions.** "Exclude brain damage" or "localise the lesion" will not get clear answers. "Assess the likelihood of focal or diffuse damage" will. Lesion localisation belongs to neuroradiology.
- **Discuss the patient** with the psychologist beforehand.
- **Interpret results with all other information.** "The worst service to psychometry, and to the patient, is likely to come from attempts to rely on test scores viewed in isolation."

**Conclusion.** Psychological tests give **standardised, quantifiable, repeatable** measures that strengthen diagnosis (dementia, organic disorders, ID, SLD), measure severity for **disability certification**, set **baselines**, guide **rehabilitation** and help in **medicolegal** assessment. They are vulnerable to motivation, mood, culture and old norms, and must always be interpreted **alongside the clinical picture**.

## In depth

### WAIS-III subtests [1]
| Verbal | Performance |
|---|---|
| Information, Comprehension, Arithmetic, Similarities, Digit Span, Vocabulary, Letter–Number Sequencing | Digit Symbol, Picture Completion, Block Design, Picture Arrangement, Matrix Reasoning, Symbol Search |
- **WASI** (short form): vocabulary, block design, similarities, matrix reasoning.

### WMS-III indices [1]
- **Immediate memory:** Logical Memory 1, Verbal Paired Associates 1, Faces 1, Family Pictures 1.
- **General (delayed) memory:** the same subtests, delayed (about 30 minutes).
- **Working memory:** Letter–Number Sequencing, Spatial Span.
