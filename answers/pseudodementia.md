## Exam answer

**Definition.**
- Pseudodementia is **cognitive impairment caused by depression (or another non-dementing condition) that mimics dementia** [2].
- Lishman: "a patient who **appears demented but is not**, perhaps because of depression or some other condition". It is a concept "**used only rarely today**", and careful clinical examination usually allows the distinction [1].
- **Kiloh (1961)** coined the term [2].

**Types [1]:**
- **Depressive pseudodementia** (commonest);
- **hysterical pseudodementia / Ganser state**, and rarely **simulation**: suggested by inconsistent performance.

**Frequency [1]:**
- In consecutive series of suspected dementia, about **15%** were judged not demented after full evaluation.
- **Non-organic psychiatric disorder masquerading as dementia** accounted for about **8%** overall, **most often depression**.
- **Wells:** potentially correctable disorders (depression, drug toxicity, hydrocephalus, benign masses) occur in about **15%** of patients.

### Clinical differentiation [1, 2, 3]
| Feature | Depressive pseudodementia | True dementia |
|---|---|---|
| Onset | **Related to a depressive episode**; relatively acute | Insidious, no clear precipitant |
| Duration before presentation | Relatively short | Months to years |
| Subjective complaints | **Prominent, amplified; distress about deficits** | Minimised or denied |
| Answers | **"I don't know"**; poor effort | Confabulation, **near-misses** |
| Consistency | **Variable** performance | Consistent impairment |
| Cognitive profile | Diffuse, no pattern | Pattern-specific |
| Account of illness | **Coherent account and accurate chronology** | Poor |
| Attention | Impaired by lack of interest, anxiety and morbid preoccupation, not by disorganisation of thought | Pervasive |
| Family history | Often **affective disorder** | Often dementia |
| Response to antidepressant | **Cognitive improvement** | None |

- **Key distinguishing point:** in pseudodementia the patient **emphasises** disability; in dementia the patient **minimises** it [2].
- **Mental state:** a marked depressive component may indicate pseudodementia. Equally, **concurrent depression** may be aggravating genuine organic disease [1].
- The patient's **attitude to symptoms**, and how far the reported disabilities really interfere with daily life, are crucial observations [1].

### Assessment [1, 2]
- **History** from patient and informant: onset, course, mood, previous episodes, family history.
- **Mental state:** depressive features, consistency, effort.
- **Bedside cognitive testing** (MMSE and multi-domain testing); psychometry in borderline cases.
- **Screen for reversible causes ("DEMENTIAS")** [2]:
  - **D**rugs;
  - **E**ndocrine (hypothyroidism);
  - **M**etabolic (B12, folate, renal, hepatic);
  - **E**ye and ear;
  - **N**PH;
  - **T**rauma (subdural);
  - **I**nfections (neurosyphilis, HIV);
  - **A**ffective (pseudodementia);
  - **S**pace-occupying lesion.
- **Minimum investigations:** FBC, urea, electrolytes, LFT, plus further tests as indicated [1].

### Management [1, 2, 3]
- **Treat the depression.** Pseudodementia **improves with antidepressant treatment** [2, 3].
- **Follow up regularly.** If the diagnosis remains uncertain, the time course will reveal it [1].

### Prognosis: an important caveat [1, 3]
- **Pseudodementia is "not a benign condition".** Apparent dementia that reverses as depression resolves is followed, **5 years later, by established dementia in the majority** (Saez-Fonseca et al. 2007) [1].
- Late-life depression can be a **prodrome** of dementia. Early neurodegenerative changes may affect mood circuits (for example, the locus coeruleus) before memory circuits [3].
- Chronic depression may damage the hippocampus through **glucocorticoid neurotoxicity** [3].

**Conclusion.** Pseudodementia is depression presenting as cognitive impairment. Clues:
- an onset linked to mood;
- amplified complaints and "I don't know" answers;
- inconsistency;
- a family history of mood disorder.

It responds to antidepressants, but carries a high risk of later true dementia, so long-term follow-up is essential.

## In depth

### Reversible dementia in perspective [1]
- Clarfield (1988): **13%** had potentially reversible dementia. The 2003 meta-analysis (more than 5500 patients) found **9%** potentially reversible but only **0.6%** actually reversing.
- **India:** a higher proportion of neuroinfections and **B12 deficiency**, with genuine reversibility in many (Srikanth and Nagaraja 2005).
