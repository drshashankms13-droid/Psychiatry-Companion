## Exam answer

**Definition.** Social anxiety disorder (SAD; social phobia; ICD-11 6B04) is marked and excessive fear or anxiety in one or more social situations where the person may be scrutinised: interactions (conversations, meeting unfamiliar people), being observed (eating, drinking, writing) or performing (speeches). The core fear is of acting in a way, or showing anxiety symptoms (blushing, trembling, sweating), that will be **negatively evaluated** (humiliating, embarrassing, leading to rejection). Situations are avoided or endured with intense distress for **several months** (DSM-5-TR: ≥6 months), with significant impairment. DSM specifier: **performance only**.

### Mechanism
- **Clark & Wells cognitive model (1995):** social situation → activation of assumptions ("I must not show anxiety"; "if they see I'm nervous they'll reject me") → perceived social danger → **self-focused attention** and a **distorted observer-perspective self-image** ("I look red and stupid") + **safety behaviours** (avoiding eye contact, rehearsing sentences, gripping a glass, hiding the face) + somatic and cognitive anxiety symptoms → anticipatory and post-event processing ("post-mortem") → maintained fear.
- **Biology:** heritability ~30–40%; behavioural inhibition temperament; amygdala and insula hyper-reactivity to faces; serotonergic and dopaminergic (D2) changes [1].

### Clinical features
- Onset in adolescence (median ~13 years); chronic course without treatment.
- Fear of scrutiny, blushing, trembling, sweating, palpitations, mind going blank.
- Avoidance of speaking, eating in public, phone calls, meetings; anticipatory anxiety for days.
- Situationally bound panic attacks.
- Consequences: underachievement, dropping out of education, unemployment, fewer relationships.
- Comorbidity: depression, alcohol misuse (self-medication), avoidant personality disorder, other anxiety disorders.

### Differential diagnosis

| Condition | Distinguishing feature |
| --- | --- |
| Normal shyness / performance anxiety | No marked impairment or avoidance |
| **Avoidant personality disorder** | Pervasive, lifelong sense of inadequacy and fear of rejection across all contexts; ego-syntonic; may be a severe form of generalised SAD [2] |
| Panic disorder with agoraphobia | Fear of the panic itself and of being unable to escape, not of evaluation |
| GAD | Worries about many domains, not just social |
| Depression | Withdrawal from loss of interest, not fear of scrutiny |
| Body dysmorphic disorder | Fear focused on a perceived appearance defect |
| Olfactory reference disorder | Belief of emitting an offensive odour |
| Autism spectrum disorder | Social communication deficits, restricted interests, lifelong |
| Schizophrenia/paranoid states | Delusional conviction of persecution or reference |
| Taijin kyofusho (culture-related) | Fear of offending **others** (Japanese/East Asian variant) |
| Selective mutism | Children; failure to speak in specific settings |

### Assessment
Liebowitz Social Anxiety Scale (LSAS); Social Phobia Inventory (SPIN; ≥19 suggests SAD); Mini-SPIN; screen for depression, alcohol use and suicidality; developmental history (behavioural inhibition, bullying); occupational and educational impact.

### Management
**First line: individual CBT** (Clark & Wells model; NICE CG159 recommends 14 sessions over 4 months). It is more effective than SSRIs and more durable [3].

**CBT components**
1. Individualised formulation (vicious circle).
2. **Self-focused attention and safety-behaviour experiment:** have a conversation with and without safety behaviours and rate how anxious and how visible the anxiety felt. This demonstrates that safety behaviours increase anxiety and make the person look odd.
3. **Video feedback:** correct the distorted self-image by watching a recording of oneself.
4. **Attention training:** shift attention outward to the task and the other person.
5. **Behavioural experiments in feared situations:** test predictions (e.g., deliberately spill water; blush intentionally).
6. **Survey technique:** ask others what they think of visible anxiety.
7. Dealing with anticipatory worry and **post-event rumination**.
8. Restructuring early memories and self-beliefs (imagery rescripting).
9. Relapse prevention.

Group CBT (Heimberg's CBGT) is effective but less so than individual cognitive therapy. Internet CBT with guidance is an alternative.

**Pharmacotherapy** [4]
- **SSRIs** (escitalopram 10–20 mg, sertraline 50–200 mg, paroxetine 20–50 mg, fluvoxamine) or **venlafaxine** 75–225 mg. Standard starting doses are tolerated; benefit by 8 weeks; continue at least 1 year.
- If no response: switch SSRI or SNRI; then **phenelzine** (MAOI, most effective but diet restrictions), moclobemide, pregabalin, gabapentin; clonazepam (short-term or selected cases).
- **Performance-only SAD:** propranolol 10–40 mg 1 hour before the event (for tremor and tachycardia).
- **Combination:** CBT + SSRI for severe or comorbid cases.

### Key evidence
- **Mayo-Wilson 2014 (Lancet Psychiatry)** network meta-analysis (101 trials, 13,164 participants): individual CBT had the largest effect among psychological treatments; SSRIs/SNRIs were effective among drugs; CBT was recommended as first line.
- **Clark 2003 / 2006:** cognitive therapy superior to fluoxetine + self-exposure and to exposure + applied relaxation.
- **Stangier 2011 (SOPHO-NET, n=495):** CBT superior to psychodynamic therapy on remission.

**Conclusion.** SAD is a common, early-onset, chronic and disabling disorder that is often mistaken for shyness. Individual cognitive therapy based on the Clark–Wells model, with safety-behaviour experiments and video feedback, is the treatment of choice. SSRIs or venlafaxine are effective alternatives or adjuncts, continued for at least a year.

## In depth

### Background
Hippocrates described a man who "loves darkness" and fears being seen. Janet (1903) wrote of "phobie des situations sociales". Marks and Gelder (1966) separated social phobia from other phobias. DSM-III (1980) recognised it; DSM-IV added a "generalised" subtype; DSM-5 renamed it social anxiety disorder and replaced "generalised" with a "performance only" specifier. Liebowitz (1985) called it "the neglected anxiety disorder".

### Epidemiology
- Lifetime prevalence 7–13% in Western surveys (NCS-R 12.1%); 12-month about 7%. Lower in Asian countries, including India (~1–2% in community studies) [1].
- Female:male ratio about 1.5:1 in the community; equal in clinics.
- Median onset age 13; 75% before 18; if onset is after 25, consider another diagnosis.
- Only about half ever seek treatment, often after 15–20 years.
- Predicts later depression and alcohol use disorder.

### Classification and criteria

| Feature | ICD-11 6B04 | DSM-5-TR |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Core | Fear of negative evaluation in social situations | Same, out of proportion to the actual threat |
| Duration | At least several months | ≥6 months |
| Avoidance | Avoided or endured with intense anxiety | Same |
| Specifier | — | Performance only |
| Children | — | Anxiety must occur with peers, not only adults |

Avoidant personality disorder (DSM-5) or a personality disorder with prominent detachment and negative affectivity (ICD-11) frequently co-occurs [2].

### Mechanism in detail
- **Cognitive biases:** attention bias to threat (angry faces), interpretation bias (ambiguous social cues seen as negative), memory bias, observer-perspective imagery.
- **Rapee & Heimberg model (1997):** mental representation of the self as seen by the audience compared with perceived standards.
- **Neural:** amygdala and insula hyperactivity to faces; reduced prefrontal regulation; reduced D2/D3 binding in striatum (Schneier 2000); serotonin transporter changes.
- **Development:** behavioural inhibition (Kagan), overprotective or critical parenting, peer victimisation, modelling.
- **Evolutionary:** submissive behaviour to avoid social exclusion.

### Clinical features in detail
- **Physical:** blushing (more specific to SAD), trembling, sweating, nausea, urge to urinate (paruresis).
- **Cognitive:** "they'll think I'm stupid", "my mind will go blank", "they'll see I'm anxious".
- **Behavioural:** safety behaviours (rehearsing, avoiding eye contact, wearing make-up to hide blushing, holding objects); avoidance; alcohol use before events.
- **Subtypes:** generalised (most social situations) vs performance-only (public speaking, eating or writing in public).
- **Course:** chronic without treatment. Personality disorder (avoidant) predicts 39% lower likelihood of remission [2].

### Assessment in detail

| Tool | Detail |
| --- | --- |
| LSAS | 24 situations × fear and avoidance; clinician- or self-rated |
| SPIN | 17 items; screen |
| Mini-SPIN | 3 items |
| Social Phobia Weekly Summary Scale; SCQ | CBT process measures |
| Brief Fear of Negative Evaluation scale | Cognitive core |
| AUDIT | Alcohol |

### Differential diagnosis
See the table in the exam answer. Additional points:
- In **India**, consider dhat syndrome-related social withdrawal, the somatic presentation of anxiety, and cultural norms of modesty.
- **Stuttering or essential tremor** with secondary social anxiety: DSM-5 requires that fear is unrelated to, or excessive for, a medical condition.

### Management details
- **CBT session structure (Clark):** 14 weekly sessions of about 90 minutes plus up to 3 booster sessions; homework; therapist-assisted exposure outside the clinic.
- **SSRIs:** escitalopram and paroxetine have the strongest evidence; sertraline is commonly used. Relapse after stopping is common (one-third to one-half within 3–6 months), so continue for at least 1 year [1,4].
- **Phenelzine:** most effective drug in some meta-analyses, but tyramine diet and interactions limit its use.
- **Benzodiazepines:** clonazepam effective; avoid in alcohol misuse; dependence risk [4].
- **Pregabalin/gabapentin:** second-line.
- **Avoid:** beta-blockers in generalised SAD (ineffective); alcohol as self-medication.
- **Adolescents:** CBT with parental involvement; SSRIs with suicidality monitoring.
- **Comorbid avoidant personality disorder:** longer therapy (schema therapy, longer CBT) [2].

### Critical appraisal
- Effects of CBT are larger vs waiting list than vs active treatments [1].
- **Psychodynamic therapy** is also effective (SOPHO-NET), but less so than CBT on remission [5].
- The boundary between SAD and avoidant personality disorder is unclear (a dimensional view is probably better) [2].
- **Cultural validity:** Western criteria may miss "other-focused" fears (taijin kyofusho). Prevalence is lower in Asian surveys, possibly reflecting measurement differences.
- **Access:** therapist-delivered individual CBT is scarce in India, so iCBT and group CBT are pragmatic options.

### Indian context
- Often unrecognised; presents as "shyness", poor exam or interview performance, or somatic complaints. Students in competitive settings (engineering, medical entrance) are at risk.
- Alcohol and cannabis are used for social confidence.
- College counselling services, Tele-MANAS, and culturally adapted CBT (modesty norms, family involvement) help.
- Fewer psychologists than needed, so psychiatrists use SSRIs plus brief CBT techniques.

### Recent advances
- **Established:** guided internet CBT (Andersson); imagery rescripting.
- **Emerging:** VR exposure; attention bias modification (mixed); MDMA-assisted therapy for autistic social anxiety (small pilot).
- **Investigational:** oxytocin (negative); D-cycloserine-augmented exposure (no clear benefit in Cochrane) [1]; fasedienol (PH94B) nasal spray for acute social anxiety (phase 3; check status).
