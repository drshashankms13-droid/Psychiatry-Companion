## Exam answer

**Definition.** **Vascular depression** is a subtype of late-life depression in which **cerebrovascular disease**, especially **cerebral small vessel disease (cSVD)**, predisposes to, precipitates or perpetuates depressive symptoms. It does so by damaging **frontal–subcortical circuits** (cortico–striato–pallido–thalamo–cortical loops). The concept was proposed in **1997** in two forms:
- **Alexopoulos et al.:** a clinical definition (the "vascular depression hypothesis").
- **Krishnan et al.:** an MRI definition (subcortical hyperintensities) [1].

Note: the seminar abbreviates it "VaD", but in most texts **VaD means vascular dementia**. Write "vascular depression" in full in the exam.

### Mechanism
Vascular risk factors (hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidaemia, smoking, obesity, CKD) → **cSVD**: arteriolosclerosis, endothelial and **blood–brain barrier dysfunction**, ↓ cerebral blood flow → **white matter hyperintensities (WMH)**, lacunes, microbleeds in deep white matter and basal ganglia → **disconnection** of DLPFC–caudate, anterior cingulate and orbitofrontal circuits → **executive dysfunction, apathy, psychomotor slowing and depressed mood** → poor treatment response and progression to cognitive decline [1, 2].

Contributing pathways:
- Neuroinflammation (IL-6, TNF-α).
- AQP4 (glymphatic) genetic variation.
- Amyloid and glial pathology.
- Mesolimbic dopamine changes.

The relationship is **bidirectional**: depression also raises vascular risk [1].

### Clinical features: vascular vs early-onset depression

| Feature | Early-onset depression | Vascular (late-onset) depression |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Onset | Usually before 50 | **Late life (typically >60)**; no prior episodes before cerebrovascular disease |
| Family history of mood disorder | Common | Less common |
| Vascular risk factors / stroke | Low | **High** |
| Core picture | Sadness, guilt, rumination | **Psychomotor retardation, apathy, anhedonia, lack of initiative**, little guilt, **poor insight** |
| Cognition | Mild, state-dependent | **Executive dysfunction, slow processing speed**; reduced fluency, impaired naming |
| Disability | Lower | **Greater IADL impairment** |
| Treatment response | Better | **Poorer, more relapse, chronic** |
| Risk of later dementia | Lower | **Higher** |

Sources: [1].

Related clinical constructs:
- **Depression–executive dysfunction syndrome (DED; Alexopoulos):** psychomotor slowing, reduced interest, poor insight, executive dysfunction, paranoia, IADL impairment, poor response [1].
- **Subcortical ischaemic depression** [1].

### Assessment and diagnostic criteria
- **Clinical criteria** (Alexopoulos 1997; Steffens & Krishnan 1998): depression after age 50–65, or after stroke or vascular disease, with **MRI evidence** of subcortical vascular lesions; cardinal features are executive dysfunction and psychomotor retardation [1].
- **International consensus (Aizenstein et al. 2016)** [1]:
  1. Evidence of vascular pathology, with or without cognitive impairment.
  2. No depressive episodes before obvious cerebrovascular disease.
  3. Cerebrovascular risk factors present.
  4. Depression coincides with cerebrovascular risk factors.
  5. Characteristic symptoms: executive dysfunction, slowed processing, lethargy.
  6. **Neuroimaging confirming cerebrovascular disease.**
- **Investigations:**
  - **MRI with FLAIR/T2:** WMH graded on the **Fazekas** scale (deep white matter 0–3, periventricular 0–3, subcortical grey matter 0–3) [1].
  - Vascular screen: BP, glucose/HbA1c, lipids, ECG, carotid Doppler where indicated.
  - Cognition: MoCA, Trail Making Test, verbal fluency, Stroop.
  - Depression: GDS, HAM-D [1].
- **Nosology:** vascular depression has **no separate category in ICD-11 or DSM-5-TR**. Code as a depressive episode, plus depressive disorder due to another medical condition (cerebrovascular disease) where appropriate [1].

### Management
1. **Treat vascular risk factors:** BP, glucose, lipids, smoking cessation, exercise. This is associated with improved depression and anxiety [1].
2. **Antidepressants:** SSRIs (sertraline, escitalopram) or SNRIs; **vortioxetine** may improve executive and other cognitive function. **Avoid TCAs and MAOIs** (anticholinergic, cardiac and orthostatic effects) [1].
3. **Augmentation if unresponsive:** **aripiprazole** or **lithium**, shown effective in late-life depression unresponsive to antidepressants (Lenze 2015; Cooper 2011) [1]. **Nimodipine** augmentation (Taragano) is an option [1].
4. **Neuromodulation:** ECT (response may be reduced when vascular risk factors are present); **rTMS / deep TMS** (Jorge 2008) and **tDCS** as adjuncts [1].
5. **Psychotherapy:** **problem-solving therapy** (effective in DED), behavioural activation, caregiver support [1].
6. **Adjuncts under study:** statins (protective association), anti-inflammatory agents in those with raised inflammatory markers [1].
7. **Monitor cognition** over time because of the risk of conversion to dementia.

### Key evidence
- **Herrmann 2008, Arnone 2012, Wang 2014** (systematic reviews): WMH, especially frontal and subcortical, are more prevalent in late-life depression and correlate with severity [1].
- A **2.5-fold higher risk of vascular dementia** with late-life depression is reported (Diniz 2013 meta-analysis) [2].
- In the **US population**, the prevalence of vascular depression is about **3.4%** [1].

**Conclusion.** Vascular depression is a useful, biologically plausible construct linking late-life depression to cerebral small vessel disease. It explains the distinctive phenotype of apathy, slowing and executive dysfunction, the poor treatment response and the high risk of dementia. However, it lacks a validated, agreed definition and has no place in ICD-11 or DSM-5-TR. It is best used clinically as a prompt to image, treat vascular risk aggressively, choose cognitively safe antidepressants, and follow up for cognitive decline.

## In depth

### Background
| Year | Concept | Contributor |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1905 | **Atherosclerotic depression**: arteriosclerotic brain disease with aphasia and hemiplegia, progressing to apathetic or euphoric dementia | R. Gaupp [1] |
| 1993 | **Silent cerebral infarcts** in major depression: structural abnormalities affecting mood and executive function without major cognitive deficits | Fujikawa et al. [1] |
| 1997 | **Vascular depression hypothesis**: cerebrovascular disease may predispose, precipitate or perpetuate geriatric depression | Alexopoulos et al. [1] |
| 1997 | **MRI-defined vascular depression**: deep white matter and subcortical lesions, linked to atrophy and treatment resistance | Krishnan et al. [1] |
| 1998 | Diagnostic criteria | Steffens & Krishnan [1] |
| 2000s | **Depression–executive dysfunction syndrome**; **subcortical ischaemic depression** (worse neuropsychological profile, working and non-verbal memory deficits) | Alexopoulos; Potter et al. [1] |
| 2016 | International consensus criteria | Aizenstein et al. [1] |

**Post-stroke depression** affects 20–30% of stroke patients. It depends on lesion location and causes greater disability. It is a related but distinct entity [1].

### Epidemiology
- **US:** population prevalence of vascular depression about 3.4% (depressive episode after age 49 plus more than one vascular risk factor, or stroke) [1].
- **Korean population survey (n=783):** prevalence of major depression **without** MRI-defined vascular disease 2.36%, consistent with a sizeable vascular subgroup [1].
- **Ethnic variation:** overrepresented in African Americans in a multi-ethnic sample, probably reflecting higher rates of cardiovascular disease, hypertension and stroke [1].
- **cSVD burden:** cSVD causes about 20% of all strokes, 25% of ischaemic strokes and **45% of vascular dementias** [1].
- **Imaging prevalence:** up to 90% of people over 65 have some WMH, and at least 30% have silent infarcts [2].
- **Microvascular dysfunction:** recent epidemiological evidence links cerebral microvascular dysfunction to late-onset depression (Empana 2021) [1].
- **Mid-life vascular risk** (hypertension, diabetes, obesity, metabolic syndrome) predicts late-life depression [1].

### Classification and criteria
- **Not in DSM-5-TR or ICD-11** [1].
- **Categorical approach:** Alexopoulos's clinical criteria vs Krishnan's MRI criteria.
- **Dimensional approach:** Sneed et al. proposed vascular depression as the end of a continuum of WMH burden and executive dysfunction, rather than a discrete category.
- **Differences between criteria sets:** whether imaging is mandatory, the age cut-off, and whether executive dysfunction is required.

### Mechanism in detail
**Imaging findings** [1]:
- WMH types: **periventricular**, **deep white matter** and **subcortical grey matter** (caudate, putamen, globus pallidus, thalamus, internal capsule).
- Rated on the Fazekas-modified Coffey scale, the Scheltens scale, or Virchow–Robin space counts.
- **Fazekas grading:**
  - Deep white matter: 0 absent, 1 punctate, 2 beginning confluence, 3 large confluent.
  - Periventricular: 0 absent, 1 caps, 2 smooth halo, 3 irregular, extending into deep white matter.
  - Subcortical grey matter: 0 absent, 1 punctate, 2 multipunctate, 3 diffuse.
- **DTI** is more sensitive than T2 MRI. It shows microstructural changes in the anterior cingulate, superior and middle frontal gyri and right parahippocampal gyrus.
- **fMRI** shows altered anterior cingulate, amygdala and PFC activity.
- **Newer methods:** MR perfusion, magnetisation transfer imaging, T1 mapping (a marker of oedema), and diffusion basis spectrum imaging (which separates axon injury, demyelination and inflammation).

**Pathways** [1, 2]:
1. **Disconnection:** WMH interrupt DLPFC–caudate circuits, causing executive dysfunction and apathy.
2. **Hypoperfusion:** reduced cerebral blood flow and impaired autoregulation.
3. **Endothelial and blood–brain barrier dysfunction:** leakage, perivascular inflammation.
4. **Inflammation:** raised IL-6 and TNF-α (Kalkman 2020; Vogelzangs 2014).
5. **Glymphatic impairment:** AQP4 genetic variation.
6. **Non-vascular contributors:** ageing, amyloid, glial pathology, mesolimbic dopamine.

**Neuropathology:** an ischaemic basis for deep WMH in major depression has been confirmed (Thomas et al. 2002) [2].

### Clinical features in detail
- **Distinctive symptoms** [1]:
  - Psychomotor slowing.
  - Apathy, lack of initiative, loss of energy.
  - Anhedonia.
  - Poor insight.
  - **Alexithymia.**
  - Greater disability; chronic course.
- **Sleep:** more sleep-related breathing disorders, daytime sleepiness and disturbed 24-hour sleep structure [1].
- **Lacunes:** older depressed patients with lacunar infarcts in deep white matter have more "motivational" problems [1].
- **Suicide:** executive impairment (poor cognitive control) raises the risk of suicide attempts [1].
- **Self-management:** patients manage their medical co-morbidities poorly, which worsens outcomes [1].
- **Typical vignette:** a 72-year-old retired teacher with hypertension and diabetes; 10 months of low mood, apathy, fatigue and poor concentration; difficulty managing finances; MRI shows confluent deep WMH. Differentials: mild vascular neurocognitive disorder, or vascular depression [1].

### Assessment in detail

| Tool | Purpose |
| --- | --- |
| GDS-15, HAM-D, MADRS | Depression severity |
| Apathy Evaluation Scale | Apathy, distinct from depression |
| MoCA, Trail Making B, Stroop, verbal fluency, digit symbol | Executive function and processing speed (the MMSE is insensitive) |
| ADL / IADL scales | Disability |
| MRI with FLAIR, Fazekas rating, SWI for microbleeds | Vascular burden |
| Framingham or QRISK, BP, HbA1c, lipids | Vascular risk |
| Polysomnography if indicated | Sleep-disordered breathing |

### Differential diagnosis

| Condition | Distinguishing features |
| --- | --- |
| Early-onset recurrent depression in old age | Past episodes, family history, less executive dysfunction |
| Mild vascular neurocognitive disorder or vascular dementia | Cognitive decline dominates and persists despite mood improvement |
| Apathy syndrome without depression | Apathy without sadness or guilt |
| Post-stroke depression | Follows a clinical stroke |
| Alzheimer's disease with depression | Amnestic profile, hippocampal atrophy, AD biomarkers |
| Parkinson's disease | Motor signs |
| Hypothyroidism, B12 deficiency, OSA | Laboratory tests, polysomnography |
| Medication-induced (beta-blockers, steroids) | Temporal link |

### Management details
- **SSRIs:** start low, go slow. Citalopram is limited to 20 mg/day over 65 years (QTc). Monitor sodium for hyponatraemia. Bleeding risk rises with antiplatelets; consider PPI cover.
- **Vortioxetine** 5–20 mg: evidence for benefit on processing speed and executive function in older adults [1].
- **Augmentation:**
  - Aripiprazole 2–10 mg (Lenze 2015, IRL-GRey: remission 44% vs 29% with placebo in late-life TRD) [1].
  - Lithium at a lower serum target in the elderly (about 0.4–0.6 mmol/L).
- **Nimodipine** (calcium channel blocker): augmenting fluoxetine improved outcomes in vascular depression (Taragano 2001, 2005) [1].
- **rTMS:** left DLPFC rTMS was effective in vascular depression (Jorge 2008). Deep TMS for late-life depression (Kaster 2018) [1].
- **tDCS** added to pharmacotherapy (Zanardi 2020) [1].
- **ECT:** remains effective in severe or psychotic depression. Watch for cognitive adverse effects and post-ECT delirium in patients with a heavy WMH burden.
- **Psychotherapy:** **problem-solving therapy** outperformed supportive therapy in depression with executive dysfunction (Alexopoulos 2011) [1].
- **Vascular prevention:** statins (protective association, Parsaik 2014); BP control; antiplatelets where indicated for vascular disease; exercise [1].
- **Anti-inflammatory approaches:** NSAIDs and cytokine antagonists may help when inflammatory markers are raised (Raison 2017 meta-analysis); investigational [1].

### Critical appraisal
- **Definition problem:** clinical, MRI and dimensional definitions identify different patients [1].
- **WMH are non-specific and ubiquitous in old age:** up to 90% of people over 65 have them [2]. Their precise impact on risk and outcome has not been quantified [1].
- **Causality:** cross-sectional associations. Depression itself (through inflammation, inactivity, smoking and non-adherence) may cause vascular damage. Longitudinal studies (e.g., LADIS) support WMH progression predicting depression [1, 2].
- **Treatment:** few RCTs specifically in vascular depression. Most evidence is extrapolated from late-life depression trials [1]. Studies disagree on whether vascular risk factors reduce ECT response [1].
- **Overlap with mild vascular cognitive impairment and prodromal dementia** blurs the boundary [1].
- **Strength:** the concept gives clear, practical guidance (image, treat risk factors, focus on executive function and apathy).

### Indian context
- India has a rapidly ageing population with a high burden of **hypertension and diabetes**, often undiagnosed. The vascular depression phenotype is therefore likely to be common but under-recognised.
- **LASI wave 1 (2017–18)** found a high prevalence of depressive symptoms in adults over 45, linked to multimorbidity (check exact figures in LASI reports).
- **Services:** NPHCE (geriatric clinics), NP-NCD (BP and diabetes screening), DMHP, Tele-MANAS. The NIMHANS Geriatric Clinic and Services provides specialist care.
- **Cost:** MRI is costly and not always available outside cities. Clinical features plus CT or cardiovascular risk assessment may have to suffice.
- **MHCA 2017:** advance directives and nominated representatives are relevant as cognition declines.

### Recent advances
- **Established:** consensus criteria (2016); recognition of cSVD as a shared substrate for depression, stroke and dementia [1].
- **Emerging:**
  - DTI, perfusion MRI, diffusion basis spectrum imaging [1].
  - Inflammatory and endothelial biomarkers.
  - Vortioxetine for cognition [1].
  - Neuromodulation (deep TMS, tDCS) [1].
  - Personalised medicine approaches [1].
- **Investigational:** anti-inflammatory and vascular-targeted treatments (statins, calcium channel blockers, angiotensin modulators) [1, 2].
