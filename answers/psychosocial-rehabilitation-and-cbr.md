## Exam answer

**Definition.**
- **Psychiatric (psychosocial) rehabilitation (PSR)** "attempts to maximise functioning, while at the same time acknowledging the possibility of relatively fixed disabilities and the necessity of providing supportive environments" (Shepherd 1995). The word comes from the Latin *rehabilitare*, "to make fit again" [1].
- It combines **skill development** (in the person) with **environmental supports** (in the community), so that people with severe mental illness (SMI) can live, learn, work and socialise in the community.
- The **recovery** model complements it: a personal process of building a meaningful life with hope, agency and identity beyond illness [1].
- **Community-based rehabilitation (CBR)** (WHO/ILO/UNESCO, 1994; CBR Guidelines 2010) delivers rehabilitation **in the community through local resources**: families, community workers, self-help groups, local government. Its matrix covers **health, education, livelihood, social and empowerment** domains.

### Mechanism: why rehabilitation works
Illness (positive, negative and cognitive symptoms) → impairments (cognition, social skills) → activity limitations (self-care, work) → participation restriction (unemployment, isolation, stigma).

PSR acts at each level [1]:
- **Medication** reduces symptoms.
- **Skills training and cognitive remediation** reduce impairments.
- **Vocational, housing and family support** reduce activity limitations and participation barriers.
- **Recovery orientation** builds hope and agency.

Cognitive deficits are the **"rate-limiting factor"** for functional recovery. 90% of patients have at least 1 deficit and 75% have at least 2 [1].

### Components of PSR in schizophrenia and SMI [1]

| Component | Description | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Assessment and goal setting** | CASIG (strengths, interests, goals; 60–90 min); short-term goals in the acute phase, long-term goals when stable | Basis for planning [1] |
| **Social skills and problem-solving training** | Liberman's UCLA modules (medication and symptom self-management, workplace fundamentals, basic conversation, recreation, friendship, community re-entry); instructions → video modelling → role-play → problem-solving → in-vivo and homework | Kurtz & Mueser 2008 (22 RCTs): large effect on skills knowledge (d=1.20); moderate on daily living skills and community function (d=0.52); small on relapse (d=0.23) [1] |
| **Family interventions** | Psychoeducation, collegial relationship, "least pathology" attitude, crisis management, community resources | Relapse 6% vs 44% at 6 months, 11% vs 76% at 24 months vs supportive counselling (Dixon 2001). Psychoeducation avoids about 12 relapses per 100 patients per year (Pekkala & Merinder) [1] |
| **Cognitive remediation (CRT)** | Drill-and-practice and strategy training (Delahunty & Morice 1993), errorless learning | Moderate effect on cognition; larger functional gains when combined with rehabilitation (Wykes 2011) [1] |
| **CBT for psychosis** | Alternative explanations, reality testing | Small–moderate effects on positive and negative symptoms (Wykes 2008, 34 RCTs) [1] |
| **Vocational rehabilitation** | Sheltered work → transitional employment → **supported employment (IPS)** | IPS best validated: 50–60% obtain jobs, but about half leave within 6 months [1] |
| **Housing** | Halfway homes (Richmond Fellowship), long-stay homes, supported housing, "ward-in-a-house" | Supported housing reduces homelessness [1] |
| **Day care / day hospitals** | Acute day hospital as an alternative to admission; transitional day hospital | Mixed Cochrane evidence [1] |
| **Clubhouses and self-help** | Fountain House model; peer-run; members, not patients | Non-RCT: re-hospitalisation 37% vs 77% with peer outreach [1] |
| **Case management / ACT** | 24/7 multidisciplinary team; low caseload | Reduces admissions; improves housing and engagement [1] |
| **Other** | Sex education, physical health, Morita therapy, dance and movement therapy | Limited [1] |

### Community-based rehabilitation models
- **WHO CBR matrix:**
  - **Health:** promotion, prevention, medical care, rehabilitation, assistive devices.
  - **Education:** early childhood to lifelong.
  - **Livelihood:** skills, self-employment, wage employment, financial services, social protection.
  - **Social:** relationships, culture, leisure, justice.
  - **Empowerment:** advocacy, community mobilisation, political participation, **self-help groups**, DPOs.
- **Indian CBR approaches:**
  - **DMHP-linked CBR:** PHC follow-up; Manasadhara day care (Karnataka) [3].
  - **NGO models:** The Banyan (Chennai), SCARF (Chennai), Richmond Fellowship Society India (Bengaluru), Ashadeep (Assam), BasicNeeds (livelihood + community mental health).
  - **Family-based CBR:** in Indian settings, home-based care by families, supported by community workers.
  - **Lay-health-worker models:** **COPSI trial** (community care for people with schizophrenia in India): collaborative community-based care plus facility-based care slightly improved disability and symptoms compared with facility-based care alone (Chatterjee 2014).
  - **Rural community psychiatry:** NIMHANS outreach (Thirthahalli, Turuvekere); taluk mental health programme [3].

### Assessment for rehabilitation
- Symptoms (PANSS), cognition (MoCA, MATRICS), function (**IDEAS**, GAF, PSP, WHODAS 2.0), skills (CASIG, ILS, UPSA), family burden (Burden Assessment Schedule), needs (CAN), and quality of life [1].
- Disability certification for benefits (≥40%) [4].

### Management: principles of PSR [1]
- Most helpful **when the patient is in reasonable remission**; the critical period is aftercare.
- A **long-term** approach: benefits are rarely apparent before about 12 months.
- The relationship with the psychiatrist or case manager is central.
- Person-centred, strengths-based, hope-giving and **recovery-oriented** (SAMHSA 10 components: self-direction, individualised, empowerment, holistic, non-linear, strengths-based, peer support, respect, responsibility, hope).

**Conclusion.** Psychosocial rehabilitation turns symptom control into **functional recovery**. It combines skills training, family work, cognitive remediation, vocational and housing supports and case management. **CBR** extends this into the community using local resources, which suits India, where families provide most care and specialists are scarce. Evidence is strongest for family psychoeducation, supported employment, social skills training and ACT. Indian outcome data (ISoS; COPSI) show recovery is common and community models are feasible. Scaling up requires integration with DMHP, disability benefits and NGOs.

## In depth

### Background
- **Kraepelin's** pessimism ("dementia praecox"); **Bleuler's** "healing with scarring" [1].
- **1950s–60s:** antipsychotics and **deinstitutionalisation**. People with SMI in the community needed skills and supports, so the focus shifted from treatment to rehabilitation [1].
- **1970s:** psychiatric rehabilitation was defined (Anthony; Liberman's skills training).
- **1970s–90s:** consumer movements (Insane Liberation Front 1970; Mental Patients' Liberation Projects 1971) led to the **recovery vision of the 1990s** (Anthony 1993) [1]. President's New Freedom Commission (2003): recovery as "the single most important goal".
- **CBR:** WHO (1978 Alma Ata; 1994 joint position paper; 2010 CBR guidelines with a mental health supplement).

### Epidemiology
- **Long-term outcome:**
  - 20–65% of people with schizophrenia eventually do relatively well (Jobe & Harrow 2005; Hopper 2007).
  - Warner's meta-analysis: about **20% complete recovery and 40% social recovery** [1].
- **ISoS** (over 1,000 patients, 16 centres, 12–26-year follow-up): better outcomes in developing countries [1]:
  - In Agra, 60% had no long-term disability.
  - In Chandigarh, 54% were in paid employment.
  - About three-quarters of Indian patients were married, compared with about a third in developed centres.
- **Employment:** under 20% of people with schizophrenia work, although 55–70% want to [1].

### Classification and criteria: recovery definitions
- **Operational (Liberman 2002):** for 2 years, BPRS positive and negative items ≤4; work or school at least half-time; independent management of daily needs; cordial family relations; recreation; satisfying peer relationships [1].
- **Consumer-based:** a fulfilling, meaningful life and positive identity founded on hope and self-determination (Andreasen 2003) [1].
- **Stage model** (Andresen 2003): moratorium → awareness → preparation → rebuilding → growth [1].

### Mechanism in detail
- **Skills training:** operant and social learning; generalisation needs in-vivo practice and homework [1].
- **Family intervention:** reduces **expressed emotion** (criticism, hostility, emotional over-involvement), which predicts relapse.
- **CRT:** neuroplasticity; strategy learning; transfer to function improves when CRT is embedded in rehabilitation.
- **IPS:** real-world learning plus continuous support counters cognitive and motivational deficits.

### Clinical features in detail: predictors of recovery [1]
- **Favourable:** good family relationships, short duration of untreated psychosis, good initial medication response, adherence, supportive therapy, better cognition and social skills, access to care.
- **Unfavourable:** substance abuse, prominent negative symptoms.
- **ISoS:** the **first 2 years** best predict the long-term course, yet a fifth of those with a poor early course recover later [1].

### Assessment in detail: instruments [1]
| Tool | Measures |
| --- | --- |
| CASIG | Skills, quality of life, symptoms, side effects, behaviour |
| UPSA | Performance-based functional capacity |
| SSPA | Social skill performance |
| MMAA | Medication management ability |
| ILS | Independent living |
| GAF / PSP | Global functioning |
| IDEAS | Indian disability (certification) [4] |

### Differential: rehabilitation settings
| Setting | Role |
| --- | --- |
| Inpatient rehabilitation ward | Long-stay patients; skills; discharge preparation |
| Day care centre | Structured day; skills; respite for carers |
| Halfway home (maximum about 1 year) | Transition from hospital [1] |
| Long-stay home / sheltered housing | Chronic disability |
| Supported housing | Independent living with support |
| Clubhouse | Peer-run, work-ordered day |
| Home-based care / CBR | Family-centred, community workers |

### Management details: building a PSR plan
1. Engage the patient and family (the first step) [1].
2. Assess strengths, deficits and goals (CASIG); set phase-appropriate goals [1].
3. Optimise medication (clozapine if refractory; minimise sedation and EPS).
4. Psychoeducation and family intervention.
5. Skills training (medication and symptom management first), plus cognitive remediation.
6. Vocational plan (IPS; government reservation through disability certification).
7. Housing and financial entitlements (pension, UDID, DDRS-funded NGOs).
8. Peer support and self-help groups.
9. Physical health (metabolic monitoring).
10. Relapse plan and continuity (case manager or ACT).

### Evidence table
| Study | Design | Result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Kurtz & Mueser 2008 (J Consult Clin Psychol) | Meta-analysis, 22 RCTs (social skills training) | d=1.20 knowledge; 0.52 function; 0.23 relapse [1] |
| Pekkala & Merinder 2002 (Cochrane) | Psychoeducation | About 12 relapses avoided per 100 per year [1] |
| Wykes et al. 2011 (Am J Psychiatry) | Meta-analysis, CRT (40 studies) | Cognition d about 0.45; function d about 0.42, better with rehabilitation |
| Bond et al. 2008 | IPS review | 25–50% enrolled in SE do not work at 1–2 years [1] |
| Hopper et al. 2007 (ISoS) | International cohort | Better outcomes in developing countries [1] |
| Chatterjee et al. 2014 (Lancet), COPSI | RCT, India (n=282) | Community care improved disability modestly |
| Tungpunkom & Nicol 2008 (Cochrane) | Life skills, 4 RCTs | No significant benefit; underpowered [1] |

### Critical appraisal
- **Evidence is uneven:** strongest for family intervention and IPS; mixed for day care, life skills and clubhouses (few RCTs) [1].
- **Generalisation:** skills learned in sessions often fail to transfer without in-vivo practice.
- **Western models in India:** ACT and IPS need adaptation to informal economies, family-centred care and scarce staff. CBR and lay-worker models fit better.
- **"Better outcome in developing countries" debate:** Cohen et al. 2008 challenged it (mortality, chronicity). Critics replied that their sample overrepresented chronic cases [1].
- **Funding and workforce:** rehabilitation is chronically underfunded. MHCA 2017 promises rehabilitation as a right (Section 18), but services lag.

### Indian context
- **MHCA 2017:**
  - Section 18: right to community-based rehabilitation, halfway homes, sheltered and supported accommodation.
  - Section 19: right to community living.
- **RPwD Act 2016:** reservations; DDRS grants; UDID [4].
- **DMHP:** day care centres, long-term residential continuing care (PPP); Karnataka's Manasadhara (14 districts, 1,493 beneficiaries) and Manasakendra halfway homes [3].
- **NGOs:** Richmond Fellowship (halfway homes; the first was founded in Richmond, UK, by Elly Jansen) [1]; The Banyan; SCARF; MPA; Anjali.
- **Families:** high involvement and low criticism are protective factors in Indian outcome data [1].

### Recent advances
- **Established:** IPS; family psychoeducation; recovery-oriented services; CBR guidelines.
- **Emerging:**
  - Digital cognitive remediation; virtual-reality social skills training.
  - **Peer support workers.**
  - Recovery colleges.
  - "Housing First".
  - Tele-rehabilitation (Tele-MANAS linkages).
  - Lay-counsellor task-sharing (COPSI, SUNDAR-type models).
