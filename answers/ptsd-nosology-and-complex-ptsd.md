## Exam answer

> **How to use this answer:** for "Nosology of PTSD" write the history, the ICD-10 / DSM-5 / ICD-11 comparison and the critical points. For "Complex PTSD" focus on the complex PTSD section, its differential diagnosis and treatment, with a brief note on how it arose from PTSD nosology.

**Definition.** Post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) is a disorder that may follow exposure to an extremely threatening or horrific event, characterised by re-experiencing the event in the present, avoidance of reminders and a persistent sense of current threat [1]. Its **nosology** — how it is defined and where it sits in classification — has changed more than almost any other diagnosis since it entered DSM-III in **1980**.

### Historical evolution

| Period | Concept |
| --- | --- |
| 19th century | "Railway spine" (Erichsen), "irritable heart"/soldier's heart (Da Costa) |
| World War I–II | Shell shock, war neurosis, combat fatigue |
| 1952–1968 | DSM-I "gross stress reaction" (transient); DSM-II dropped it |
| **1980** | **DSM-III introduces PTSD** among anxiety disorders, driven by Vietnam veterans and survivors of rape and disasters |
| 1992 | **ICD-10 F43.1** PTSD within "reactions to severe stress and adjustment disorders"; F62.0 enduring personality change after catastrophic experience |
| 1994 | DSM-IV adds the subjective Criterion A2 (fear, helplessness, horror); "disorders of extreme stress not otherwise specified" (DESNOS) in appendix |
| **2013** | **DSM-5** moves PTSD to a new chapter, "**trauma- and stressor-related disorders**"; 4 clusters; A2 removed; dissociative subtype |
| **2019/2022** | **ICD-11** narrows PTSD to 3 core features and adds **complex PTSD** and **prolonged grief disorder** in "**disorders specifically associated with stress**" [1, 2] |

(History before ICD-11 from the author's knowledge.)

### Current definitions compared

| Feature | ICD-10 | DSM-5 | ICD-11 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Grouping | Reactions to severe stress and adjustment disorders | Trauma- and stressor-related disorders | **Disorders specifically associated with stress** [1] |
| Stressor | Exceptionally threatening or catastrophic | Actual or threatened death, serious injury or sexual violence (direct, witnessed, learned, repeated professional exposure) | Extremely threatening or horrific event(s) [1] |
| Symptoms | Re-experiencing, avoidance, arousal or amnesia; numbing common | **4 clusters, 20 symptoms**: intrusion, avoidance, negative cognitions and mood, arousal/reactivity | **3 core features**: re-experiencing **in the here and now**, avoidance, **persistent sense of current threat** [1, 2] |
| Onset/duration | Usually within 6 months | >1 month; delayed expression if ≥6 months | At least several weeks [2] |
| Impairment | Not required | Required | Required [1] |
| Complex form | Enduring personality change after catastrophic experience | Dissociative subtype only | **Complex PTSD** [1] |
| Acute reaction | Acute stress reaction (a disorder) | Acute stress disorder (a disorder) | **Acute stress reaction is not a mental disorder** (coded as a factor influencing health status) [1] |

**Why ICD-11 narrowed PTSD:** the ICD-10 threshold was low (memories alone could count as re-experiencing). Requiring re-experiencing **in the present** with fear or horror, and reducing symptoms to a core set, improves specificity and clinical utility; DSM-5's 20 symptoms allow over 600,000 symptom combinations (author's note) [1].

### Complex PTSD (ICD-11, 6B41)

**Stressor:** extreme or **prolonged/repeated** events from which **escape is difficult or impossible** — torture, slavery, genocide, prolonged domestic violence, repeated childhood sexual or physical abuse, child soldiering, imprisonment [1, 2].

**Features:** all three **core PTSD features** plus **disturbances in self-organisation (DSO)** [1]:
1. **Affect dysregulation** — heightened reactivity, violent outbursts, reckless or self-destructive behaviour, or dissociation and emotional numbing
2. **Negative self-concept** — persistent beliefs of being diminished, defeated or worthless, with shame or guilt
3. **Disturbances in relationships** — difficulty sustaining relationships and feeling close to others

**Rationale for inclusion:** people with complex PTSD have a **poorer prognosis** and benefit from **different treatments** than those with PTSD [1]. It replaces ICD-10 **enduring personality change after catastrophic experience** and draws on Judith **Herman's (1992)** concept and DSM-IV's DESNOS [1, 2].

| Complex PTSD vs borderline personality disorder | Complex PTSD | BPD |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Trauma | Required | Common but not required |
| Self-concept | Consistently negative | Unstable, shifting |
| Relationships | Avoidance, distance | Intense, unstable; fear of abandonment |
| Self-harm | Less central | Frequent, impulsive |
| PTSD core symptoms | Present | Not required |

(Distinction from Cloitre's research; author's knowledge.)

### Management in brief

- **PTSD:** trauma-focused psychological therapies first line — **trauma-focused CBT (prolonged exposure, cognitive processing therapy)** and **EMDR**; SSRIs (sertraline, paroxetine) or venlafaxine when therapy is unavailable or as adjuncts; prazosin for nightmares (mixed evidence); avoid benzodiazepines.
- **Complex PTSD:** phase-based or multicomponent approach — stabilisation and skills (emotion regulation, e.g. **STAIR**, DBT skills) → trauma processing → reintegration; address safety, relationships and self-concept; longer treatment.

(Management from the author's knowledge, consistent with NICE 2018 and ISTSS guidance.)

### Critical points

- PTSD is the only major diagnosis (besides adjustment disorder) that requires a specific cause — this has made the definition of the **stressor criterion** ("criterion creep") controversial.
- **DSM-5 and ICD-11 now diverge** markedly: DSM-5 is broad, ICD-11 narrow; prevalence estimates differ by system.
- Complex PTSD has good factor-analytic support (distinct from PTSD and BPD), but DSM-5 has not adopted it.

**Conclusion.** PTSD's nosology has moved from anxiety disorder (DSM-III/IV) to a stress-specific grouping, and from a broad symptom list to a focused core in ICD-11. ICD-11's addition of complex PTSD recognises that prolonged, inescapable trauma produces a distinct syndrome — PTSD plus disturbances of affect, self-concept and relationships — with different prognosis and treatment needs.

## In depth

### Background — the grouping "disorders specifically associated with stress"

- ICD-11 groups disorders that share the **necessary (but not sufficient)** requirement of exposure to a stressor, distinguishing them from other disorders that can arise as reactions to stress (e.g. depression) [1].
- Members: PTSD, complex PTSD, **prolonged grief disorder**, **adjustment disorder**, **reactive attachment disorder**, **disinhibited social engagement disorder**; acute stress reaction is cross-listed but is not a disorder [1, 2].
- The stressor can range from normal life events (adjustment disorder, prolonged grief) to exceptional trauma (PTSD, complex PTSD) [2].

### Classification and criteria in detail

- **ICD-11 adjustment disorder** is defined positively — **preoccupation with the stressor** (excessive worry, rumination) and **failure to adapt** — and ICD-10 subtypes are removed; in ICD-10 it was essentially a residual category [1, 2].
- **Prolonged grief disorder:** persistent longing for, or preoccupation with, the deceased with intense emotional pain, for **more than 6 months** after the loss, clearly exceeding social, cultural or religious norms [1].
- **Complex PTSD** history: Herman's "complex PTSD" (1992) → DSM-IV field trial of DESNOS (not adopted) → ICD-10 F62.0 enduring personality change → ICD-11 complex PTSD [2].

### Mechanism in detail

- **Fear-conditioning model:** trauma → conditioned fear to reminders → avoidance prevents extinction → persistent threat appraisal.
- **Neurobiology:** amygdala hyper-reactivity, reduced medial prefrontal regulation, hippocampal changes; HPA-axis alterations (low or normal cortisol with enhanced feedback).
- **Cognitive model (Ehlers and Clark):** poorly elaborated trauma memories (re-experienced as "now") plus negative appraisals produce a sense of current threat — the basis for ICD-11's "here and now" requirement.
- **Complex PTSD:** early, repeated interpersonal trauma disrupts attachment and the development of emotion regulation and self-concept.

### Epidemiology

- Lifetime PTSD prevalence varies widely by country and trauma exposure (roughly 1–9% in community surveys); higher in women, conflict-affected and refugee populations.
- Complex PTSD prevalence in community samples is about 1–8%, and higher than PTSD in clinical samples of people with childhood abuse.
- ICD-11 PTSD prevalence tends to be lower than DSM-5 PTSD because of narrower criteria.

### Assessment

| Tool | Use |
| --- | --- |
| **International Trauma Questionnaire (ITQ)** | ICD-11 PTSD and complex PTSD (self-report) |
| CAPS-5 | Clinician-administered DSM-5 PTSD |
| PCL-5 | DSM-5 PTSD self-report |
| Life Events Checklist | Trauma exposure |
| Dissociative Experiences Scale | Dissociation |

### Differential diagnosis

| Condition | Distinguishing point |
| --- | --- |
| Adjustment disorder | Stressor need not be extreme; preoccupation rather than re-experiencing [1] |
| Prolonged grief disorder | Longing for the deceased, not threat [1] |
| Depression | Pervasive low mood without re-experiencing |
| BPD | Unstable self-image, abandonment fear; trauma not required |
| Dissociative disorders | Amnesia, identity disturbance |
| Psychosis | Flashbacks are memory-based; hallucinations are not |
| Post-concussional syndrome | Head injury, cognitive symptoms |

### Critical appraisal

- **ICD-11's narrower PTSD** improves specificity but may miss people with marked dysphoric or numbing symptoms who would meet DSM-5.
- **Complex PTSD vs BPD overlap:** latent class studies support distinct groups, but many patients meet both; clinicians risk labelling trauma survivors with BPD — or the reverse.
- **Treatment evidence for complex PTSD** is still developing; whether phase-based treatment is better than direct trauma-focused therapy is debated.
- **Cultural validity:** idioms of distress after trauma vary; ICD-11's culture-related features help but rely on limited cross-cultural data.

### Indian context

- Trauma exposure in India includes disasters (tsunami 2004, floods, earthquakes), communal and political violence, conflict in Kashmir and the North-East, domestic violence, child sexual abuse and road traffic accidents.
- **NIMHANS psychosocial support in disaster management** programmes and the **National Disaster Management Authority guidelines** on psychosocial support emphasise community-based care.
- Help-seeking is often somatic; screening should include trauma history in people with chronic pain, dissociative symptoms or self-harm.
- **POCSO Act 2012** and the **Protection of Women from Domestic Violence Act 2005** are relevant when trauma is ongoing — safety comes first.

### Recent advances

- ICD-11 CDDR published (2024); ITQ validated in several languages.
- Brief, scalable trauma-focused interventions (e.g. Problem Management Plus, Narrative Exposure Therapy) for low-resource and humanitarian settings.
- MDMA-assisted psychotherapy for PTSD — positive phase 3 trials but **FDA declined approval in 2024**; remains investigational.
