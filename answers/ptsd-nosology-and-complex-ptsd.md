## Exam answer

**Definition.** Post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) is a disorder that follows exposure to an extremely threatening or horrific event and is marked by re-experiencing the event in the present, avoidance of reminders and a persistent sense of current threat. **Complex PTSD**, new in ICD-11, adds pervasive **disturbances in self-organisation**: affect dysregulation, negative self-concept and relational difficulties, usually after prolonged or repeated trauma from which escape is difficult or impossible [1]. Nosology is the study of how such a condition is defined and placed in classification, and PTSD shows how this has changed with each revision.

### Historical evolution of the concept

| Era | Concept |
| --- | --- |
| 19th century | "Railway spine" (Erichsen), traumatic neurosis (Oppenheim), hysteria after trauma (Charcot, Janet) |
| First World War | "Shell shock", war neurosis |
| Second World War | "Combat fatigue"; concentration camp syndrome |
| DSM-I (1952) | Gross stress reaction |
| DSM-II (1968) | Adjustment reaction of adult life (concept nearly lost) |
| **DSM-III (1980)** | **PTSD introduced** after Vietnam veterans' advocacy; placed among anxiety disorders |
| ICD-10 (1992) | F43.1 PTSD in "reaction to severe stress and adjustment disorders"; F62.0 enduring personality change after catastrophic experience |
| DSM-IV (1994) | Criterion A2 (fear, helplessness, horror); acute stress disorder added |
| **DSM-5 (2013)** | Moved to new chapter **Trauma- and stressor-related disorders**; A2 dropped; four clusters; dissociative subtype |
| **ICD-11 (2019)** | **Disorders specifically associated with stress**; PTSD narrowed to 3 core features; **complex PTSD** and prolonged grief disorder added [1] |

Herman (1992) proposed "complex PTSD" for survivors of prolonged interpersonal trauma; DSM-IV field trials studied it as "disorders of extreme stress not otherwise specified" (DESNOS), which remained in DSM-IV's appendix [2].

### Placement in the classification

- **ICD-11** groups disorders whose **necessary (but not sufficient) cause is a stressor**: PTSD, complex PTSD, prolonged grief disorder, adjustment disorder, reactive attachment disorder and disinhibited social engagement disorder [1]. This separates them from disorders that merely follow stress (e.g. depression).
- **Acute stress reaction** is no longer a mental disorder in ICD-11; it is classed as a normal reaction in "factors influencing health status", cross-listed for differential diagnosis [1].
- **DSM-5** uses "trauma- and stressor-related disorders", which includes acute stress disorder as a disorder.

### Diagnostic comparison

| Feature | ICD-10 | DSM-5 / DSM-5-TR | ICD-11 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Stressor | Exceptionally threatening or catastrophic | Exposure to actual or threatened death, serious injury or sexual violence (direct, witnessed, learning of it in close others, repeated professional exposure) | Extremely threatening or horrific event or series of events |
| Symptom structure | Re-experiencing + avoidance (+ arousal or amnesia) | **4 clusters, 20 symptoms**: intrusion (B), avoidance (C), negative alterations in cognition and mood (D), arousal and reactivity (E) | **3 core features**: re-experiencing **in the present**, avoidance, persistent perception of heightened current threat [1] |
| Threshold | Low; remembering the event could count | Many symptom combinations (over 600,000) | Higher; flashbacks or nightmares with a sense of reliving required [1] |
| Duration | Onset usually within 6 months | More than 1 month | At least several weeks [2] |
| Impairment | Not explicit | Required | Required [1] |
| Subtypes/specifiers | – | Dissociative subtype; delayed expression | – |
| Related disorder | Enduring personality change after catastrophic experience | None (DESNOS not adopted) | **Complex PTSD** replaces enduring personality change [1] |

### Complex PTSD (ICD-11 6B41)

**Typical stressors:** torture, slavery, genocide, prolonged domestic violence, repeated childhood sexual or physical abuse, child soldiers, trafficking, imprisonment [1, 2].

**Requirements:** all three PTSD core features **plus** severe, persistent **disturbances in self-organisation (DSO)** [1]:

| DSO domain | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| Affect dysregulation | Heightened emotional reactivity, violent outbursts, reckless or self-destructive behaviour, or dissociation and emotional numbing |
| Negative self-concept | Persistent beliefs of being diminished, defeated or worthless, with shame, guilt or failure related to the trauma |
| Disturbed relationships | Difficulty sustaining relationships and feeling close to others; avoidance or deriding of relationships |

**Rationale for inclusion:** people with complex PTSD have a **poorer prognosis** and **benefit from different treatments** than those with PTSD [1].

### Complex PTSD versus borderline personality disorder

| Feature | Complex PTSD | Borderline personality disorder |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Trauma | Required | Not required (common) |
| Self-concept | Stably negative | Unstable identity |
| Relationships | Avoidant, distant | Intense, unstable, idealisation–devaluation |
| Fear of abandonment | Not characteristic | Core feature |
| Self-harm, suicidality | Possible | Frequent, recurrent |

### Assessment

- **International Trauma Questionnaire (ITQ)** for ICD-11 PTSD and complex PTSD; **CAPS-5** and **PCL-5** for DSM-5 PTSD.
- Screen for depression, substance use, suicidality, dissociation and ongoing danger.

### Treatment implications of the nosology

- **PTSD:** trauma-focused psychotherapy first line (trauma-focused CBT, cognitive processing therapy, prolonged exposure, EMDR); SSRIs (sertraline, paroxetine) or venlafaxine when therapy is unavailable or declined.
- **Complex PTSD:** phase-based or modular treatment: stabilisation and emotion-regulation skills (e.g. STAIR), then trauma processing, then reintegration; address safety and relationships. Recent trials suggest trauma-focused therapy can often begin without a prolonged stabilisation phase.

### Conclusion

PTSD has moved from an anxiety disorder (DSM-III) to a stressor-defined grouping (DSM-5, ICD-11). ICD-11 deliberately narrowed PTSD to three core features to lift the low ICD-10 threshold and added complex PTSD to capture survivors of prolonged trauma whose self-organisation is damaged. DSM-5 chose breadth (20 symptoms, dissociative subtype) instead, so the two systems now diverge on threshold and on the existence of complex PTSD, a divergence with direct consequences for prognosis and treatment planning.

## In depth

### Background

- Kardiner described "traumatic neuroses of war" (1941). Lifton and Shatan's work with Vietnam veterans and feminist research on rape trauma syndrome (Burgess and Holmstrom, 1974) shaped DSM-III's PTSD.
- Herman's *Trauma and Recovery* (1992) argued that a single-event PTSD model missed the effects of captivity and chronic abuse. DSM-IV field trials of DESNOS found it usually co-occurred with PTSD, and it was not adopted.
- ICD-10 F62.0 required a personality change lasting at least two years after catastrophic stress; it was rarely used and poorly validated, and ICD-11 replaced it with complex PTSD [1].

### Epidemiology

- Lifetime trauma exposure is common (over 70% in World Mental Health surveys), but only a minority develop PTSD; lifetime PTSD prevalence is about 4% across countries and higher in conflict-affected populations.
- Risk is highest after interpersonal violence (rape, torture) and lower after accidents or natural disasters.
- ICD-11 PTSD identifies fewer cases than DSM-5 PTSD in most comparison studies, but the cases it identifies are similarly impaired; complex PTSD is often more common than PTSD alone in clinical trauma samples.
- In India, studies after the 2004 tsunami, the Gujarat earthquake, communal violence and in Kashmir report substantial PTSD rates, especially in women and children.

(Epidemiological figures from the author's knowledge of the World Mental Health surveys and ICD-11 validation studies.)

### Classification and criteria in detail

**DSM-5 PTSD (summary):** Criterion A exposure; B intrusion (1 of 5: memories, nightmares, flashbacks, distress at cues, physiological reactivity); C avoidance (1 of 2); D negative alterations in cognition and mood (2 of 7: amnesia, negative beliefs, distorted blame, persistent negative emotion, diminished interest, detachment, inability to feel positive emotions); E arousal (2 of 6: irritability or aggression, recklessness, hypervigilance, startle, poor concentration, sleep disturbance); F duration over 1 month; G distress or impairment; H not due to substance or medical condition. Specifiers: dissociative (depersonalisation or derealisation), delayed expression (full criteria at least 6 months after the event). Separate criteria for children aged 6 or under.

**ICD-11 design logic:** the working group removed symptoms shared with depression and anxiety (sleep, concentration, irritability, diminished interest) to reduce comorbidity and focus on trauma-specific features [1]. Requiring re-experiencing in the present (flashbacks, vivid intrusions, nightmares with re-living) addresses the low ICD-10 threshold [1].

**Adjustment disorder in ICD-11:** defined by preoccupation with the stressor and failure to adapt, no longer a residual category; ICD-10 subtypes removed [1, 2].

**Prolonged grief disorder:** grief beyond 6 months, exceeding cultural norms, with longing or preoccupation and intense emotional pain [1].

### Mechanism in detail

- **Fear conditioning and failed extinction:** trauma cues become conditioned stimuli; avoidance prevents extinction learning.
- **Neurocircuitry:** hyperactive amygdala, hypoactive ventromedial prefrontal cortex and reduced hippocampal volume, explaining exaggerated threat responses, poor contextual memory and failure to inhibit fear.
- **HPA axis:** lower baseline cortisol with enhanced negative feedback in many studies.
- **Cognitive model (Ehlers and Clark):** a sense of current threat arises from negative appraisals of the trauma and a poorly elaborated, poorly contextualised trauma memory; this maps directly onto ICD-11's "sense of current threat" feature.
- **Complex PTSD:** prolonged trauma during development disrupts attachment, emotion regulation and self-concept, explaining the DSO domains.

### Clinical features in detail

- Re-experiencing in ICD-11 means the event is relived as happening now, with fear or horror and strong bodily sensations, not just remembered [1].
- Avoidance may be internal (thoughts, memories) or external (places, people, activities).
- Heightened current threat: hypervigilance, exaggerated startle, scanning for danger.
- Associated features: depression, substance misuse, somatic symptoms, guilt, shame, suicidal ideation, dissociation.
- Children may show traumatic play, regression and repetitive re-enactment.

### Differential diagnosis

| Condition | Distinguishing feature |
| --- | --- |
| Acute stress reaction | Transient, within days to about a month; normal reaction in ICD-11 [1] |
| Adjustment disorder | Stressor not necessarily traumatic; preoccupation and failure to adapt [1] |
| Depression | Low mood and anhedonia dominate; no re-experiencing |
| Prolonged grief disorder | Yearning for the deceased rather than fear and threat [1] |
| Borderline personality disorder | Unstable self and relationships, abandonment fears |
| Psychotic disorder | Hallucinations not tied to trauma memories; other psychotic features |
| Dissociative disorders | Dissociation without the PTSD core |
| Post-concussional syndrome | Head injury, cognitive symptoms |

### Management details

| Intervention | Notes |
| --- | --- |
| Trauma-focused CBT, cognitive processing therapy, prolonged exposure | Strongest evidence; 8–16 sessions |
| EMDR | Comparable efficacy to trauma-focused CBT |
| Narrative exposure therapy | Useful for refugees and multiple trauma |
| STAIR + narrative therapy (Cloitre) | Developed for complex PTSD |
| SSRIs (sertraline, paroxetine), venlafaxine | Moderate effect; if psychotherapy unavailable or declined |
| Prazosin | Nightmares; evidence mixed |
| Benzodiazepines | Avoid; may impair extinction learning |
| Psychological debriefing immediately after trauma | Not recommended; may increase PTSD risk |

### Critical appraisal

- **Breadth versus specificity:** DSM-5's polythetic structure produces great heterogeneity (two people can share no symptoms); ICD-11's narrow definition reduces heterogeneity but may miss impaired cases.
- **Validity of complex PTSD:** latent class analyses using the ITQ support distinct PTSD and complex PTSD groups; critics argue it overlaps with borderline personality disorder and comorbid depression.
- **Criterion A debates:** whether indirect exposure (media) or non-life-threatening events (bullying, racism) should count remains contested.
- **Medicalisation:** defining normal distress after disasters as disorder may divert resources from social support; ICD-11's reclassification of acute stress reaction as normal reflects this concern [1].

### Indian context

- Mass trauma (disasters, terrorism, communal violence, conflict in Kashmir and the north-east), road traffic accidents and gender-based violence are major causes.
- Somatic presentation and religious or fatalistic explanations are common; stigma limits help seeking.
- The **NDMA guidelines on psychosocial support and mental health services in disasters (2009)** emphasise community-level psychosocial first aid and referral.
- NIMHANS psychosocial support teams and Tele-MANAS provide post-disaster care; trained therapists for trauma-focused therapy remain scarce.

### Recent advances

- ITQ validation across countries supports the ICD-11 PTSD and complex PTSD distinction.
- **MDMA-assisted therapy** for PTSD showed benefit in phase 3 trials, but the US FDA declined approval in 2024 because of methodological concerns.
- Brief written exposure therapy and internet-delivered trauma-focused CBT broaden access.
- Stellate ganglion block and ketamine are under investigation.
