## Exam answer

### Introduction [1, 2]
- Most patients presenting with headache have **migraine or TTH** (high lifetime prevalence) [10].
- Diagnosis rests on **ICHD-3** after excluding secondary causes. **Misdiagnosis** is a common cause of treatment failure [1].

### Classification (ICHD-3 framework as reflected in the sources)

**Part 1: Primary headaches**

| Group | Members and key points |
| --- | --- |
| **1. Migraine** | Without aura; with aura (visual, sensory, language, motor, **brainstem**; "migraine with brainstem aura" replaces "basilar migraine"; **hemiplegic** migraine); **chronic migraine** (15 or more headache days a month, 8 or more of them migrainous) [1, 2, 4] |
| **2. Tension-type headache** | "Featureless" headache (no photophobia, phonophobia, nausea or vomiting in the ICHD-3 appendix description cited for children). Common in the population, but rarely the reason for clinic attendance: in one primary-care adult study only 3% had TTH and 94% had migraine or probable migraine [9] |
| **3. Trigeminal autonomic cephalalgias (TACs)** | **Cluster headache**, **paroxysmal hemicrania**, **SUNCT**, **SUNA**, **hemicrania continua** (moved here in ICHD-3 from "other primary"). **Chronic cluster** now means remission of **less than 3 months** (formerly less than 1 month) [5] |
| **4. Other primary headaches** | **Primary cough**, **exercise**, **sexual-activity** headache, **cold-stimulus**, **primary stabbing**, **nummular**, **hypnic** headache; headache attributed to space travel [10] |

**Part 2: Secondary headaches (ICHD-3 groups)** [7]:
- headache attributed to:
  - **trauma or injury** to the head or neck;
  - **cranial or cervical vascular disorder**;
  - **non-vascular intracranial disorder** (e.g., **high or low CSF pressure**, neoplasm);
  - a **substance or its withdrawal** (including **medication-overuse headache**, caffeine withdrawal);
  - **infection**;
  - **disorder of homoeostasis**;
  - **disorder of the cranium, neck, eyes, ears, nose, sinuses, teeth, mouth** or other facial or cervical structures;
- **headache attributed to psychiatric disorder** [7].

**Part 3: Painful cranial neuropathies, other facial pain**:
- **neuralgias are a separate ICHD-3 chapter**: **trigeminal** ("classical" replaces "primary"), **occipital**, **glossopharyngeal**, **nervus intermedius**;
- painful trigeminal neuropathies, of which the commonest is **post-herpetic** [6].

### Clinical approach [1, 7, 8, 9]
1. **History:**
   - onset (new vs long-standing: **a new severe headache is more worrying**), time to peak, duration, frequency;
   - location, quality, associated features (nausea, photophobia, **cranial autonomic** features), triggers;
   - **premonitory and postdrome** symptoms;
   - **medication use** (days a month);
   - sleep, mood, caffeine.
2. **SNOOP red flags** (Table 11-1) [7]:
   - **S**ystemic symptoms or disease: fever, weight loss, HIV/immunosuppression, cancer, **pregnancy or postpartum**;
   - **N**eurological symptoms or signs: altered consciousness, diplopia, **pulsatile tinnitus**, focal deficit, seizure;
   - **O**nset **sudden** or **thunderclap** ("worst ever");
   - **O**lder onset (**over 50**);
   - **P**attern change: progressive, **Valsalva-provoked**, **postural**, **papilloedema**.
   - Also: **new headache in pregnancy or within 6 weeks postpartum**, raised BP, and a longer attack duration in a known migraineur [8]; **new headaches in children** [9].
3. **Examination:** neurological and fundi. Symptoms alone cannot separate primary from secondary headache [7].
4. **Investigations, targeted:**
   - **CT then LP** (xanthochromia) for possible SAH;
   - **MRI/MRA/CTA** for dissection, RCVS and venous thrombosis;
   - temporal arteritis: **prompt glucocorticoids** to prevent blindness (specific blood tests are not given in the source);
   - **LP opening pressure** and **perimetry** for raised CSF pressure;
   - **CT or MRI myelography** for CSF leaks;
   - **liberal non-contrast MRI** and **pre-eclampsia** screening in pregnancy [7, 3, 8].

### Key secondary causes [7, 3]

| Cause | Clues |
| --- | --- |
| **Subarachnoid haemorrhage** | Thunderclap (**about 25%** of thunderclap headaches); peak **within 1 second**; occipital, stabbing; meningism; sentinel bleed in up to 50%; CT may be normal, so do LP |
| **Arterial dissection** | Carotid: headache in **60–95%**, ipsilateral face or neck pain, **Horner**, amaurosis fugax. Vertebral: headache in about 70% |
| **RCVS** | Recurrent bilateral **thunderclap** headaches lasting 1–3 hours over days to weeks |
| **Temporal arteritis** | **Blindness in about half if untreated**; prevented by prompt glucocorticoids |
| **Brain tumour** | Rare cause; **headache in 30%** of tumours but the sole symptom in only 1–2%; positional, Valsalva or nocturnal headache; vomiting (posterior fossa) |
| **Subdural haematoma** | Insidious; elderly; mental-state change |
| **Pseudotumor cerebri (IIH)** | Variable, migraine-like or TTH-like headache; **pulse-synchronous tinnitus**, **transient visual obscurations**, papilloedema. **Up to 10%** have severe permanent visual loss. Screen for **sleep apnoea**. Acetazolamide lowers pressure but **did not improve headache disability** (IIHTT) |
| **Spontaneous intracranial hypotension** | **Orthostatic** headache (may fade over time). **Opening pressure is often normal**; at least 25% have normal imaging. **Epidural blood patch** (non-targeted helps about 30%). Consider in **refractory "chronic migraine"** |
| **Cardiac cephalalgia** | Exertional headache in older vascular patients; **triptans contraindicated** |
| **Others** | Angle-closure glaucoma (mimics migraine); "sinus headache" often migraine with autonomic features; sphenoid sinusitis; TMJ disorder; **post-traumatic headache**; **post-dural-puncture headache** and pneumocephalus after epidural |

### Management principles

**A. General (all headache types)** [1, 2, 12]:
- Correct **diagnosis** (ICHD-3) after excluding secondary causes.
- **Headache diary**; **lifestyle**: regular sleep and meals, aerobic exercise (150 minutes a week), weight control, caffeine moderation.
- **Limit acute medication:** **medication overuse** means **15 or more days a month** for simple analgesics, or **10 or more days a month** for triptans, ergots, combinations or opioids. **Avoid butalbital and opioids.**
- **Treat comorbidity:** sleep disorders, obesity, **psychiatric disease** (a risk factor for chronification), anxiety and depression.
- **Behavioural therapy:** relaxation, biofeedback, CBT.
- **Multidisciplinary, stepped care** for refractory or complicated cases.

**B. Migraine** (details in the migraine answers) [1, 2]:
- **Acute, stratified care:** NSAIDs or acetaminophen for mild attacks; **triptans** (avoid in vascular disease, hemiplegic migraine and brainstem aura); antiemetics; nasal or injectable routes; **gepants and ditans** emerging (2018).
- **Prevention** when there are 3 or more disabling or 6–8 or more headache days a month:
  - **propranolol, metoprolol, topiramate, amitriptyline, candesartan**; valproate (weak recommendation);
  - **onabotulinumtoxinA for chronic migraine**; **erenumab**;
  - nutraceuticals (magnesium, riboflavin, coenzyme Q10);
  - neuromodulation devices.

**C. Tension-type headache** [9, 12] (⚠️ thin; see gaps):
- Diagnosis is clearest when the headache is **"featureless"**. **Nausea is not part of TTH**; in children nausea strongly points to migraine [9].
- Evidence from systematic reviews (TTH with chronic neck pain) [12]:
  - **simple analgesics**: dipyrone +, ibuprofen +, paracetamol +, ketoprofen + (low-level evidence); aspirin o;
  - **tricyclic antidepressants** + / o; **SSRI/SNRI (venlafaxine)** o;
  - **exercise** + and **multimodal treatment** +;
  - **multimodal manual therapy** ++; spinal/thoracic manipulation +;
  - **acupuncture** +; **botulinum toxin A** o.
  - Grades: ++ moderate, + low, o no evidence.
- Paediatric TTH: no RCTs to guide treatment [9].
- Watch for **medication overuse**, and consider **CSF-pressure disorders**, which can mimic TTH [3].

**C (continued). Adult TTH from Levenson 2019** [13, 14]:
- **Clinical picture** [13]: a **global** headache, usually **mild to moderate**, sometimes **band-like or pressing**. It often **worsens as the day goes on or after stress**, with **few associated symptoms**.
- **Chronic TTH** = **more than 15 headache days a month**. It is one type of **chronic daily headache (CDH)** [13].
- **CDH** (about **5%** of the population) includes:
  - transformed (chronic) migraine;
  - medication-overuse headache;
  - chronic TTH;
  - new daily persistent headache;
  - hemicrania continua [14].
- **Most CDH patients have underlying migraine**, often worsened by **analgesic overuse**. **Any analgesic** (not only opioids) can cause it. Withdrawal usually helps, with **careful explanation** to the patient. So the key task is to **apportion migraine vs TTH vs analgesic overuse** [13].
- **Psychiatric comorbidity:**
  - CDH has a close, **probably bidirectional** link with **depression and anxiety**, which predict **poorer outcomes**;
  - **antidepressants and CBT** for comorbid mood disorder help [13];
  - CDH patients are more likely to overuse analgesics, to have depression or anxiety, to be disabled, and to have stress-related exacerbations [14].
- **Drug management** [14]:
  - **TCAs and SNRIs** are effective for TTH, among other pain syndromes. **TCAs** are effective for headache syndromes;
  - **SSRIs are less effective than TCAs for TTH** (Cochrane);
  - **mirtazapine reduced the duration and intensity of chronic TTH** in controlled trials;
  - for **CDH**, placebo-controlled evidence is sparse but supports **amitriptyline**, gabapentin, tizanidine, mirtazapine, topiramate, memantine and onabotulinumtoxinA;
  - **medication plus CBT beats either alone**.
- ⚠️ **Discrepancy:**
  - Henningsen's Table 1 [12] grades TCAs only "+ / o" and SSRI/SNRI (venlafaxine) "o" for **TTH with chronic neck pain**;
  - Levenson [14] states that TCAs and SNRIs are effective for TTH;
  - the two sources rate different evidence bases and are **not reconciled here**.
- **No adult doses** for TTH prophylaxis are given in these sources.

**D. Trigeminal autonomic cephalalgias** [5]:
- **Cluster headache:**
  - **men 3:1**, onset 20–40 years; excruciating, clockwork, restless patient; **alcohol triggers** in a bout;
  - **acute**: **high-flow oxygen**, **subcutaneous sumatriptan** (subcutaneous better than nasal, better than oral), **non-invasive vagus nerve stimulation**;
  - **prevention**: **verapamil**;
  - **refractory**: invasive neuromodulation.
- **Paroxysmal hemicrania and hemicrania continua:** **absolute response to indomethacin**. Reconsider the diagnosis if 75 mg three times daily for 1–2 weeks fails. Give gastroprotection; titrate down.
- **SUNCT/SUNA:** **lamotrigine**.

**E. Cranial neuralgias** [6]:
- **Classical trigeminal neuralgia** (V2/V3; neurovascular compression):
  - **carbamazepine or oxcarbazepine** first;
  - then baclofen or other AEDs; onabotulinumtoxinA;
  - **surgery**: microvascular decompression, rhizotomy, gamma knife.
- Post-herpetic neuropathy: AEDs, TCAs or SNRIs, topical capsaicin or lidocaine. Early **gabapentin** with antivirals may prevent it.
- **Occipital neuralgia:** peripheral **nerve block** first.

**F. Other primary headaches** [10]:
- Cough, exercise and sexual headaches: **exclude secondary causes first** (thunderclap at orgasm is an emergency).
- Treatments:
  - **indomethacin** (cough; **30 minutes before sex**; frequent stabbing headaches);
  - **β-blockers** (sexual or exercise headache prophylaxis);
  - **hypnic headache**: **caffeine, melatonin**, lithium (toxicity risk in the elderly);
  - **exploding head syndrome**: reassurance.

**G. Secondary headaches:** treat the cause [7, 3]:
- SAH: urgent CT, then LP if CT is negative (management beyond diagnosis is not in the source);
- **steroids** for giant cell arteritis;
- IIH: acetazolamide; remove CSF at LP to a mid-normal closing pressure; treat sleep apnoea; shunting or optic nerve sheath fenestration under trial; **plus headache-specific prevention** chosen by phenotype;
- SIH: **epidural blood patch** (targeted with fibrin sealant best);
- MOH: withdraw overused drugs, switch class (limit to **2–3 days a week**), start prevention.

**H. Special groups** [8, 9]:
- **Pregnancy:** acetaminophen and metoclopramide; avoid ergots, butalbital and prophylactic drugs where possible; nerve blocks and neuromodulation.
- **Children:**
  - ibuprofen; triptans (rizatriptan from age 6);
  - **CBT plus prevention** for chronic migraine;
  - school accommodations.

### Psychiatric relevance [11, 1, 2]
- **Headache attributed to psychiatric disorder** is an ICHD-3 secondary category [7].
- **Depression and anxiety are 2–4 times more common** in migraine (see the comorbidity answer) [11].
- **Psychiatric illness predicts chronic migraine** [2].
- Medication overuse and dependence (opioids, butalbital) are risks [1].
- **SSRI/SNRI plus triptan:** AHS considers added serotonin-syndrome risk **unproven (Level U)** [1]. ⚠️ Maudsley warns about it; not reconciled.

**Conclusion.** ICHD-3 divides headaches into:
- **primary** (migraine, TTH, TACs, other primary headaches);
- **secondary** (eight aetiological groups, including psychiatric);
- **painful cranial neuropathies and neuralgias**.

The first clinical task is to exclude dangerous secondary causes using **SNOOP** (systemic features, neurological signs, sudden onset, older age, pattern change) plus pregnancy or postpartum status, with targeted imaging and LP. Management is then diagnosis-specific:
- migraine: stratified acute treatment and prevention (β-blockers, topiramate, amitriptyline, candesartan, botulinum toxin, CGRP antibodies);
- TACs: oxygen, sumatriptan and verapamil for cluster; indomethacin for hemicranias; lamotrigine for SUNCT/SUNA;
- neuralgias: carbamazepine, then surgery;
- secondary headaches: treat the cause.

All of this sits on a base of lifestyle measures, limits on acute medication (to prevent medication-overuse headache), treatment of sleep and psychiatric comorbidity, and behavioural therapy.

For **tension-type headache**, the held sources support:
- simple analgesics (low-level evidence) for attacks;
- **identifying and withdrawing analgesic overuse**;
- **amitriptyline or another TCA** (or mirtazapine) for chronic TTH, rather than an SSRI;
- exercise, manual and multimodal therapy;
- **CBT combined with medication**;
- treatment of comorbid depression and anxiety.

The formal ICHD-3 TTH criteria are still missing.
