> **Source discipline:** this answer uses only the departmental dementia notes NB-16 (Weave study guide, "Dementias": Part 14 "Legal and ethical issues", and Vignette 11 "Capacity to make a will") [1]. **The sources contain no Indian statutory text on wills** (for example, the Indian Succession Act), so that is not covered.

## Exam answer

**Definition [1].**
- Testamentary capacity is "the legal and psychiatric determination that a person has sufficient mental capacity to **make a valid will at the time of execution**".
- It is one of the legal instruments relevant in dementia, alongside advance directives, lasting power of attorney and guardianship.

### Legal test: Banks v Goodfellow (1870) [1]
A testator must:
| Component | Meaning | Assessment question |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Know the **nature of making a will** | Understands that the document disposes of property after death | "What is a will? What happens to your property when you die?" |
| 2. Know the **extent of the estate** | A general (not necessarily exact) understanding of what they own | "Broadly, what property, savings or assets do you have?" |
| 3. Know the **natural objects of their bounty** | Knows who would ordinarily expect to benefit (spouse, children) | "Tell me about your family. Who are your children?" |
| 4. **Absence of a disorder of the mind** that "poisons affections, perverts judgement, or prevents exercise of natural faculties" | No delusion or mental disorder **directly influences** the testamentary decision | "Is a delusion driving the decision to exclude this person?" |

**Key principles [1]:**
- **Capacity is decision-specific and time-specific.**
- A **diagnosis of dementia does not automatically remove capacity**. A person with dementia can have testamentary capacity for a particular will if the criteria are met.
- **Complex decisions** (financial matters, wills) demand more capacity than simple ones.

### Conducting the assessment [1]
1. **Review records:** current cognitive scores (for example, MMSE, CDR), the neuropsychological profile, medication, recent notes.
2. **Interview the patient alone**, and the informant separately.
3. **Apply the four Banks v Goodfellow criteria** with the questions above.
4. **Explore the reasons** for the dispositions, especially any **exclusion** of an expected beneficiary:
   - **Delusions are the critical issue.** The commonest problem is a **persecutory or theft delusion** about the excluded person, which is common in Alzheimer's disease. If such a delusion drives the decision, capacity **for this decision is compromised**.
   - If there is a **clear, rational, non-delusional reason** (for example, a decades-long estrangement), capacity may be **intact**.
5. **Use the general capacity framework** (MacCAT-T components): **understanding, appreciation, reasoning and communication of a consistent choice**.
6. **Document thoroughly:** time, date, **verbatim answers**, reasoning, conclusion.
7. **Outcome:**
   - capacity intact: a **capacity certificate** supporting execution of the will;
   - capacity impaired: advise the solicitor; guardianship or court involvement may be needed.

### Related issues in dementia [1]
- **Plan early:** execute **power of attorney** while capacity is present. In India, the **MHCA 2017** provides for **advance directives and nominated representatives**.
- **Financial exploitation** is the **commonest form of elder abuse** in dementia. Be alert to undue influence by beneficiaries.
- **Treatment consent** is assessed separately for each decision.

**Conclusion.** Testamentary capacity rests on the four Banks v Goodfellow limbs:
1. nature of the act;
2. extent of property;
3. claims of beneficiaries;
4. no mind-disorder distorting the decision.

The psychiatrist assesses it for the specific will at the specific time, looks especially for delusions about beneficiaries, and documents verbatim.

## In depth

### Worked example (NB-16 Vignette 11) [1]
- **Case:** an 80-year-old man with mild to moderate AD (MMSE 18, CDR 1) wishes to exclude one of four children.
- **Assessment:**
  - Does he know all four children and can he name them?
  - Does he understand what the will does?
  - Is his reason rational and his own (for example, "never visits, estranged 30 years"), or delusional ("stealing from me")?
- **Decision:** capacity intact means a supportive certificate; impaired means legal advice and guardianship.
