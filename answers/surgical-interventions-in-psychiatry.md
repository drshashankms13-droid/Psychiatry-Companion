## Exam answer

**Definition.** **Neurosurgery for psychiatric disorders (NPD)**, historically called **psychosurgery**, means surgical procedures on the brain that are intended to treat a mental illness by changing the function of specific neural circuits. Today they are reserved for **chronic, severe, treatment-refractory** illness. There are two modern families [1]:
- **Ablative (lesional) stereotactic procedures:** anterior capsulotomy, anterior cingulotomy, subcaudate tractotomy, limbic leucotomy. Delivered by thermocoagulation, **gamma-knife radiosurgery** or **MR-guided focused ultrasound (MRgFUS)**.
- **Reversible neuromodulation:** **deep brain stimulation (DBS)** and **vagus nerve stimulation (VNS)**.

### Historical evolution [1]
1930s–60s **prefrontal leucotomy/lobotomy** (Moniz; Freeman–Watts; transorbital) → 1960s **stereotactic lesions** (cingulotomy, capsulotomy) → 1999–2000s **DBS** → late 2010s **circuit-based targeting, closed-loop techniques**.

### Mechanism
Psychiatric disorders are modelled as **dysfunctional networks**. Precise lesions or stimulation at a network node or white-matter tract interrupt pathological circuit activity [1]:
- **OCD:** orbitofronto-striato-thalamo-cortical loop.
- **Depression:** fronto-limbic circuits (subgenual cingulate, ventral striatum).

### Procedures and targets

| Procedure | Target | Circuit interrupted | Main indications |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Anterior capsulotomy** | Anterior limb of internal capsule | Fronto-striatal / fronto-thalamic tracts | **OCD**, anxiety |
| **Anterior cingulotomy** | Dorsal anterior cingulate cortex and cingulum bundle | Limbic (Papez) circuit | Depression, OCD, chronic pain |
| **Subcaudate tractotomy** | Substantia innominata below the head of caudate | Orbitofrontal–subcortical fibres | Depression, OCD |
| **Limbic leucotomy** | Bilateral cingulotomy + subcaudate tractotomy | Combined | OCD, depression |
| **DBS** | VC/VS, STN, SCC, MFB, NAcc, CM-Pf thalamus | Reversible modulation | OCD, TRD, Tourette |
| **VNS** | Left vagus nerve → nucleus tractus solitarius | Brainstem-limbic projections | Chronic TRD (FDA 2005) |

Sources: [1].

### Patient selection
**Indian guidelines** (Doshi et al. 2019) [1]:
- **Indications:** chronic, severe, **treatment-refractory OCD, depression and Tourette syndrome**.
- **Prerequisites:** failure of adequate trials of medication (including augmentation), evidence-based psychotherapy (ERP or CBT) and ECT where relevant.
- **Team:** psychiatrist, functional neurosurgeon, neuropsychologist, an **independent oversight committee**.
- **Consent:** capacity-based, with clear explanation that surgery is **symptomatic, not curative**, and is one part of an ongoing treatment programme (Nuttin 2014 consensus) [1].
- **Follow-up:** at least 1 year with a multidisciplinary team; imaging; continue standard treatments [1].
- **Exclusions:** psychosis, current substance dependence, severe personality disorder, organic brain disease, inability to consent.

### Outcomes and risks
- **Ablation for OCD:**
  - Capsulotomy: roughly half of patients respond in published series (check exact figures).
  - **Gamma-knife capsulotomy RCT** (Lopes 2014, n=16): active was superior to sham at long-term follow-up [2].
  - Cingulotomy: response is somewhat lower than capsulotomy in OCD series.
- **DBS for OCD:** about 60% responders [1].
- **Risks of ablation:** irreversibility; haemorrhage; seizures; apathy, disinhibition and executive deficits (especially with large or bilateral lesions); weight gain; radiation-induced oedema or cysts (gamma knife).
- **Evidence level:** current ablative procedures have **level II evidence only**, from non-randomised studies, in refractory MDD and OCD (Nuttin 2014) [1].

### Legal safeguard in India
**MHCA 2017, Section 96:** psychosurgery shall not be performed as treatment for mental illness unless (a) the person gives **informed consent** and (b) **approval from the concerned Mental Health Review Board** has been obtained [1].

**Conclusion.** Neurosurgery for psychiatric disorders has moved from crude, abused lobotomy to precise, stereotactic and often reversible circuit-based interventions. It is a **last-resort** option for a small number of patients with severe, refractory OCD, depression or Tourette syndrome, delivered by expert multidisciplinary teams within strict ethical and legal safeguards. DBS adds reversibility and the possibility of sham-controlled trials, but it is not clearly more effective than modern ablation.

## In depth

### Background
- **1888:** Burckhardt performed topectomies (excision of cortex) in Switzerland on patients with psychosis.
- **1935:** Egas Moniz and Almeida Lima performed prefrontal leucotomy. Moniz won the Nobel Prize in 1949.
- **1936–50s:** Walter Freeman and James Watts popularised lobotomy in the US. Freeman's ice-pick **transorbital lobotomy** led to tens of thousands of operations, many on people with schizophrenia, with severe apathy, disinhibition and deaths.
- **1950s:** chlorpromazine arrived and lobotomy declined rapidly. Public outrage followed, and US legislation and commissions (1970s) followed.
- **1947–60s:** stereotactic frames (Spiegel & Wycis) enabled small lesions. Leksell developed capsulotomy and the gamma knife; Ballantine developed cingulotomy (Massachusetts General Hospital); Knight developed subcaudate tractotomy (UK); Kelly developed limbic leucotomy.
- **1999 onward:** DBS (Nuttin) [1].

### Epidemiology
- NPD is rare: probably a few hundred procedures worldwide each year, concentrated in specialist centres (US, Belgium, the Netherlands, UK, Sweden, Spain, Brazil, China and a few Indian centres).
- About 10% of OCD and a smaller fraction of depression is "highly refractory", but only a fraction of these patients are candidates.

### Classification and criteria
| Category | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| Open lesion (historical) | Leucotomy, lobotomy, topectomy |
| Stereotactic ablation | Thermocapsulotomy, cingulotomy, subcaudate tractotomy, limbic leucotomy |
| Non-invasive ablation | Gamma-knife radiosurgery; MRgFUS |
| Implanted neuromodulation | DBS, VNS, epidural cortical stimulation (EpCS) |

### Mechanism in detail
- **Capsulotomy** interrupts fibres from orbitofrontal cortex and ACC to thalamus and striatum, damping the "hyperactive loop" seen on PET in OCD.
- **Cingulotomy** reduces affective distress and the salience of pain, obsessions and negative mood.
- **Subcaudate tractotomy** cuts orbitofrontal-hypothalamic and limbic connections.
- **Connectomics:** good outcomes after capsulotomy and DBS converge on a shared ventral prefrontal–thalamic/STN tract, which suggests a common circuit target.

### Clinical features in detail: time course
- **Ablation:** benefit usually emerges over months. Gamma knife needs 6–24 months for the radiation lesion to mature. Postoperative ERP and medication improve outcome.
- **DBS:** titration over months.
- **VNS:** benefit builds over 1–2 years [1].

### Assessment in detail
- **Refractoriness documentation:** dose, duration and adherence for each trial; ERP sessions; ECT course.
- **Scales:** Y-BOCS, HAM-D/MADRS, YGTSS, GAF, quality of life.
- **Neuropsychology** and personality assessment (baseline for change).
- **Imaging:** MRI; DTI for targeting.
- **Capacity assessment** and independent second opinion; ethics committee review.

### Differential: choosing between procedures
| Factor | Ablation | DBS | VNS |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Reversibility | No | Yes | Yes |
| Cost | Lower | High (device, batteries) | Moderate–high |
| Follow-up burden | Low | High (programming) | Moderate |
| Sham-controlled RCT possible | Difficult (gamma knife sham possible) | Yes | Yes |
| Best evidence | OCD (level II) | OCD (level I for STN) | Long-term TRD open data |

### Management details
- Continue pharmacotherapy and psychotherapy. Introduce ERP after surgery, when patients often engage better.
- Monitor for apathy, frontal-lobe syndrome, seizures, weight gain, mood elevation and suicidality.
- **VNS details:**
  - Left vagus, chosen because of fewer cardiac effects.
  - Side effects: hoarseness, cough, dyspnoea, neck pain, paraesthesia, dysphagia, **hypomania 1–3%**.
  - Surgical risks: infection about 3%, transient vocal cord paresis, **asystole about 1 in 1,000** during testing [1].
- **VNS evidence:**
  - Rush 2005 acute RCT (n=235, 10 weeks): non-significant on the primary outcome.
  - George 2005 (1 year, VNS + TAU vs TAU): response 27% vs 13%.
  - Aaronson 2017 (5-year registry, n≈795): cumulative response 67.6% vs 40.9%; remission 43.3% vs 25.7% [1].

### Critical appraisal
- **Historical abuse** (lobotomy) shapes public distrust. Modern safeguards (consent, independent review, outcome registries) are essential.
- **Evidence quality:** few RCTs; small samples; open follow-up; publication bias [1].
- **Personality and identity concerns** with lesions; irreversibility.
- **Equity:** cost and geography limit access; the risk of "surgical tourism".
- **Law:** Section 96 means people who lack capacity cannot be treated, even when severely ill. Some argue this protects them; others argue it denies effective care.

### Indian context
- MHCA 2017, Section 96: informed consent plus MHRB approval [1].
- Indian guidelines (*Indian J Psychiatry* 2019), written jointly by neurosurgeons and psychiatrists [1].
- **Experience:** Indian centres (e.g., Mumbai, Delhi, Chandigarh, Bengaluru) have reported capsulotomy and DBS in refractory OCD, with outcomes similar to international series.
- **Cost:** gamma-knife and DBS costs are high. Thermocapsulotomy is cheaper, which is relevant to resource-limited settings.

### Recent advances
- **Established:** stereotactic, image-guided lesions; DBS for OCD (FDA HDE 2009) [1].
- **Emerging:**
  - **MRgFUS capsulotomy** (incisionless, real-time thermal monitoring).
  - Tractography-guided targeting.
  - Closed-loop DBS [1].
- **Investigational:** laser interstitial thermal therapy (LITT); epidural cortical stimulation [1].
