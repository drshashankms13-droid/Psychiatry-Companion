## Exam answer

> **How to use this answer:** the same content answers three questions. For "salient differences" (short note), write the introduction, the structure table and the disorder-by-disorder table. For "ICD-11 is superior" or "not very different in reliability and validity" (critical essays), add the evaluation section and take a position in the conclusion. The CDDR-vs-DSM-5 comparison is covered in more depth in the answer on ICD-11 guiding principles and CDDR.

**Introduction.** The **ICD-11** was approved by the **World Health Assembly in May 2019** and came into effect for health reporting on **1 January 2022** — the first major revision of the world's most widely used classification of mental disorders in nearly 30 years [1, 2]. The clinical version, the **Clinical Descriptions and Diagnostic Requirements (CDDR**; earlier called the CDDG), replaces the ICD-10 "blue book" [1]. Its organising principles were **clinical utility** and **global applicability**, alongside scientific validity [1].

### What changed in the process

- The most global, multilingual, multidisciplinary revision ever: Working Groups from all six WHO regions, many members from low- and middle-income countries [1].
- **Global Clinical Practice Network**: nearly **15,000 clinicians from 155 countries** took part in internet field studies; clinic-based field studies in **13–14 countries** tested reliability and clinical utility [1].
- Harmonised with DSM-5 to minimise arbitrary differences, but justified conceptual differences allowed [1].

### Salient structural differences

| Feature | ICD-10 (1992) | ICD-11 (2022) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Chapter | Chapter V (F00–F99) | Chapter 06, codes **6A00–6E8Z**; 21 blocks [1, 2] |
| Groupings | Maximum 10 (decimal F-codes) — forced heterogeneous groupings such as "neurotic, stress-related and somatoform" | Flexible alphanumeric coding → **21 groupings** based on evidence and clinical utility [1, 2] |
| Order | Kraepelinian (organic → psychoses → neuroses → personality) | **Developmental** — neurodevelopmental first, neurocognitive last [1] |
| Organic vs non-organic | Retained (sleep, sexual dysfunction) | Abandoned: new chapters for **sleep–wake disorders (Ch. 7)** and **conditions related to sexual health (Ch. 17)** [1, 2] |
| Childhood disorders | Separate grouping | **Lifespan approach** — distributed to adult groupings (e.g. separation anxiety → anxiety disorders) [1] |
| Format | Description + guidelines, inconsistent across chapters | Standard template: essential features, **boundary with normality**, **boundary with other disorders**, course, developmental, **culture-related** and gender-related features [1, 2] |
| Cut-offs | Some arbitrary counts and durations | Avoided unless empirically supported (exception: depressive episode, 5 of 10 symptoms) [1] |
| Dimensions | Categorical | **Dimensional** for personality disorder (severity + trait domains) and psychosis (symptom qualifiers) [1] |
| Hierarchy rules | Many exclusions (e.g. OCD not with depression) | Most removed; co-occurrence allowed [1] |

### Disorder-by-disorder changes (high-yield)

| Area | ICD-10 | ICD-11 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Neurodevelopmental | Mental retardation; hyperkinetic disorder in behavioural chapter | **Disorders of intellectual development** (with behavioural indicator tables); **ADHD** moved here; autism and Asperger's merged into **autism spectrum disorder** [1] |
| Schizophrenia | Subtypes (paranoid, hebephrenic, catatonic…); first-rank symptoms emphasised | **Subtypes abolished**; **6 symptom dimensions** rated (positive, negative, depressive, manic, psychomotor, cognitive); first-rank symptoms de-emphasised; duration remains 1 month [1, 2] |
| ATPD | Several subtypes | Only the **polymorphic** form retained; ≤3 months [1] |
| Catatonia | Subtype of schizophrenia / organic | **Separate grouping** — with mental disorder, substance-induced, secondary [1] |
| Mood | Episodes coded independently; bipolar not split | Diagnosis by **pattern of episodes**; **bipolar I and II**; depressive episode needs **5 of 10** symptoms (hopelessness added; fatigue no longer an entry symptom); mania requires **increased activity/energy**; mixed depressive and anxiety disorder moved to depressive disorders [1] |
| Anxiety | Phobic vs other anxiety | **Focus of apprehension**; GAD with worry; can co-occur with depression [1] |
| OCD and related | OCD in neurotic group | **New OCRD grouping**: OCD, **body dysmorphic disorder**, **olfactory reference disorder**, **hypochondriasis**, **hoarding**, BFRBs (trichotillomania, excoriation); insight qualifier [1] |
| Stress | Reactions to severe stress | **Complex PTSD** and **prolonged grief disorder** added; PTSD narrowed (3 core features, re-experiencing in the present); **acute stress reaction no longer a mental disorder** [1] |
| Dissociative | Dissociative (conversion) | "Conversion" dropped; **dissociative neurological symptom disorder**; partial DID; trance and possession trance separated [1] |
| Eating | Atypical categories common | **Binge eating disorder**, **ARFID**; AN BMI threshold **18**; no endocrine or fat-phobia requirement [1] |
| Somatic | Somatoform disorders, neurasthenia | **Bodily distress disorder** (positive features, not "medically unexplained"); body integrity dysphoria [1] |
| Substances/addictive | Harmful use, dependence | **Single episode of harmful use**, harmful pattern, dependence (2 of 3 features); **gaming disorder**; gambling moved to addictive behaviours [1, 2] |
| Impulse control | Habit and impulse disorders | **Intermittent explosive disorder**, **compulsive sexual behaviour disorder** [1] |
| Personality | 10 categorical types | **Mild / moderate / severe** + 5 trait domains + optional borderline pattern; personality difficulty [1] |
| Gender | Gender identity disorder (mental) | **Gender incongruence**, moved to sexual health chapter — not a mental disorder [1] |
| Other new | — | PMDD, burn-out (as occupational phenomenon, QD85) [1, 2] |

### Comparison with DSM-5 (for the CDDR question)

| Aspect | DSM-5 | ICD-11 CDDR |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Approach | Operational criteria with counts and durations | Prototype descriptions of **essential features**, flexible clinical judgement [1, 2] |
| Schizophrenia duration | 6 months incl. prodrome | 1 month [2] |
| PTSD | 4 clusters, 20 symptoms | 3 features; complex PTSD separate [2] |
| Personality disorder | Categorical (Section II); alternative model (Section III) | Fully dimensional [1] |
| Prolonged grief | Added only in DSM-5-TR (2022) | Present from the start [1] |
| Cost/access | Proprietary | Free, multilingual, digital [2] |

### Critical evaluation — is ICD-11 superior?

**For superiority**
- **Clinical utility:** field studies in 13 countries rated it high in ease of use and goodness of fit [1].
- **Reliability:** diagnostic reliability is **at least as high** as criteria-based approaches despite more flexible guidelines [1].
- **Validity:** groupings follow shared pathophysiology and phenomenology (OCRD, stress disorders); obsolete organic/non-organic split removed; new categories with good evidence (BED, complex PTSD, prolonged grief) [1].
- **Global and cultural applicability:** culture-related features for every disorder [1].
- **Less stigma and artificial comorbidity:** "intellectual development", gender incongruence outside mental disorders, dimensional personality [1].

**Against / limitations**
- **Validity is still syndromal:** no biomarkers; categories remain descriptive, as in ICD-10.
- Flexible guidelines may **reduce reliability in routine practice** and research comparability.
- **Personality model** is unfamiliar; loss of specific types (e.g. narcissistic) and research continuity.
- Controversial additions: **gaming disorder**, compulsive sexual behaviour disorder, mixed anxiety–depression — fears of medicalising normal behaviour.
- Implementation burden: training, software, legal and insurance systems; many countries (including India) still use ICD-10 codes widely.
- For some disorders (e.g. schizophrenia), changes are modest — supporting the statement that ICD-11 is "**not very different**" in core diagnostic validity.

**Conclusion.** ICD-11 is **structurally and clinically superior** to ICD-10 — evidence-based groupings, a lifespan and cultural approach, dimensional options and better clinical utility — and its reliability is at least comparable. But its **validity is not fundamentally different**: both remain descriptive, syndrome-based systems without biological markers. The improvement is real but incremental; its value will depend on implementation and training.

## In depth

### Background and timeline

- ICD-10 mental disorders chapter (1992) was produced in several versions: short glossary definitions for statistics, the **Clinical Descriptions and Diagnostic Guidelines ("blue book")** for clinicians, research criteria (DCR-10), and a primary care version [1].
- A global survey found that clinicians regularly use the CDDG text when making an initial diagnosis, not only for coding [1].
- Timeline: alpha browser 2011 → beta draft 2012 → pre-final version released **18 June 2018** → approved by the **72nd World Health Assembly, May 2019** → reporting from **1 January 2022** [2].
- ICD-11 is built on a **digital "foundation" component** with machine-readable content, an API, extension codes and code combinations ("post-coordination") [2].

### Classification and criteria — the CDDR template

Each disorder in the CDDR contains [2]:
1. Brief definition (100–125 words)
2. Inclusion and exclusion terms
3. **Essential (required) features**
4. **Boundary with normality (threshold)**
5. **Boundary with other disorders (differential diagnosis)**
6. Coded qualifiers/subtypes
7. Course features
8. Associated clinical presentations
9. **Culture-related features** (e.g. panic presenting as *khyâl cap* or *ataque de nervios*) [1]
10. Developmental presentations (children, adolescents, older adults)
11. Gender-related features

**The 21 groupings of Chapter 06** [1, 2]: neurodevelopmental; schizophrenia and other primary psychotic; catatonia; mood; anxiety and fear-related; obsessive-compulsive and related; specifically associated with stress; dissociative; feeding and eating; elimination; bodily distress and bodily experience; substance use and addictive behaviours; impulse control; disruptive behaviour and dissocial; personality and related traits; paraphilic; factitious; neurocognitive; associated with pregnancy, childbirth and the puerperium; psychological or behavioural factors affecting disorders classified elsewhere; secondary mental or behavioural syndromes.

### More detail on selected changes

- **Schizophrenia:** at least 2 of 6 symptoms, at least one core — persistent delusions, persistent hallucinations, thought disorder, or experiences of influence/passivity/control; course qualifiers (first episode, multiple episodes, continuous; in partial or full remission) [2].
- **Schizoaffective disorder:** requires schizophrenia and a moderate or severe depressive, manic or mixed episode **simultaneously**; describes the current episode, not a lifetime diagnosis [1, 2].
- **Delusional disorder:** persistent, induced and other acute predominantly delusional disorders collapsed into one; at least 3 months [2].
- **Depressive episode:** three symptom clusters — affective, cognitive, neurovegetative; melancholic qualifier replaces the somatic syndrome; qualifiers for anxiety, panic attacks, seasonal pattern, persistence >2 years [1].
- **Mixed episodes** retained (unlike DSM-5's "mixed features" specifier) and indicate bipolar I [1].
- **PTSD:** re-experiencing must be in the present (not just memories), addressing ICD-10's low threshold; duration at least several weeks [1, 2].
- **Adjustment disorder:** defined positively by **preoccupation with the stressor** and failure to adapt; subtypes removed [1, 2].
- **Substance dependence:** 2 of 3 features (impaired control; increasing priority; physiological features) over 12 months, or continuous use for 1 month [2]. Hazardous use is coded outside the mental disorders chapter to prompt brief interventions [1].
- **Gaming disorder:** impaired control, increasing priority, continuation despite harm, usually for 12 months [2].
- **Paraphilic disorders:** focus on non-consenting others; fetishism, fetishistic transvestism and sadomasochism removed [1].
- **Neurocognitive:** delirium, mild neurocognitive disorder, amnestic disorder, dementia (mild/moderate/severe), with the underlying cause coded separately [1].

### Evidence — field studies

| Study | Finding |
| --- | --- |
| GCPN internet studies (about a dozen) | Compared ICD-11 vs ICD-10 guidelines on standardised case vignettes; generally improved accuracy and consistency; identified points of confusion that were revised [1] |
| Clinical utility study, 13 countries (Reed et al., 2018) | Clinicians rated ICD-11 guidelines as easy to use, good fit, clear [1] |
| Reliability study, 14 countries (Reed et al., 2018) | Reliability at least as high as strict criteria-based approaches [1] |

Author's note (not in uploaded sources; check before marking `reviewed`): in the reliability study, intraclass kappas ranged roughly from 0.45 (dysthymic disorder) to 0.88 (social anxiety disorder), with most high-burden disorders above 0.60.

### Critical appraisal in more depth

- **Clinical utility vs research precision:** flexible prototypes suit clinicians but make cross-study comparison harder; DSM-5 remains dominant in research.
- **Dimensional within categorical:** ICD-11 adds dimensions without abandoning categories — a compromise between RDoC/HiTOP-style science and administrative needs [1].
- **Cultural applicability** is improved but still largely derived from Western concepts; culture-bound syndromes are handled through culture-related features rather than separate categories.
- **Diagnostic inflation concerns:** single episode of harmful substance use, gaming disorder and prolonged grief could pathologise normal experiences if applied loosely; ICD-11's "boundary with normality" sections are designed to guard against this [1].
- **India:** Indian psychiatrists (e.g. AIIMS, New Delhi) contributed to Working Groups [1]; transition requires training, revised forms for MHCA documentation, disability certification and insurance coding.

### Indian context

- The **Mental Healthcare Act 2017** defines mental illness broadly and requires diagnosis "in accordance with nationally or internationally accepted medical standards (including the latest edition of the ICD)" — so ICD-11 has legal relevance (author's note — check wording of Section 3).
- Disability assessment (IDEAS) and insurance coding still largely use ICD-10 codes; parallel use will continue during transition.
- ATPD retention is relevant to India and other LMICs, where acute polymorphic psychoses with good outcome are common [1].

### Recent advances

- ICD-11 CDDR published by WHO in **2024** (free online).
- Primary health care version (ICD-11 PHC) for non-specialists; ongoing training programmes through WHO and national societies.
- Research linking ICD-11 dimensional qualifiers to HiTOP and RDoC frameworks.
