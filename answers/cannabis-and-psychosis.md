## Exam answer

**Introduction.** Cannabis is the most widely used illicit drug worldwide and in India (2.8% current users, 2019 national survey) [general knowledge]. Its psychiatric relevance spans:
- intoxication and acute anxiety and psychosis;
- **cannabis use disorder** and withdrawal;
- **cannabis-induced psychotic disorder**;
- its role as a **component cause of schizophrenia**;
- cognitive and mood effects;
- emerging **therapeutic cannabinoids** (CBD).

Prospective cohorts show cannabis use is associated with psychosis in a **dose-dependent** way, with greater risk from **early adolescent** use and in the **genetically vulnerable**. Cannabis is **neither necessary nor sufficient**: it is a **component cause** [1].

### Pharmacology [1]
- **Δ9-THC:** CB1 partial agonist; psychoactive; addictive; psychotogenic.
- **CBD:** opposes THC effects; anxiolytic and possibly **antipsychotic**. **Low CBD:THC ratio** products (sinsemilla, "skunk") carry the highest risk.
- **Endocannabinoid system:**
  - **CB1** receptors are presynaptic in the cortex, hippocampus, basal ganglia and cerebellum, and regulate dopamine, GABA and glutamate release;
  - **CB2** receptors are on immune cells and glia;
  - ligands are **anandamide** and **2-AG**.

### Cannabis-induced psychotic disorder: clinical features
- **Onset:**
  - during or soon after intoxication or heavy use (ICD-11 6C41.6; DSM-5-TR substance-induced psychotic disorder);
  - DSM-5-TR: symptoms in excess of intoxication, with evidence they developed during or **within 1 month** of use.
- **Phenomenology:**
  - **persecutory delusions**, hallucinations (often **visual**), ideas of reference;
  - affective lability, hypomanic features;
  - depersonalisation, **perplexity and confusion**;
  - relatively preserved insight in some patients.
- **Course:** usually resolves within **days to weeks** of abstinence.
  - About **a third or more (up to 47%) convert to schizophrenia or bipolar disorder** over follow-up (Danish register: Arendt 2005; Starzer 2018) [general knowledge; check].
  - Conversion is highest in young men and within 3–5 years.
- **Differentiating from primary schizophrenia:**
  - temporal link to heavy use;
  - more visual hallucinations;
  - more insight and less formal thought disorder;
  - rapid resolution with abstinence;
  - family history is often present in those who later convert.

### Evidence that cannabis causes psychosis (Bradford Hill) [1]

| Criterion | Evidence |
| --- | --- |
| **Strength and consistency** | Swedish conscripts (n ≈ 45,000–50,000): OR **2.4** ever-use, **6.0** heavy use (15-year follow-up); 3.1 (27 years); 3.7 (35 years). NEMESIS OR 2.8; Christchurch 1.8; EDSP 1.7. **Moore 2007 meta-analysis: OR 1.4** for any psychotic outcome after adjusting for about 60 confounders |
| **Dose–response** | Consistent across cohorts; daily high-potency use carries the greatest risk (Di Forti 2014) |
| **Temporality** | **Dunedin:** use by 15 years led to **10% vs 3%** schizophreniform disorder at 26. Christchurch: psychotic symptoms at 18 did *not* predict later use (against self-medication). Foti 2010 found bidirectional effects |
| **Earlier onset** | Meta-analysis: psychosis onset **2.7 years earlier** in users (alcohol showed no effect). Age at first use predicts age at onset (gap of about 7–8 years) |
| **Sibling control** | McGrath 2010: association persists in sibling-pair analysis |
| **Biological plausibility** | ↑ anandamide in the CSF of patients with schizophrenia; ↑ CB1 in DLPFC and cingulate; THC disrupts VTA dopamine regulation, causing **aberrant salience**; ↓ frontal theta coherence; prefrontal, cingulate and cerebellar structural changes |
| **Experimental** | IV THC induces transient positive symptoms in healthy volunteers; CBD attenuates this |
| **Population impact** | **About 8–14% of schizophrenia** cases preventable (Zammit 13%; Moore 14% of UK psychotic outcomes). Individual lifetime risk is still **below 3%** even in regular users |

### Gene–environment interaction [1]
- **Family history:** relatives of patients with cannabis-associated psychosis have **10×** the schizophrenia risk (McGuire 1995).
- **COMT Val158Met:** Val/Val adolescent users had RR **10.9** vs 1.1 for Met/Met (Caspi 2005). **Not replicated** (Zammit 2007).
- **AKT1 rs2494732** (replicated: van Winkel 2011; Di Forti 2012); **DAT1 × AKT1** interaction.
- **Environmental cofactors:** **childhood sexual abuse**, **urban upbringing**. Stress cross-sensitisation is dopamine-mediated.

### Management of cannabis-induced psychosis
1. **Assess:**
   - substance history (quantity, potency, route, synthetic cannabinoids, other drugs);
   - urine drug screen (THC metabolites detectable for days to weeks in heavy users);
   - rule out organic causes;
   - risk to self and others.
2. **Acute treatment:**
   - safe environment;
   - **short-term benzodiazepine** for agitation;
   - **antipsychotic** if symptoms persist beyond a few days or are severe. Use low doses, because users may be more sensitive to EPS.
3. **Abstinence** is the main treatment:
   - MET and **CBT**, contingency management;
   - family involvement;
   - withdrawal management (insomnia, irritability; symptomatic).
4. **Follow-up for at least 1–2 years** because of the high conversion rate. Use an early intervention in psychosis framework.
5. **Continued use in established schizophrenia:**
   - more relapses, admissions and non-adherence;
   - worse positive symptoms.

   Integrated dual-diagnosis treatment helps. **Clozapine** may reduce cannabis use (observational evidence) [general knowledge].

### Applying this to "Cannabis in psychiatry"
For the broader question, structure the answer as:
1. **Cannabis use disorder:**
   - about **9–10%** of users become dependent (**1 in 6** adolescent starters);
   - **cannabis withdrawal syndrome:** irritability, anxiety, insomnia and vivid dreams, decreased appetite, restlessness, low mood; onset 24–72 hours, peak in the first week, lasting up to 2 weeks;
   - **treatment:** MET/CBT/CM; no approved drug; gabapentin, NAC (adolescents), nabiximols under study.
2. **Psychosis** (as above).
3. **Mood and anxiety:**
   - anxiety and panic in intoxication;
   - adolescent use associated with later depression (OR about 1.4) and suicidality (Gobbi 2019, general knowledge);
   - bipolar disorder: worse course.
4. **Cognition:** acute memory impairment; IQ decline with persistent adolescent use (Dunedin, Meier 2012); amotivation.
5. **Medical complications:**
   - cannabis hyperemesis syndrome;
   - respiratory effects;
   - cardiovascular events;
   - impaired driving.
6. **Synthetic cannabinoids** (see `synthetic-cannabinoids`): full CB1 agonists, more severe psychosis, seizures.
7. **Therapeutic cannabinoids:**
   - **CBD:** approved for Dravet and Lennox–Gastaut syndromes; **antipsychotic signal** (Leweke 2012, McGuire 2018 adjunct trial); anxiety and cannabis use disorder trials;
   - **nabilone and dronabinol;**
   - **nabiximols** for MS spasticity.

   Evidence for psychiatric indications (depression, PTSD, anxiety) is weak (Black 2019 *Lancet Psychiatry* meta-analysis) [general knowledge].
8. **Policy:** see `cannabis-legalisation-debate-india`.

**Conclusion.** Heavy, early and high-potency cannabis use is a **modifiable component cause** of psychosis, acting in combination with genetic and environmental vulnerability. Cannabis-induced psychosis often heralds schizophrenia or bipolar disorder. Management centres on **abstinence, short-term antipsychotics and long follow-up**. Public education about adolescent risk is a key prevention target.

## In depth

### Background
- **History:**
  - the Indian Hemp Drugs Commission (1893–94) found no harm from moderate use;
  - Chopra & Smith (1974) described psychotic reactions in East Indians;
  - Andréasson (1987) produced the first cohort evidence [1].
- **Changing product:** street cannabis potency has risen (high-THC sinsemilla, resin), and CBD content has fallen [1].

### Epidemiology
- Cannabis use is **2–3× higher** in first-episode psychosis than in the general population.
- **EU-GEI (Di Forti 2019):**
  - daily use: OR about 3;
  - daily high-potency use: OR about 5;
  - population attributable fraction of first-episode psychosis 12% across sites, **30% London, 50% Amsterdam** [general knowledge; check].
- **India:** cannabis-induced psychosis is common in de-addiction and emergency settings (bhang and ganja). PGIMER and NIMHANS case series describe it [Likely].

### Classification
- **ICD-11 (6C41):**
  - episode of harmful use;
  - harmful pattern;
  - **dependence**;
  - intoxication;
  - withdrawal;
  - **cannabis-induced psychotic disorder**, mood disorder and anxiety disorder;
  - delirium.
- **DSM-5-TR:** cannabis use disorder (11 criteria; mild 2–3, moderate 4–5, severe ≥6); cannabis intoxication; **cannabis withdrawal**; cannabis-induced psychotic disorder.

### Mechanism (expanded) [1]
1. **Dopamine:**
   - CB1 activation on GABA interneurons in the VTA causes disinhibition, which increases mesolimbic dopamine;
   - but chronic users show **reduced** dopamine synthesis capacity (Bloomfield 2014), so the model is not simple hyperdopaminergia.
2. **Aberrant salience:** THC alters striatal and prefrontal salience processing (Bhattacharyya 2012).
3. **Neurodevelopment:** adolescent THC disrupts interneuron maturation, synaptic pruning and white matter development.
4. **Endogenous endocannabinoid dysregulation:** elevated CSF anandamide in prodrome and acute schizophrenia (possibly compensatory, since it correlates inversely with symptoms).
5. **Cross-sensitisation** with stress and trauma.

### Clinical features: distinguishing types
| Feature | Acute intoxication | Cannabis-induced psychotic disorder | Primary schizophrenia with cannabis use |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Duration | Hours | Days to weeks | Persistent |
| Consciousness | Clear or mildly altered | May be clouded | Clear |
| Hallucinations | Perceptual distortions | Visual > auditory | Auditory |
| Insight | Usually preserved | Partial | Poor |
| Resolution on abstinence | Rapid | Within a month | No |

### Assessment
- **History:** age at first use, frequency, potency, route; bhang, ganja, charas, synthetic.
- **Collateral history; family history of psychosis.**
- **Urine immunoassay:** THC-COOH detectable for 3 days (occasional use) to 30 or more days (heavy chronic use). Synthetic cannabinoids are **not detected**.
- **Organic work-up:** first-episode psychosis protocol.
- **Scales:** CUDIT-R, Cannabis Withdrawal Scale, PANSS.

### Differential diagnosis
- Primary schizophrenia, schizophreniform disorder, mania.
- Synthetic cannabinoid psychosis.
- Other substances: stimulants, hallucinogens, alcohol withdrawal.
- Delirium (infection, head injury).
- Organic psychosis (temporal lobe epilepsy, encephalitis).

### Management (expanded)
- **Antipsychotic choice:** no specific evidence. Use an SGA at low dose. Olanzapine and risperidone have been studied in small trials [general knowledge].
- **Duration:** taper after 1–3 months of remission if diagnosis remains substance-induced; continue if conversion is suspected.
- **Psychosocial treatment:**
  - **integrated treatment** of psychosis and substance use;
  - **contingency management** has the best evidence for reducing cannabis use in psychosis;
  - MI;
  - CBT (CAPS trial: limited effect) [general knowledge].
- **Family psychoeducation.**
- **Relapse prevention:** identify triggers and peer networks.

### Critical appraisal
- **For causation:** the cohort evidence is consistent, with temporality, dose–response and experimental evidence.
- **Against or uncertain:**
  - **schizophrenia incidence has not risen in line with cannabis use** in some countries (UK modelling, Hickman 2007), though potency changes complicate this;
  - residual confounding (other drugs, childhood adversity, genetic correlation: schizophrenia polygenic risk predicts cannabis use);
  - **Mendelian randomisation** shows bidirectional effects;
  - candidate-gene findings are unreliable (COMT not replicated).
- **Conclusion:** the best fit is **component cause** (Rothman sufficient-component model) [1].

### Indian context
- **Bhang** is culturally sanctioned and legal under state excise laws, so cannabis-related psychosis presentations peak around festivals (Holi, Shivratri) [Guessing].
- **High prevalence** in Punjab, Uttar Pradesh and the northeast; synthetic cannabinoids are emerging in metros.
- **Treatment services:** NDDTC-AIIMS, PGIMER and NIMHANS; Drug Treatment Clinics under NAPDDR; Tele-MANAS.
- **Research:** Basu (PGIMER) and colleagues have contributed theoretical work on cannabis–psychosis links [1].

### Recent advances
- **Hjorthøj 2023 (*Psychological Medicine*):** in Denmark, up to **30% of schizophrenia cases in men aged 21–30** may be attributable to cannabis use disorder [general knowledge; check].
- **CBD as antipsychotic:** a phase II adjunctive trial was positive (McGuire 2018); larger trials are pending.
- **THC potency regulation** and labelling in legal markets.
- **Polygenic risk** and machine-learning prediction of conversion from substance-induced psychosis.
