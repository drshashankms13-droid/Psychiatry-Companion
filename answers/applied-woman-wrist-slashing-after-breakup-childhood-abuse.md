## Exam answer

**Case summary.** A 26-year-old woman presents to the emergency department after **slashing her wrist following a break-up**. Since then she feels **worthless** and cries incessantly. She describes **frequent fights and anger outbursts** with her boyfriend over trivial issues, during which she **"zones out"** (dissociation). Work is affected and her employer has reprimanded her. She was **sexually abused by her uncle in childhood**, which returns as **frequent nightmares**, and she **wakes easily** (hyperarousal).

**Working formulation:** a self-harm episode after an interpersonal loss in a woman with chronic emotional and relational instability, dissociation under stress and trauma-related symptoms. The main contenders are **borderline personality disorder**, **complex PTSD** (or PTSD) and a **depressive episode**, which commonly coexist [1, 2].

### 1. Further clarifications needed in history

**The current episode and risk**
- Medical seriousness of the wound; was it intended to die, to relieve tension, or to communicate? Planning, concealment, alcohol or drugs at the time.
- Ongoing suicidal ideation, plans, access to means; previous self-harm and attempts; final acts.

**Personality features (BPD)** [1]
- Fears of abandonment and frantic efforts to avoid it; pattern of intense, unstable relationships with idealisation and devaluation.
- Identity disturbance; chronic emptiness; impulsivity (spending, sex, substances, binge eating, reckless driving).
- Mood reactivity lasting hours; intense anger; stress-related paranoia or dissociation.
- Onset by adolescence and pervasiveness across work, family and friendships (collateral history).

**Trauma-related features (PTSD and complex PTSD)** [2]
- Age, duration and nature of abuse; disclosure and response of the family; ongoing contact with the uncle.
- Re-experiencing in the present: flashbacks, intrusive images, nightmares with a sense of reliving.
- Avoidance of reminders; persistent sense of threat, hypervigilance, startle.
- **Disturbances in self-organisation:** affect dysregulation, stably negative self-concept (shame, worthlessness), difficulty sustaining closeness.

**Dissociation**
- Nature of "zoning out": depersonalisation, derealisation, amnesia for episodes, time loss, identity alteration (dissociative identity disorder, partial DID), dissociative seizures.

**Mood**
- Duration of worthlessness and crying before and after the break-up; anhedonia, sleep, appetite, energy, concentration, guilt; past depressive or hypomanic episodes.

**Other**
- Alcohol and substance use; eating disorder symptoms (bulimia is common in BPD) [1].
- Medical history, medications, pregnancy, contraception.
- Family history of mood disorder, suicide, personality disorder, substance use.
- Social: work situation, finances, support, current safety (including intimate partner violence).

### 2. Differential diagnosis

| Diagnosis | For | Against / check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Borderline personality disorder** (ICD-11 personality disorder with borderline pattern) | Self-harm after break-up, anger outbursts over trivial issues, unstable relationship, dissociation under stress, worthlessness [1] | Requires pervasive, long-standing pattern from adolescence |
| **Complex PTSD** (ICD-11 6B41) | Prolonged childhood sexual abuse, nightmares, hyperarousal, affect dysregulation, negative self-concept, relationship difficulty [2] | Requires re-experiencing in the present, avoidance and sense of threat; self-concept stably negative; abandonment fears not central |
| **PTSD** | Nightmares, easy waking | Without DSO features |
| **Depressive episode** | Worthlessness, crying after loss | Duration under 2 weeks? Pervasive low mood and biological features? |
| **Adjustment disorder** | Clear stressor (break-up) | Does not explain long-standing pattern |
| **Dissociative disorder** (depersonalisation–derealisation, DID, partial DID) | Zoning out | Extent, amnesia, identity alteration |
| **Bipolar II disorder** | Mood instability, anger | Discrete hypomanic episodes |
| **Substance-related disorder** | Can cause or worsen all of the above | History and screening |
| **Histrionic or dependent PD** | Interpersonal focus | Self-destructiveness and identity disturbance point to BPD [1] |

Complex PTSD and BPD can be diagnosed together in ICD-11 when criteria for both are met [2].

### 3. Approach to management

**A. Emergency phase**
- Surgical care of the wound; tetanus prophylaxis; treat any overdose or intoxication.
- Non-judgemental **risk assessment**; brief admission only if imminent risk or unsafe environment.
- **Safety plan** with warning signs, coping steps, contacts and **means restriction**; Tele-MANAS (14416).
- Document; attempted suicide is no longer an offence (MHCA 2017 Section 115; BNS 2023), so the focus is care.

**B. Assessment and formulation (first weeks)**
- Structured assessment: SCID-5-PD or ZAN-BPD, **International Trauma Questionnaire**, Dissociative Experiences Scale, PHQ-9, AUDIT.
- Shared formulation linking childhood trauma, emotional vulnerability, invalidation and current self-harm and dissociation [3].
- Psychoeducation for her (and, with consent, a trusted family member) about BPD and trauma responses as understandable and treatable.

**C. Psychological treatment (core)**
- **Stage 1: stabilisation and safety, DBT** [3]:
  - Targets: self-harm and suicidal behaviour → therapy-interfering behaviour → quality-of-life issues (work, relationships, sleep).
  - Skills: mindfulness and grounding for dissociation; distress tolerance; emotion regulation for anger; interpersonal effectiveness for relationships.
  - Diary cards, chain analysis of the wrist-slashing episode, phone coaching.
- **Stage 2: trauma processing** once self-harm is controlled: **DBT-PE/DBT-PTSD**, trauma-focused CBT, cognitive processing therapy or EMDR; for complex PTSD, STAIR followed by narrative work [2, 3].
- Alternatives: **MBT** for relational instability and mentalizing failures [4]; schema therapy.

**D. Pharmacological treatment (adjunctive)**
- No medication treats BPD itself; use targeted, time-limited prescribing [1].
- **SSRI** (sertraline or paroxetine) for depressive or PTSD symptoms.
- **Prazosin** may be tried for trauma nightmares (mixed evidence).
- Short-term low-dose SGA (e.g. quetiapine or aripiprazole) only for severe anger or transient psychotic-like symptoms [1].
- Avoid benzodiazepines and TCAs; dispense limited quantities [1].

**E. Social and occupational interventions**
- Liaison with employer (with consent) for leave and graded return; workplace adjustments.
- Address safety from the abuser (contact, family dynamics); legal options if she wishes (POCSO applies only to offences against children at the time; adult survivors may consult legal services).
- Relationship skills; couple or family sessions if helpful.

**F. Follow-up**
- Regular appointments with one coordinating clinician; review of risk and crisis plan; treat comorbid substance use and sleep problems.

**Conclusion.** This woman most likely has borderline personality disorder with probable complex PTSD after childhood sexual abuse, presenting with self-harm after interpersonal loss. Management starts with medical care and a non-judgemental risk assessment and safety plan, then a staged psychological approach: DBT for self-harm, dissociation and emotion regulation, followed by trauma-focused therapy, with targeted medication and occupational support.

## In depth

### Background

- Childhood sexual abuse is reported by 40–70% of people with BPD, but the association is moderate (r = 0.28), so abuse is one contributor among many [3].
- Complex PTSD was introduced in ICD-11 to capture survivors of prolonged trauma with disturbances in self-organisation; it replaces ICD-10 enduring personality change after catastrophic experience [2].

### Distinguishing complex PTSD from BPD

| Feature | Complex PTSD | BPD |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Trauma | Required | Common, not required |
| Self-concept | Stably negative | Unstable identity |
| Relationships | Avoidant, distant | Intense, unstable, idealisation–devaluation |
| Abandonment fears | Not core | Core |
| Self-harm | Possible | Frequent |
| Mood shifts | Persistent dysregulation | Rapid reactivity over hours |

In this case, intense relationship conflict and self-harm after a break-up point toward BPD, while nightmares and hyperarousal point toward a trauma disorder; both may be present.

### Mechanism in detail

- **Biosocial model:** emotional vulnerability + invalidating environment (including abuse and non-protective responses) → emotion dysregulation; self-harm relieves unbearable emotion; dissociation is an escape when arousal is overwhelming [3].
- **Trauma model:** fear conditioning, poorly integrated trauma memories and a sense of current threat; shame and worthlessness from abuse.
- **Mentalizing model:** attachment stress (break-up) collapses mentalizing, producing impulsive self-harm [4].

### Assessment in detail

| Tool | Purpose |
| --- | --- |
| International Trauma Questionnaire | ICD-11 PTSD and complex PTSD |
| CAPS-5 / PCL-5 | DSM-5 PTSD |
| Dissociative Experiences Scale | Dissociation |
| ZAN-BPD / SCID-5-PD | BPD |
| C-SSRS | Suicide risk |
| AUDIT / ASSIST | Substance use |

### Management details

- **DBT-PTSD and DBT with prolonged exposure (Harned)** allow trauma processing once self-harm has been controlled for a period, avoiding the risk of worsening self-harm by early exposure [3].
- **Validation** of the emotional response to the break-up while working on alternatives to self-harm (e.g. "It makes sense that you felt devastated, and cutting is not effective") [3].
- **Grounding skills** (5-4-3-2-1 senses, paced breathing, naming the present setting) for dissociation.

### Evidence

| Study | Finding |
| --- | --- |
| DBT RCTs | Reduced self-harm and suicide attempts in women with BPD [3] |
| Harned et al., 2010 | DBT as precursor to PTSD treatment in self-harming women [3] |
| MBT trials | Reduced self-harm, sustained benefit [4] |
| ITQ validation studies | Support distinct PTSD and complex PTSD profiles |

### Critical appraisal

- Labelling a trauma survivor with BPD can feel blaming; framing symptoms as adaptations to trauma improves engagement.
- Overlap between complex PTSD and BPD remains debated; treatment choice matters more than the label in the early phase.
- Trauma-focused therapy delivered too early in an actively self-harming patient may destabilise; staged models address this [3].

### Indian context

- Childhood sexual abuse is under-reported, and disclosure may be met with disbelief or pressure to remain silent within the family.
- Stigma around divorce, break-ups and premarital relationships can intensify shame.
- Women's safety at home and at work must be assessed; links with one-stop crisis centres (Sakhi), women's helplines (181) and legal aid where needed.
- Trained trauma therapists are scarce; DBT skills groups and Tele-MANAS provide partial access.

### Recent advances

- DBT-PTSD trials show effectiveness for complex PTSD after childhood abuse.
- Brief, intensive trauma-focused treatments and online trauma-focused CBT.
