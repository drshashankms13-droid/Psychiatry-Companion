## Exam answer

**Introduction.**
- "Brain damage" can mean **structural change** (volume loss, enlargement of nuclei), **lasting functional injury** (irreversible movement disorders, cognitive decline) or **neurotoxicity** at cellular level.
- The question matters because antipsychotics are taken for years: maintenance after a first episode is recommended for **1–2 years**, often longer [2].

### Evidence FOR the statement
**1. Brain volume loss after the first episode [1]:**
- Serial MRI shows "a **general loss of brain substance (grey more than white)**" after the first episode, like an accelerated normal age-related decline.
- Lishman notes that "currently attention is focusing on the possible effects of medications, particularly **typical or first-generation antipsychotics** (Lieberman et al. 2005)".

**2. Structural change in the striatum [1]:**
- In contrast to reduced cerebral volume, the **striatum (especially the caudate) is enlarged**. "This has been attributed to treatment with **conventional neuroleptic drugs**."
- Harrison's table rates "**enlarged basal ganglia secondary to antipsychotic medication**" as strong (+++) evidence.

**3. Tardive dyskinesia: persistent, sometimes irreversible injury [1, 2]:**
- **10–20%** of people given antipsychotics for a year or more develop clinically appreciable TD, more in the elderly. **5–10%** have functional impairment from it [1].
- Course: about **half stable**, one-quarter fluctuating, **11% improved**, 8% worse. In tardive syndromes, up to half improve but **remission is under 15%** [1].
- TD appears in about **5% of patients per year of exposure** [2]. Reversibility after stopping "is unclear" [2].
- **Mechanism suggesting cell death (Cadet and Lohr; Gunne and Andren) [1]:**
  - loss of **GABAergic pallidothalamic neurones** through **free radicals or excitotoxicity**, causing apoptosis;
  - neuroleptics **increase catecholamine turnover** (more free radicals) and cause **iron accumulation in the basal ganglia**;
  - antioxidants may help (mixed reports).
  - For irreversible TD, "structural or neurotoxic changes in the neurones … **must nevertheless be postulated**".
- **Tardive akathisia** and **tardive dystonia** also persist after the drugs are stopped [1].

**4. Receptor changes that outlast treatment [1, 2]:**
- In animals, prolonged blockade gives way to **dopamine receptor supersensitivity** that persists for months after withdrawal [1].
- Relapse clusters soon after abrupt cessation (60% of relapses over 4 years occurred within 3 months of stopping). The withdrawal process itself may cause relapse [2].

**5. Cognition and negative symptoms [1, 2]:**
- Negative and cognitive symptoms "may even be **exacerbated** by antipsychotic treatment" in some circumstances [2].
- **High-dose antipsychotic** medication is a cause of **secondary negative symptoms** [2].
- In dementia, drugs with **anticholinergic** actions (including some antipsychotics) may **hasten cognitive decline** [1].

**6. Harm in the vulnerable brain [2]:** in dementia, antipsychotics **increase mortality** and carry a warning of **cerebrovascular events**. Haloperidol has higher mortality, with a dose–response relationship.

### Evidence AGAINST, or confounders
**1. Brain abnormalities exist before treatment [1]:**
- Ventricular enlargement and cortical volume loss are present in **first-episode** patients (+++).
- Cognitive deficits of **1–3 SD** are present **from the first episode** and in **drug-free** patients.
- **Premorbid deficits** in development and IQ are among "the few consistent findings".
- So much of the brain abnormality in schizophrenia **is not caused by drugs**.

**2. Much post-onset decline has other causes [1]:** "lost opportunities, physical illness, substance misuse, medication, lack of motivation, social isolation and selection factors". Medication is only one of several.

**3. No classic degenerative pathology [1]:**
- **Absence of gliosis** (+++). Volume loss reflects **reduced neuropil and neuronal size, not neuronal loss**.
- **AD is not commoner** in schizophrenia.
- **No convincing pathology** has been found to explain even irreversible TD.

**4. Causation versus association:**
- Progressive volume loss could reflect **illness progression**: Lishman calls it a possible "strand of evidence in favour of a neurodegenerative element".
- TD risk is also raised by **age, diabetes, smoking, pre-existing brain damage, alcohol misuse, affective illness and organic disorders** [1]. Vulnerable brains may be the ones that develop TD.

**5. Benefits of long-term treatment are substantial [2]:**
- **First episode:** relapse **26% on maintenance vs 61% on placebo** at 6–12 months. Withdrawal after 1–2 years led to **almost 80%** relapse at 1 year and **98%** at 2 years in one study.
- **Swedish population study:** longer treatment, **lower hospitalisation** (5 years' treatment halved the rate).
- **Earlier effective treatment** is linked to **fewer negative symptoms** over time (with caveats about duration of untreated psychosis).
- **Long term:** the 5-year follow-up of a dose-reduction RCT found **better functional outcomes** with dose reduction or discontinuation. The study has been heavily criticised, and other studies show disastrous outcomes after stopping. "There remains more evidence in favour of continuing antipsychotics than for stopping them."

**6. Drug class matters [1, 2]:**
- Concern centres on **first-generation** drugs [1].
- **TD incidence is lower with SGAs** [2]. Most physicians now prefer atypicals first line to prevent movement disorders [1].
- **Clozapine** is the antipsychotic most likely to be associated with **resolution** of TD [2].

### Synthesis
- **Partly true.**
  - Long-term antipsychotics, especially **FGAs at high doses**, are associated with **striatal enlargement**, possibly some **grey-matter loss**, and **persistent tardive syndromes** with a plausible **neurotoxic** mechanism.
  - In **dementia**, they cause harm (mortality, stroke risk, possible cognitive acceleration through anticholinergic effects).
- **Overstated as a general claim.**
  - Much of the brain abnormality in schizophrenia **precedes treatment**.
  - There is **no gliosis or neuronal loss** pathology.
  - Confounders are many.
  - Untreated or relapsing illness carries its own serious harms.
- **Practical implications [1, 2]:**
  - use the **lowest effective dose**;
  - prefer drugs with **lower EPS and TD liability**;
  - monitor for TD (AIMS);
  - review long-term need, and if stopping, **taper slowly** (hyperbolic tapering);
  - avoid antipsychotics in BPSD unless distress is severe or the risk is serious.

**Conclusion.** The evidence supports antipsychotic-related **structural and functional brain changes**, most clearly **tardive dyskinesia** and **basal ganglia enlargement** with FGAs. It does not show that long-term antipsychotics cause a degenerative "brain damage" in the usual sense. Many abnormalities are intrinsic to the illness, and the benefits of maintenance are large. The statement should be qualified to: "**long-term, high-dose, first-generation antipsychotic exposure carries a risk of persistent neurological harm, which must be weighed against the harms of relapse**".

## In depth

### Risk factors for tardive dyskinesia [1]
| Factor | Note |
|---|---|
| Age | Higher rate in the elderly |
| Pre-existing brain damage | ECT, leucotomy, cognitive dysfunction, negative symptoms; less uniformly upheld |
| Acute dyskinesia history | Raised risk |
| Organic psychiatric disorder | Especially high prevalence |
| Diabetes | Raised risk |
| Alcohol misuse | About 3-fold |
| Affective disorder | Possibly more than schizophrenia |
| Genetics | D3 receptor variant; CYP2D6 mutant homozygosity (single studies) |
| Imaging | Facial dyskinesia linked with ventricular enlargement on CT |

### TD management principles [2]
- Stop the anticholinergic.
- Reduce the antipsychotic dose, or switch to one with lower TD liability (data conflicting).
- **Clozapine** (most likely to resolve TD), possibly quetiapine.
- **Valbenazine or deutetrabenazine** as add-ons; some evidence for tetrabenazine and *Ginkgo biloba*.
