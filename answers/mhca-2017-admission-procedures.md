## Exam answer

**Introduction.** The **Mental Healthcare Act (MHCA) 2017**:
- came into force on **29 May 2018**;
- has **16 chapters and 126 sections**;
- replaced the Mental Health Act 1987, to align Indian law with the **UN Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities (UNCRPD, ratified 2007)** [1, 2].

**Chapter XII (Sections 85–98)** governs **admission, treatment and discharge**. Its core principles [1, 2]:
- **Presumption of capacity** (Section 4).
- **Least restrictive** care.
- **Supported decision-making** through the **nominated representative (NR)**.
- Review of non-voluntary admissions by the **Mental Health Review Board (MHRB)**.

**Capacity (Section 4):** the person can **understand** relevant information, **appreciate** the reasonably foreseeable consequences of a decision, and **communicate** the decision [1].

### Types of admission

| Type | Section | Who / criteria | Key safeguards and durations |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Independent admission (adult)** | **85–86** | Person with capacity requests admission; needs treatment; the MHE judges admission necessary | Patient consents to treatment; can seek **discharge at any time** (Section 88). The MHP may **delay discharge up to 24 h** to assess for conversion to supported admission [1] |
| **Independent admission of a minor** | **87** | Request by **NR/guardian**; minor's views considered; all community options exhausted | Minor kept separate from adults; NR stays with the minor; **MHRB informed within 72 h**; MHRB reviews if admission exceeds **30 days** [1, 2] |
| **Supported admission, up to 30 days** | **89** | Application by **NR**. **Two assessments** (psychiatrist + MHP/medical practitioner) within 7 days find: severe mental illness **and** (a) recent threat or attempt of **bodily harm to self**, or (b) **violent behaviour or harm to others**, or (c) **inability to care for self** to a degree that places the person at risk; **and** lack of capacity to make treatment decisions | Treatment with NR consent, considering any **advance directive**. Admission reported to the MHRB within **3 days for women and minors** and **7 days for others**. **Capacity reviewed** periodically; becomes independent when capacity returns. Person or NR can appeal to the MHRB, which decides within **7 days** [1] |
| **Supported admission beyond 30 days (high support needs)** | **90** | Continued criteria certified by **two psychiatrists**; application to MHRB | **MHRB permission** needed; maximum **90 days** first time, then **120 days**, then **180 days** each time. Capacity reviewed fortnightly (at least every 14 days). MHRB decides within **21 days** [1] |
| **Emergency treatment (not an admission)** | **94** | Any registered medical practitioner, in a health establishment or the community, when **immediately necessary** to prevent death or irreversible harm, serious harm to self or others, or serious damage to property, **if the behaviour flows from mental illness** | NR consent where available. Lasts up to **72 h** or until assessed at an MHE (whichever is earlier); **ECT not allowed** as emergency treatment [1] |
| **Police and court routes** | **100–105** | Police take a wandering or at-risk person to a public health establishment **within 24 h**; magistrate orders; prisoners | No judicial order needed for admission or discharge of homeless persons; reintegration via police or social welfare [1] |

### Supporting provisions
- **Nominated representative (Sections 14–17):**
  - Appointed by the person (or by advance directive).
  - If none is appointed, the order of precedence is relative → carer → person appointed by the MHRB → **Director of Social Welfare**.
  - Duties: consider the person's wishes, life history and best interests; apply for admission; take part in discharge planning [1].
- **Restraint (Section 97):** physical restraint only as the last resort for imminent harm, authorised by a psychiatrist, recorded, and the **NR informed within 24 h**. **Seclusion is prohibited** [1].
- **Prohibited treatments (Section 95):** unmodified ECT; **ECT for minors** (except with guardian consent and MHRB permission); sterilisation as treatment; chaining [1].
- **Discharge planning (Section 98)** for all admissions; **leave of absence (Section 91)** [1].
- **Forms:** Rules 2018 prescribe standard forms for each admission type and for discharge. Examples include Form G for discharge of an independent patient and Form H for discharge of a minor [1].

### Compared with the Mental Health Act 1987
| MHA 1987 | MHCA 2017 |
| --- | --- |
| Voluntary, special-circumstance admission (90 days), reception orders by magistrate | Independent, supported (≤30 days / >30 days), minor admission |
| Judicial (magistrate) involvement in involuntary admission | Clinical decision, reviewed by the quasi-judicial **MHRB** |
| No capacity concept | Capacity-based; advance directives; NR |
| Custodial focus | Rights-based (Chapter V rights) |

**Conclusion.** MHCA 2017 changed admission from a custodial, magistrate-driven process to a **capacity-based, rights-oriented** one with supported decision-making and MHRB oversight. The safeguards are strong, but the **paperwork, short time limits, scarce MHRBs and dependence on capacity assessment** have made admissions more cumbersome in practice. Faithful implementation needs functioning boards, trained staff and a clear role for families.

## In depth

### Background
- **Lunacy Acts of 1858** → **Indian Lunacy Act 1912** (custodial; "asylum" became "mental hospital"; first defined admission and certification procedures) → **Mental Health Act 1987** (passed 1987, implemented 1993; 10 chapters, 98 sections) [1].
- **The Bhore Committee (1946)** criticised the ILA. The **IPS** proposed a Mental Health Bill as early as 1949 [1].
- **UNCRPD (2006):** India signed and ratified it on 1 October 2007, which required harmonisation of national law [1].
- **Drafting:** led by the Centre for Mental Health Law and Policy, ILS Pune (Pathare and Sagade). First draft 2010; Bill 2013; passed 2017; notified **29 May 2018** [1, 2].

### Epidemiology: why admission law matters
- NMHS 2015–16 found current prevalence of 10.6% and a treatment gap of 70–92% [2].
- Most admissions in India are voluntary or family-initiated. Families commonly accompany inpatients.
- Hospitals have reported a rise in "supported admissions" and in documentation burden since 2018. Evidence is mostly experiential and from surveys.

### Classification and criteria
- **"Mental illness" (Section 2(s)):** a substantial disorder of thinking, mood, perception, orientation or memory that **grossly impairs** judgement, behaviour, capacity to recognise reality, or ability to meet the ordinary demands of life. It includes conditions associated with alcohol and drug abuse and **excludes intellectual disability** [1, 2].
- **Determination of mental illness (Section 3):** by nationally or internationally accepted medical standards (ICD). Section 3 and Section 2(s) are in tension [2].
- **Mental health establishment:** any public or private, allopathic or AYUSH establishment, including general hospitals, where persons with mental illness are admitted. Family homes are excluded [1].

### Mechanism in detail: the decision pathway
Presentation → is admission needed? → **capacity assessment** (understand / appreciate / communicate) →
- **Has capacity and agrees** → independent admission (Section 86).
- **Lacks capacity and meets risk or self-care criteria** → supported admission (Section 89), with NR consent and advance directive checked.
- **Needs care beyond 30 days** → Section 90 with MHRB permission.
- **Minor** → Section 87.
- **Immediate danger** → emergency treatment (Section 94) for up to 72 h while arranging assessment.

### Clinical features in detail: practical scenarios
1. **Acute mania refusing admission:** assess capacity → supported admission (Section 89) through the family as NR → inform the MHRB.
2. **Depressed patient who wants to leave against advice:** independent patient can be held for up to 24 h for assessment → convert to Section 89 if criteria are met.
3. **Wandering homeless person:** police (Section 100) → public health establishment within 24 h.
4. **Adolescent with psychosis:** Section 87, with a guardian staying in the ward; MHRB informed.

### Assessment in detail: capacity
- Use structured tools (e.g., the **MacCAT-T**, or the NIMHANS capacity assessment guidance) and document each of the three Section 4 limbs.
- **Capacity fluctuates**, so reassess regularly (fortnightly under Section 90).
- **Interpretive dispute:** Section 4 says a person has capacity if they can do (a) **or** (b) **or** (c). Math et al. argue the "or" should be "and"; otherwise most acutely ill patients would legally "have capacity" and could refuse treatment [2].

### Differential: admission type vs situation
| Situation | Correct route |
| --- | --- |
| Capacitous adult requesting care | Section 86 |
| Adult lacking capacity with risk | Section 89 → Section 90 |
| Minor | Section 87 |
| Imminent danger in the community or ED | Section 94 emergency treatment |
| Homeless or wandering person | Section 100 police |
| Prisoner with mental illness | Section 103 (prison MHE) |

### Management details: documentation and timelines
- **Timelines to remember:**
  - Delay of discharge: 24 h (Section 88).
  - Emergency treatment: 72 h (Section 94).
  - Section 89 admission: up to 30 days.
  - Section 90: 90/120/180 days.
  - MHRB decisions: 7 days (Section 89) and 21 days (Section 90).
  - Police: 24 h.
  - NR informed of restraint: 24 h [1].
- **Treatment under supported admission:** with NR consent and the advance directive, and with the patient's participation as far as possible.
- **Records:** forms, capacity notes, NR details, advance-directive check, MHRB intimation, restraint register.

### Critical appraisal
**Strengths** [1, 2]:
- Rights-based (Chapter V is the "heart and soul").
- Capacity and autonomy.
- Advance directives and NR.
- Bans unmodified ECT, chaining and seclusion.
- Decriminalises attempted suicide (Section 115).
- Regulates AYUSH and private establishments.

**Criticisms** [1, 2]:
- **Admission is more tedious:** people with psychosis often lack insight, and the supported-admission paperwork and time limits burden families and staff.
- **MHRBs** are not functional in many districts. Delays undermine both rights and treatment.
- **Family role undermined:** India's care depends on families, yet the Act treats families and professionals as potential rights violators.
- **No community treatment orders**, so there is no legal route for continuity after discharge.
- **Registration of every MHE** (including general hospitals) may deter general hospitals from admitting psychiatric patients, which contradicts NMHP integration.
- **Harsh penalties** without a test of intent could lead to defensive practice.
- **The definition** covers only "substantial" illness, so some argue the Act protects only a small fraction of patients (about 0.8%).
- **Suggested reforms:** hospital-level review boards; a 72-hour MHRB decision limit; a "family-accompanied admission" category; CTOs; amending Section 4 to "and" [2].

### Indian context
- **Implementation (2025–26):** central and state Mental Health Authorities have been constituted. MHRB coverage varies widely between states.
- **Court guidance:** the Supreme Court and High Courts have used MHCA provisions when issuing directions on homeless persons with mental illness and on long-stay patients in mental hospitals (check specific judgments before citing).
- **Linkages:** RPwD Act 2016 (disability rights); NDPS Act Section 64A (immunity for addicts seeking treatment); BNS 2023 (the new criminal code omits the IPC 309 equivalent, so attempted suicide is no longer a crime).

### Recent advances
- **Established:** online advance-directive registers (provided for in the Act); state MHRB constitution.
- **Emerging:** proposals for MHCA amendments (family-supported admission, CTOs); digital MHRB hearings; capacity-assessment training modules.
- **Note:** BNS 2023 (in force 1 July 2024) has no IPC 309 equivalent, so Section 115's presumption now operates in a setting where attempted suicide is not criminalised at all.
