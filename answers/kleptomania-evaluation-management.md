## Exam answer

### Definition

Kleptomania is an impulse-control disorder characterised by recurrent failure to resist impulses to steal objects that are not needed for personal use or monetary value. The behaviour is associated with mounting tension before the theft and pleasure, gratification or relief during or after it.

### Clinical features and diagnostic considerations

Theft is not primarily motivated by anger, revenge, delusion, hallucination, financial need or peer pressure. Objects may be discarded, given away or accumulated. The behaviour causes distress, impairment or legal consequences.

### Assessment

Obtain a detailed history of urges, triggers, frequency, consequences and attempts to resist. Assess comorbid depression, anxiety, substance use, bipolar disorder, OCD and other impulse-control problems. Evaluate legal, occupational and interpersonal consequences.

### Differential diagnosis

-   Ordinary theft for gain.
-   Conduct disorder or antisocial personality disorder.
-   Mania or psychosis.
-   Substance intoxication.
-   Neurocognitive disorders.
-   Other impulse-control disorders.

### Neurobiology

Proposed mechanisms involve impaired inhibitory control and reward circuitry, including frontostriatal pathways and serotonergic, dopaminergic and opioid systems. These models remain provisional.

### Management

-   Psychoeducation and a non-judgemental therapeutic alliance.
-   CBT, including trigger identification, cognitive restructuring and
    relapse prevention.
-   Behavioural strategies to reduce exposure to high-risk situations.
-   Treatment of comorbid psychiatric disorders.
-   Naltrexone has shown benefit in some trials, possibly by reducing
    urges and reward; use requires assessment of opioid use and hepatic
    considerations.
-   SSRIs have inconsistent evidence and may be considered for comorbid
    conditions rather than assumed to be specific treatment.
-   Address legal and social consequences sensitively.

### Conclusion

Kleptomania is a treatable but often under-recognised impulse-control disorder. Assessment of comorbidity and consequences, CBT-based interventions and selected pharmacotherapy may help reduce urges and recurrence.
