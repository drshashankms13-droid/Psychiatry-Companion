## Exam answer

**Definition.** Borderline personality disorder (BPD) is a pervasive pattern of instability of affect, self-image and relationships with marked impulsivity, beginning by early adulthood. DSM-5 requires **5 of 9** criteria: frantic efforts to avoid abandonment; unstable, intense relationships alternating between idealisation and devaluation; identity disturbance; impulsivity in at least two self-damaging areas; recurrent suicidal behaviour or self-harm; affective instability; chronic emptiness; intense anger; and stress-related paranoid ideation or dissociation [1]. In **ICD-11**, a personality disorder is graded mild, moderate or severe and may carry an optional **borderline pattern** qualifier [2].

### Mechanism

**Linehan's biosocial theory:** biological **emotional vulnerability** (high sensitivity, high reactivity, slow return to baseline) × an **invalidating environment** (criticism, trivialising of emotions, intermittent reinforcement of extreme displays) → pervasive **emotion dysregulation** → self-harm, impulsivity and chaotic relationships as attempts to regulate emotion [3].

Other models: **failure of mentalizing** under attachment stress (Bateman and Fonagy) [4]; identity diffusion, splitting and primitive defences (Kernberg) [1]; tripartite model of childhood trauma + vulnerable temperament + triggering events [1].

### Key facts

| Item | Figure |
| --- | --- |
| Prevalence | About **2%** general population; **10%** outpatients; **20%** inpatients; 30–60% of patients with personality disorders [1] |
| Sex ratio | About 75% of diagnosed cases are women (possible diagnostic bias) [1] |
| Familial risk | Five times more common in relatives of probands [1] |
| Childhood sexual abuse | Reported by 40–70%, but only a moderate association (r = 0.28) [3] |
| Suicide | Lifetime suicide mortality about **50 times** the general population [3] |
| Course | Instability greatest in young adulthood; improves in the 4th–5th decades [1] |

### Assessment

- Diagnosis from a longitudinal history, with **collateral information**; features must be pervasive and not confined to a mood episode or substance effect [1].
- **Suicide and self-harm risk assessment at every contact**; distinguish chronic risk from acute risk.
- Comorbidity: depression, bipolar disorder, substance use, eating disorders (especially bulimia), PTSD and complex PTSD, ADHD [1].
- Differential diagnosis: bipolar disorder (episodic, sustained mood change), complex PTSD (stable negative self, avoidant relationships), other personality disorders, identity problems of adolescence [1].
- Tools: SCID-5-PD, Zanarini Rating Scale for BPD, IPDE.

### Principles of management

1. **Psychotherapy is the primary treatment**; medication is adjunctive [1, 3].
2. **Structured, consistent, team-based care** with a written crisis plan.
3. **Validation with change:** take distress seriously while expecting progress.
4. **Avoid harm:** avoid polypharmacy, prolonged admissions and dismissive or punitive attitudes. Monitor countertransference [1].
5. **Hope:** the myth that BPD is untreatable becomes a self-fulfilling prophecy; most patients improve [1].

### Stepped management

| Stage | Interventions |
| --- | --- |
| **Crisis / stabilisation** | Risk assessment, safety plan, brief admission only for imminent danger, treat intoxication; short-term medication if needed [1] |
| **Engagement** | Psychoeducation about the diagnosis; agree simple concrete goals; treatment contract [1] |
| **Specialised psychotherapy** | **DBT** (best evidence), MBT, transference-focused psychotherapy (TFP), schema therapy, STEPPS |
| **Generalist care** | Good Psychiatric Management (GPM), structured clinical management (SCM) where specialist therapy is unavailable [4] |
| **Comorbidity** | Treat depression, PTSD, substance use and eating disorders in parallel |
| **Rehabilitation** | Work, education, relationships, social functioning |

### Specialised psychotherapies

| Therapy | Core idea | Format and evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **DBT** (Linehan) | Balance acceptance and change; skills for mindfulness, distress tolerance, emotion regulation, interpersonal effectiveness | Individual therapy + group skills + telephone coaching + consultation team, usually 1 year; more than 10 RCTs; first RCT 1991; Cochrane 2012 treatment of choice [3] |
| **MBT** (Bateman and Fonagy) | Strengthen the capacity to understand behaviour in terms of mental states | Individual + group; 18-month partial hospital RCT: number needed to treat about 2, benefit maintained at 8 years [4] |
| **TFP** (Kernberg) | Work through split object relations in the transference | Twice weekly, psychodynamic |
| **Schema therapy** (Young) | Change early maladaptive schemas and modes | Individual or group |
| **STEPPS** | Systems training for emotional predictability and problem solving | 20-week group adjunct |

### Pharmacotherapy

- **No drug is licensed or proven for BPD itself**; APA notes no single efficacious drug [3].
- Use only **time-limited, symptom-targeted** prescribing, preferably one drug, with limited quantities because of overdose risk.
- Symptom domains [1]:
  - **Affective instability, anger:** mood stabilisers (lamotrigine, valproate, lithium) or low-dose SGAs (aripiprazole, quetiapine).
  - **Impulsivity, affective aggression:** lithium, SSRIs, anticonvulsants; avoid benzodiazepines (disinhibition, dependence).
  - **Transient psychotic-like symptoms:** low-dose SGA during the episode only.
  - **Depression:** SSRIs; avoid **TCAs** (lethal in overdose, may worsen impulsivity) [1].
- Review and stop medication that has not clearly helped.

### Hospital admission

Brief, goal-directed and crisis-focused; prolonged admission may encourage regression and dependence. Admission under MHCA 2017 when there is serious risk and capacity is impaired.

### Prognosis

Symptomatic remission is common: in the McLean Study, most patients remitted over 10 years and relapse was uncommon, though social and vocational recovery lagged [5]. Impulsive symptoms remit fastest; affective symptoms (emptiness, loneliness) persist longest.

**Conclusion.** BPD is a treatable disorder of emotion regulation and mentalizing. Management rests on structured psychotherapy (DBT or MBT, or generalist models where specialists are scarce), consistent team care with crisis planning and risk assessment, careful time-limited medication for symptom domains, and avoidance of iatrogenic harm from polypharmacy and prolonged admission.

## In depth

### Background

- Stern (1938) used "borderline" for patients on the border between neurosis and psychosis. Knight (1953) described "borderline states". Kernberg (1967) defined **borderline personality organisation**: identity diffusion, primitive defences (splitting, projective identification) and generally intact reality testing. Gunderson and Singer (1975) operationalised the syndrome, leading to inclusion in DSM-III (1980).
- ICD-10 used **emotionally unstable personality disorder**, impulsive type and borderline type (F60.3). ICD-11 abolished specific types but kept a borderline pattern qualifier for continuity and access to specialised treatments [2].
- DBT was developed by Marsha Linehan from the late 1970s; the first RCT was published in 1991 and the manuals in 1993 [3].

### Epidemiology

- Diagnosis is more common in younger samples, suggesting a natural tendency toward maturation and remission [1].
- Familial aggregation includes antisocial personality disorder, substance misuse and mood disorders [1].
- Indian clinical samples report BPD among a substantial minority of young adults presenting with self-harm, often with comorbid depression; epidemiological data from India are limited.

### Classification and criteria

| System | Approach |
| --- | --- |
| DSM-5 Section II | 5 of 9 criteria (listed above) [1] |
| DSM-5 Section III (AMPD) | Impairment in self and interpersonal functioning + traits (emotional lability, anxiousness, separation insecurity, depressivity, impulsivity, risk taking, hostility) |
| ICD-10 | Emotionally unstable PD: impulsive type and borderline type |
| ICD-11 | Personality disorder (mild, moderate, severe) + trait domains (often negative affectivity, disinhibition) + optional borderline pattern [2] |

Linehan grouped the DSM criteria into five dysregulation domains [3]:

| Domain | Criteria |
| --- | --- |
| Emotion | Affective instability, anger |
| Behaviour | Impulsivity, self-injury |
| Cognition | Dissociation, paranoia |
| Interpersonal | Chaotic relationships, abandonment fears |
| Self | Identity disturbance, emptiness |

### Mechanism in detail

- **Temperament:** high harm avoidance with high novelty seeking (explosive temperament) and low self-directedness and cooperativeness in Cloninger's model [1].
- **Neurobiology:** amygdala hyperreactivity to emotional faces, reduced prefrontal (orbitofrontal, anterior cingulate) regulation, serotonergic dysfunction linked to impulsive aggression.
- **Invalidating environment** teaches that inner experiences are wrong, fostering self-distrust and reliance on others' cues; intermittent reinforcement of extreme displays maintains escalation [3].
- **Mentalizing failure:** under attachment stress, mentalizing collapses into pre-mentalizing modes (psychic equivalence, teleological mode, pretend mode), producing self-harm and interpersonal chaos [4].

### Assessment in detail

| Tool | Use |
| --- | --- |
| SCID-5-PD, IPDE | Structured diagnosis |
| ZAN-BPD | Severity and change |
| MSI-BPD | Screening |
| Columbia Suicide Severity Rating Scale | Suicide risk |
| Diary cards (DBT) | Daily mood, urges and behaviours [3] |

Collateral history is essential because patients may present differently across contexts [1].

### Differential diagnosis

| Condition | Distinguishing feature |
| --- | --- |
| Bipolar disorder | Episodic sustained mood change for days to weeks; grandiosity; reduced need for sleep; borderline mood shifts are reactive and last hours [1] |
| Major depression | Episodic; lacks abandonment fears, identity disturbance and chronic emptiness [1] |
| Complex PTSD | Stably negative self-concept, avoidant relationships, trauma required |
| ADHD | Childhood onset of inattention and hyperactivity |
| Histrionic PD | Attention seeking without self-destructiveness and identity disturbance [1] |
| Dependent PD | Appeasement rather than rage on abandonment [1] |
| Substance-induced states | Temporal link to use |

### Management details

**DBT target hierarchy:** life-threatening behaviours → therapy-interfering behaviours → quality-of-life-interfering behaviours [3].

**Generalist models:** GPM (Gunderson) and SCM emphasise psychoeducation, case management, crisis planning and consistent outpatient care; in the outpatient MBT trial, SCM was a strong comparator and patients with a single BPD diagnosis did as well with SCM [4].

**Medication evidence:** small trials support lamotrigine, topiramate, valproate and aripiprazole for specific symptoms, but larger trials have been negative (e.g. lamotrigine LABILE trial, 2018); NICE advises against drug treatment for BPD itself. Cloninger's symptom-domain table supports lithium and anticonvulsants for affective aggression and warns that benzodiazepines disinhibit [1].

**Crisis management:** validate distress, assess risk, agree short-term actions, involve supports, review the crisis plan; avoid escalating medication in crises.

**Self-harm wounds:** treat medically without punitive attitudes.

### Evidence table

| Study | Design | Finding |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Linehan et al., 1991 | RCT, DBT vs TAU | Fewer parasuicidal acts and admissions [3] |
| Linehan et al., 2006 | RCT, DBT vs therapy by experts | Halved suicide attempts [3] |
| Stoffers et al., 2012 (Cochrane) | Systematic review | DBT best supported psychotherapy [3] |
| Bateman and Fonagy (partial hospital MBT) | RCT, 18 months | NNT about 2; at 8 years suicidality 23% vs 74%, diagnosis 13% vs 87% [4] |
| Bateman and Fonagy (outpatient MBT vs SCM) | RCT | MBT superior, especially long term [4] |
| Zanarini et al., McLean Study | 10-year prospective | High remission, low relapse, poorer functional recovery [5] |
| Mehlum et al., 2014 | RCT, DBT-A | Reduced self-harm in adolescents [3] |

### Critical appraisal

- **Diagnosis:** heterogeneity (256 ways to meet 5 of 9 criteria), stigma and gender bias. ICD-11's severity model addresses some of this but its optional qualifier keeps the label [2].
- **Evidence:** most trials involve women in high-income countries; few include men, minorities or LMIC settings [3]. Dismantling studies have not established which DBT components are essential [3].
- **Specialist versus generalist:** in many settings, well-structured generalist care performs nearly as well as specialist therapies.
- **Medication:** widespread polypharmacy contrasts with weak evidence.
- **Functional recovery** lags behind symptomatic remission even after effective therapy [4, 5].

### Indian context

- BPD is under-diagnosed in India; self-harm in young women is often attributed to family conflict alone.
- Family involvement is central; family psychoeducation can reduce invalidation and expressed emotion.
- Few trained DBT or MBT therapists; adapted group DBT skills programmes and generalist models are more feasible. Tele-psychotherapy and Tele-MANAS can extend reach.
- **MHCA 2017 Section 115** removed criminal consequences of suicide attempts (and the BNS 2023 removed the general offence), supporting care-based responses to self-harm.

### Recent advances

- Brief and group-based DBT adaptations; DBT-A for adolescents; DBT-PTSD for comorbid trauma [3].
- MBT for adolescents and for antisocial personality disorder [4].
- ICD-11 severity-based diagnosis allows earlier recognition in youth.
- Ongoing trials of brexpiprazole and other agents; no approved drug as of 2024.
