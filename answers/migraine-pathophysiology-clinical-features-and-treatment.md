## Exam answer

### Definition and epidemiology [1]
- **Migraine** is a **familial** disorder of recurrent headache attacks, widely variable in intensity, frequency and duration:
  - commonly **unilateral**;
  - usually with **anorexia, nausea and vomiting**;
  - sometimes preceded or accompanied by **neurological and mood disturbance**.
- **Prevalence:** **1-year about 12%**; **lifetime about 16%**; **M:F about 1:3**.
- **Onset:** childhood or early adulthood (25% before 10; very few after 50). Childhood cyclical vomiting and abdominal pain may be precursors. **Family history in about two-thirds.**
- **Comorbidities:** epilepsy, Raynaud's, stroke, affective disorders.

### Diagnostic criteria: IHS 2004, migraine without aura (Box 8.7) [1]
- **A.** At least **5 attacks** fulfilling B–D.
- **B.** Lasting **4–72 hours** (untreated or unsuccessfully treated).
- **C.** At least **2 of:** unilateral; **pulsating**; **moderate or severe** intensity; **aggravated by or causing avoidance of routine physical activity**.
- **D.** At least **1 of:** **nausea and/or vomiting**; **photophobia and phonophobia**.
- **E.** Not attributed to another disorder.

**"POUNDing" mnemonic** (Detsky): **P**ulsating, duration **4–72 hOurs**, **U**nilateral, **N**ausea, **D**isabling. 4–5 of these make migraine very likely.

**IHS classification:**
1.1 without aura; 1.2 **with aura** (including familial hemiplegic and basilar-type); 1.3 childhood periodic syndromes; 1.4 retinal; 1.5 complications (**migrainous infarction**, migraine-triggered seizure).
- **Cluster headache** is a separate trigeminal autonomic cephalalgia.
- **Ophthalmoplegic "migraine"** is now a cranial neuralgia.

### Clinical features [1]
1. **Premonitory phase:** tiredness, poor concentration, **stiff neck**, irritability, **yawning, food cravings, increased energy**. Predicts an attack within 72 hours in 72% (Giffin).
2. **Aura** (about **one-third**):
   - builds over **5–20 minutes**, lasts **10–60 minutes**, then headache within the hour (sometimes no headache);
   - **visual:** **fortification spectra (teichopsia)**, shimmering zig-zags expanding from fixation; scotomata; micropsia/macropsia, "zoom" and "mosaic" vision;
   - sensory **paraesthesiae**; **dysphasia**; rarely hallucinations of taste, smell or hearing; **body-image disturbance**.
3. **Headache:**
   - usually **8–24 hours**; unilateral becoming diffuse; **throbbing**; worse on coughing or jolting;
   - **nausea, vomiting, photophobia, phonophobia**; autonomic signs (pallor, facial oedema, conjunctival injection);
   - patient lies down.
4. **Postdrome (resolution):** tired, "washed out", poor concentration, scalp tenderness; irritability, depression or occasionally euphoria.

**Variants:**
- **basilar-type** (about 10%): bilateral visual, vertigo, dysarthria, ataxia, perioral paraesthesiae, **gradual loss of consciousness**, occipital headache; women;
- **familial hemiplegic** (autosomal dominant): hemiparesis with sensory or language aura; confusion in a fifth; prolonged encephalopathic episodes; cerebellar signs in 20% of families;
- **aura without headache / "migraine equivalents"**: aura, vomiting, abdominal pain, drowsiness, mental episodes;
- **chronic ("transformed") migraine:** headache on **15 or more days a month** (more than 4 hours a day), often with **depression and over-medication**.

**Course:** lifelong with remissions; **relief in pregnancy in about 60%**; declines after 50; menstrual attacks.
**Precipitants:** fatigue or sleep, **stress** (especially sustained; "let-down" weekend attacks), foods, menstruation, weather, infection. These are not specific to migraine.

**Associations:**
- **ischaemic stroke** (especially young women with aura; absolute risk low); deep white-matter lesions about 4×; posterior-circulation silent infarcts in about 10%. Consider **CADASIL, MELAS**, PFO, SLE;
- **epilepsy** (about 6% of migraineurs; 24% of epileptics have migraine);
- **transient global amnesia**.

### Pathophysiology [1]
- **Old vascular theory** (vasoconstriction causing aura, vasodilatation causing headache): **discarded**.
- **Neuronal hyperexcitability:**
  - **familial hemiplegic migraine** mutations in ion-transport genes: **voltage-gated calcium channel (chromosome 19)**, Na⁺/K⁺ pump, **voltage-gated sodium channel**;
  - final common path: **excess K⁺/glutamate**, facilitating **cortical spreading depression**.
- **Cortical spreading depression (CSD) causes the aura:**
  - a wave of neuronal and glial **depolarisation** with reduced activity and blood flow spreading at **2–3 mm/minute**;
  - patients show a posterior-to-anterior **wave of oligaemia** (20–30%, coupled to reduced metabolism, **not ischaemia** on DWI), independent of vascular territories;
  - leading-edge **hyperaemia** gives the scintillations (fMRI BOLD).
- **Trigeminovascular system causes the headache:**
  - **meningeal nociceptor activation** with neuropeptide release (**CGRP**) → vasodilatation, plasma extravasation, mast-cell degranulation (**neurogenic inflammation**);
  - **5-HT** involvement (the basis of triptans and ergots);
  - **central sensitisation** → cutaneous **allodynia**.
- **Brainstem generator:**
  - **dorsolateral pons** activation on PET during attacks (persists after sumatriptan);
  - aminergic brainstem nuclei (trigeminal nucleus caudalis, superior salivatory nucleus) modulate the loop;
  - **lateralised activation** may explain unilateral pain;
  - premonitory symptoms reflect brainstem/hypothalamic dysfunction.

### Differential diagnosis [1]
- Sinus disease, glaucoma, hypertension, **raised intracranial pressure**; tension-type headache.
- **"Complicated" migraine:** angioma (deficits outlasting headache, always the same side); aneurysm (one-sided ophthalmoplegia); brainstem lesion (basilar). Investigate auras that first appear in adulthood or without headache.
- **TIA:** aura progresses slowly; TIA is abrupt.
- **Epilepsy:** migraine visual aura is slow and zig-zag; occipital epilepsy is sudden, brief, coloured and round. Migrainous loss of consciousness is gradual and followed by severe headache.
- **Psychiatric mimics:** delirium, transient paranoid reactions, bipolar swings, hysterical syncope (see the psychiatric comorbidity answer).

### Treatment [1]
**A. Acute attack:**
- **triptans** (selective **5-HT1B/1D agonists**; sumatriptan and newer agents):
  - effective **even several hours into the attack**; also relieve nausea and photophobia;
  - oral, **nasal spray or suppository** if vomiting;
  - **contraindicated** in **ischaemic heart disease, uncontrolled hypertension, pregnancy**;
  - interaction: an SSRI (e.g., fluoxetine) with a triptan can cause **serotonin syndrome** [2];
- **aspirin plus metoclopramide**;
- **ergotamine** (nasal or rectal);
- **CGRP antagonists:** promising (one trial).

**B. Prophylaxis:**
- **indication:** **2 or more attacks a month** regularly (Bigal & Lipton);
- **evidence-based, well tolerated:** **propranolol** (widely the **first choice**), **timolol**, **valproate**, **amitriptyline**;
- effective but with problematic side effects: **methysergide, pizotifen**.

**C. Non-pharmacological and psychological:**
- **regular sleep and meals**; judicious stress avoidance; dietary triggers;
- **detect and treat depression and anxiety**; **buspirone** may reduce headache in anxious migraineurs;
- explore work or relationship conflicts and the "function" of attacks (Sacks);
- **relaxation training** (enduring benefit), **behavioural techniques**, **hypnosis**.

**D. Chronic migraine:**
- address **over-medication** and depression;
- **occipital nerve stimulation** may help.

**Conclusion.** Migraine is a common (12% per year), female-predominant, familial disorder of recurrent unilateral, pulsating, disabling headache with nausea and photo- or phonophobia (IHS criteria). A third have a visual or sensory aura. The modern model is:
- neuronal hyperexcitability (ion-channel mutations in familial hemiplegic migraine);
- **cortical spreading depression** for the aura;
- **trigeminovascular activation with CGRP-mediated neurogenic inflammation** for the pain;
- a **dorsolateral pontine generator**.

Acute treatment is with **triptans** (avoid in IHD, uncontrolled hypertension, pregnancy), aspirin plus metoclopramide, or ergotamine. **Prophylaxis** (2 or more attacks a month) is propranolol first, then timolol, valproate or amitriptyline, with lifestyle regularisation, treatment of comorbid anxiety and depression, and relaxation or behavioural therapy. **Modern CGRP-targeted and other newer therapies are not covered by the source.**
