## Exam answer

### Definition and the problem of the concept [1]
- **Post-concussion syndrome (PCS)** is a cluster of symptoms after head injury:
  - **somatic:** headache, dizziness, fatigue, noise and light intolerance, insomnia;
  - **cognitive:** poor memory and concentration;
  - **emotional:** irritability, emotional instability, anxiety, depression.
  - Travel phobia, PTSD and chronic-fatigue-like states often coexist.
- **Criteria:**
  - **ICD-10:** a head injury plus **3 or more of 8** common symptoms;
  - **DSM-IV "post-concussional disorder"** (stricter): significant cerebral concussion, symptoms **over 3 months** that begin or worsen after injury, and **evidence of cognitive deficits**; excludes dementia due to the injury.
- **Why it is "unsatisfactory":**
  - definitions vary; there is no unique syndrome;
  - symptoms are **common without head injury** (chronic pain, depression, the general population);
  - it may lump separate disorders together (e.g., BPPV + migraine + episodic dyscontrol);
  - it overlaps with somatisation disorders;
  - the aetiology is disputed. Lewis (1942) called it "the bugbear of the clear-minded doctor and lawyer".
- **Severity:** PCS can follow injuries of **any severity** but is the main cause of disability after **mild** injury. Some symptoms (headache, dizziness without vertigo) are **commoner after milder injuries**.

### Natural history after mild TBI [1]
- **Mild TBI:** GCS 13–15 with any alteration of consciousness. Of 1000 attenders, 1 dies, 9 need intervention, and about 80 have CT abnormalities.
- **Early symptoms** (first week): headache, dizziness, fatigue, noise and light sensitivity are commoner than after limb injury; **fatigue, slowness and poor balance** discriminate best at 1 month. Emotional symptoms (anxiety, irritability) become prominent by **3 months**.
- **Persistence of complaints:** **25–65% at 3 months, 21–24% at 6 months, 14–18% at 1 year** (Jacobson). Multiple symptoms in about **2–5%** at 1 year after uncomplicated injury.
- **Cognition:**
  - deficits in speed, memory, executive function and attention **resolve by about 3 months** in unselected samples (effect size about 0.1 after 3 months);
  - **litigant and clinic samples** show effect sizes of about 0.75 (an **ascertainment** effect).
- **Sport:** most athletes recover in **days to a week or two**.
- **Loss of consciousness:** brief LOC affects testing only in the first minutes to a day.
- **Repeated concussion:** slower recovery (Gronwall & Wrightson); evidence is mixed.

### Aetiology [1]
**Lishman's (1988) model (applies to most patients):**
1. **Physiogenic nucleus:** cerebral dysfunction, even after mild injury, produces headache, dizziness and fatigue. These would normally **recede**.
2. **Psychological obstacles to resolution:**
   - worry and anxiety built around the symptoms;
   - attention focused on them by others and by protracted investigations;
   - domestic stress; **resentment** or blame about the accident;
   - **returning to work too early** or to an uncongenial job;
   - **depression**; **compensation conflict**.
3. Hence **organic factors dominate early, psychogenic factors when symptoms are prolonged** (confirmed by later reviews).

**Evidence for an organic contribution:**
- early neuropsychological slowing (PASAT);
- **S-100B**, early **CT/SPECT** abnormalities, dizziness or headache in casualty, **diplopia or anosmia** in the first 24 hours: all predict symptoms;
- SPECT, PET and evoked-potential changes in persistent PCS (not specific; seen in depression and PTSD too);
- diffuse axonal injury after mild TBI (post-mortem, animal models, DTI);
- **dizziness** predicts failure to return to work independently of distress.

**Evidence for a psychological contribution:**
- symptoms **unrelated to injury severity** (Russell & Smith; Lishman 1968); commoner without residual brain damage;
- predicted by **early anxiety, depression, horrific memories, fear of brain damage, blaming the employer**;
- **pre-injury** psychiatric history, neuroticism, low social support, lower IQ;
- **older age**, possibly female sex;
- latent onset; follows emotional shock without head trauma;
- rare with marked intellectual or neurological deficit;
- cultural expectation (Lithuania vs Canada); "good old days" bias.

**Compensation and litigation:**
- symptoms are **0.5 SD worse** in claimants, more so in mild injury;
- abrupt resolution on settlement is uncommon;
- **malingering** is possible (about 30% of personal-injury cases by neuropsychologists' estimate) but hard to distinguish from conversion;
- symptom-validity (forced-choice) testing; no-fault systems speed claim closure.

**Ruff's summary:** PCS can be caused by **neuropathology, psychopathology, secondary gain (reduced effort or malingering), or any combination**.

### Component symptoms [1]
- **Post-traumatic headache:**
  - **commoner after mild injury**: 52% at 48 hours, 27% at 1 week;
  - mostly **tension-type (75%)**, migraine (21%);
  - **exclude subdural haematoma** (CT/MRI), cervical spine or **occipital neuralgia** (responds to nerve block), scalp scar tenderness;
  - diffuse, unremitting, analgesic-resistant headache suggests a psychogenic basis;
  - poor prognosis after a few months.
- **Dizziness vs vertigo:**
  - true vertigo is often vestibular (about 50%; **BPPV**); exclude **orthostatic hypotension**;
  - non-specific dizziness is commoner after milder injury;
  - **phobic imbalance**.
- **Whiplash** (no head blow): neck pain and PCS-like symptoms; **late whiplash syndrome in 25–50%**. Predictors: neck-injury severity, pain, female sex, older age, early distress, litigation.

### Assessment [1]
1. **Thorough evaluation** of the contribution of brain injury: injury severity (GCS, PTA, imaging), neurology, **MRI once**, and neuropsychology with **effort/validity testing**.
2. **Separate the components:** BPPV, migraine, tension headache, occipital neuralgia, cervical injury, episodic dyscontrol, depression, PTSD, travel phobia, **hypopituitarism** (low mood).
3. **Psychosocial factors:** attribution, fault, litigation, family (over-solicitude), work.
4. Premorbid psychiatric history; alcohol.

### Treatment [1]
**1. Prevention (early management):**
- most mild injuries recover **without intervention**; **enforced bed rest is unhelpful**;
- **information, advice and reassurance** about expected symptoms and recovery:
  - not useful for all patients routinely (Wade 1997);
  - **reduces PCS and social disability at 6 months in those admitted** (Wade 1998; Mittenberg; Ponsford);
  - **a single assessment-and-advice session may suffice** (Paniak);
- avoid **protracted investigations** that focus attention on symptoms;
- explain residual symptoms early, without implanting new ones.

**2. Established PCS:**
- **Therapeutic alliance** based on thorough evaluation.
- **Education:** psychological factors can produce physical and cognitive symptoms (the **reattribution model** used in somatisation). **Management is the same whether symptoms are organic or psychological.**
- After a normal MRI, **stop the endless search for proof of brain damage**; minimise further investigation.
- **CBT** for persistent PCS reduced distress (Tiersky RCT).
- **Graded return to activity and work:**
  - with permission, liaise with the employer;
  - an initial period off work (a **3-month certificate** is a reasonable start; better to overestimate);
  - **graded part-time return** (Lishman's case: about 16 hours a week at first, full-time by 9 months);
  - but avoid blanket long convalescence for mild cases.
- **Mood, anxiety and PTSD:**
  - **SSRI** if depression or anxiety is prominent;
  - supportive psychotherapy; relaxation; **anger management** for irritability;
  - **trauma-focused CBT** for PTSD; early CBT for acute stress disorder prevents PTSD.
- **Headache:**
  - simple non-addictive analgesics; **valproate, propranolol, amitriptyline, sumatriptan** (uncontrolled data; inconsistent in practice); ergotamine if vascular;
  - local heat, local anaesthetic to tender sites, occipital nerve block;
  - CBT (case series);
  - remember that antidepressants themselves can cause headache.
- **Dizziness:** vestibular assessment and treatment of true vertigo/BPPV; address phobic avoidance.
- **Sleep:** sleep hygiene; zopiclone or trazodone; melatonin or low-dose amitriptyline.
- **Litigation:** **speedy resolution** is desirable where brain damage plays no identifiable part.
- **Family:** avoid overprotection and reinforcement of the invalid role.

**3. Whiplash:** rest, analgesia and a soft collar early, then **gradual mobilisation** to avoid the guarding–tension cycle.

**Conclusion.** PCS is a heterogeneous, poorly defined cluster of somatic, cognitive and emotional symptoms after head injury, typically mild injury. Early symptoms are largely organic and usually resolve within weeks to 3 months. Persistence beyond months mostly reflects psychological maintaining factors (anxiety, depression, attribution, litigation, premorbid vulnerability), per Lishman's model. Treatment rests on:
- early **information and reassurance**;
- a single thorough evaluation, then no endless investigation;
- **explanation using the reattribution model**;
- **graded return to activity and work**;
- **CBT**, SSRIs for comorbid mood and anxiety, targeted headache and vestibular treatment;
- prompt settlement of litigation.
