## Exam answer

**Definition.** Maintenance treatment is the continued, long-term use of antipsychotic medication **plus psychosocial interventions** after an acute episode remits. Its aims are to prevent relapse and rehospitalisation, consolidate recovery, reduce suicide and violence, and maximise functioning and quality of life, while limiting long-term adverse effects. The phases are acute → stabilisation (about 6 months) → stable/maintenance (years).

### Mechanism
Continued D2 blockade (60–80% occupancy) → prevents re-emergence of presynaptic striatal dopamine dysregulation under stress → fewer relapses → less relapse-related functional decline and treatment resistance. Against this: long-term blockade → D2 up-regulation/supersensitivity → withdrawal-associated relapse, tardive dyskinesia, metabolic harm [1].

### Core evidence on relapse

| Situation | Relapse on antipsychotic | Relapse off antipsychotic |
| --- | --- | --- |
| First episode, 6–12 months (placebo-controlled meta-analysis) | 26% | 61% [1] |
| First episode, 18–24 months (8 RCTs, 2018) | 35% | 61% [1] |
| First episode, stopping after 1–2 years (Gitlin 2001) | — | ~80% at 1 year, 98% at 2 years [1] |
| Multi-episode (Leucht 2012, 65 RCTs) | 27% at 1 year | 64% at 1 year |
| Depot vs oral (meta-analyses) | Relative risk of relapse 30% lower, absolute risk 10% lower with depot [1] | — |

### Assessment during maintenance
- **Symptoms and relapse signatures:** PANSS/BPRS, early warning signs (sleep loss, suspiciousness, withdrawal).
- **Function:** Personal and Social Performance scale (PSP), GAF.
- **Adherence:** up to 25% non-adherent 10 days after discharge, 50% at 1 year, 75% at 2 years [1].
- **Physical monitoring:** weight, BMI and waist (monthly for 3 months, then 3-monthly in year 1, then yearly), BP, fasting glucose/HbA1c, lipids, prolactin, ECG where indicated, AIMS for tardive dyskinesia every 6–12 months.
- **Substance use, suicidality, depression.**

### Management

**1. Duration**
- First episode: at least **1–2 years** after remission. Consider very gradual, guided dose reduction only in a stable, well-supported patient who understands the risks [1,2].
- Multi-episode (≥2 episodes): **at least 2–5 years**, often indefinite, especially with prior serious suicide attempt, violence or rapid relapse [1].

**2. Drug and dose**
- Continue the drug and dose that worked acutely [1].
- Prophylactic efficacy starts to be lost below about **5 mg/day risperidone equivalent**; higher doses add only side effects [1].
- Targeted/intermittent treatment (drug only when symptoms return) is inferior and may raise TD risk [1].

**3. Long-acting injectables (LAIs)**
- Offer early, not only after non-adherence: risperidone, paliperidone 1-/3-/6-monthly, aripiprazole, olanzapine pamoate, haloperidol and fluphenazine decanoate, flupentixol, zuclopenthixol [1].
- Real-world data favour LAIs and clozapine for preventing rehospitalisation (Tiihonen 2017) [1].

**4. Clozapine** for treatment-resistant illness (failure of two antipsychotics); it also reduces suicide (InterSePT) and mortality.

**5. Psychosocial treatment**
- Family psychoeducation (reduces relapse by about 50%).
- CBT for psychosis; adherence work; cognitive remediation.
- Supported employment (IPS model); social skills training; assertive community treatment for those at high risk.

**6. Physical health**
- Metabolic monitoring, smoking cessation, exercise, metformin for antipsychotic weight gain.

**7. Stopping, when chosen**
- Hyperbolic taper over months to years. Abrupt withdrawal doubles relapse in the first 6 months [1].
- Agree a relapse plan with patient and family.

### Key evidence
- **Leucht 2012 (Lancet):** antipsychotic vs placebo, 1-year relapse 27% vs 64%; rehospitalisation 10% vs 26%.
- **Wunderink 2013 (7-year follow-up):** guided dose reduction/discontinuation group had double the recovery rate (40% vs 18%) despite more early relapses; criticised and not replicated [1].
- **HAMLETT/RADAR (2023):** dose reduction increased relapse without improving function in the short term [1].
- **Tiihonen 2017 (Swedish cohort, n=29,823):** clozapine and LAIs had the lowest rehospitalisation; longer treatment gave lower risk [1].
- **CATIE 2005:** 74% discontinued their assigned drug within 18 months.

**Conclusion.** Evidence strongly supports continuous antipsychotic maintenance, preferably at the lowest effective dose, with early use of LAIs and clozapine where indicated. It must be combined with family and psychosocial interventions and physical health care. The open question is not whether to maintain treatment but **for whom and for how long**. A minority (perhaps 18–30% of first-episode patients) may stay well without long-term drugs [1]. We cannot yet identify them, so decisions about reduction should be shared, slow and closely monitored.

## In depth

### Background
- Before chlorpromazine (1952), most patients with schizophrenia stayed in asylums. Depot fluphenazine (1960s) allowed community maintenance.
- Hogarty's 1970s studies showed that drug plus "major role therapy" was additive, which established combined maintenance.
- The 1990s–2000s second-generation antipsychotic era was followed by CATIE and CUtLASS, which showed no broad effectiveness advantage for newer drugs.
- The 2010s brought debate about long-term harms and recovery-oriented deprescribing (Wunderink, Moncrieff), and the 2020s brought guided dose-reduction trials (RADAR, HAMLETT) and hyperbolic tapering [1].

### Epidemiology
- Over 80% of first-episode patients relapse within 5 years (Robinson 1999), and the risk is about five times higher after stopping medication.
- Madras Longitudinal Study: more than 80% relapsed over 20 years, with nearly half of relapses in years 10–20; yet only 22 of 61 were ever hospitalised and nearly half were not on treatment at 20 years [3].
- Suicide risk (lifetime ~5–10%) is concentrated in the first decade [1]. In the Madras cohort, 7 of 16 deaths were suicides, all in people aged under 35 [3].
- Non-adherence: 50% at 1 year, 75% at 2 years [1].

### Classification and criteria
- **Relapse definitions** vary: hospitalisation; a rise in PANSS of ≥25%, or ≥10 points if baseline is low; specific positive items ≥4 (moderate); suicidal or violent behaviour. The Maudsley notes that these focus on positive symptoms and miss cognitive and negative domains [1].
- **Remission (Andreasen 2005):** 8 PANSS items (P1, P2, P3, N1, N4, N6, G5, G9) ≤3 (mild) for ≥6 months.
- ICD-11 course specifiers (first episode, multiple episodes, continuous; currently symptomatic, partial or full remission) guide maintenance decisions.

### Mechanism in detail
- **Relapse biology:** stress → dopamine release in a sensitised system → aberrant salience. Maintenance D2 blockade raises the threshold.
- **Supersensitivity:** chronic blockade raises D2/D3 receptor availability. Imaging shows increased D2/D3 availability in medicated but not drug-naïve patients. This may persist for months to years after stopping, which explains why relapses cluster in the first 3 months after abrupt cessation (60% of relapses over 4 years in one analysis) and why slow tapers help [1].
- **Neurotoxicity of relapse vs of drugs:** longer DUP and more relapses are linked with worse outcomes. Antipsychotic exposure is linked with grey matter loss in some cohorts. Causality is unresolved.

### Clinical features in detail — predictors of relapse
- Non-adherence (strongest), substance use (especially cannabis), high expressed emotion in the family, residual symptoms, poor insight, male sex, adverse effect burden, a negative attitude to treatment, and stressful life events [1].
- Relapse signature: insomnia, irritability, social withdrawal, suspiciousness and preoccupation usually precede florid psychosis by 1–4 weeks.

### Assessment in detail

| Domain | Tool | Frequency |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Psychopathology | PANSS/BPRS; CDSS for depression | Every visit / 3-monthly |
| Function | PSP, SOFAS, GAF | 6-monthly |
| Adherence | Pill counts, LAI records, plasma levels (TDM) | Every visit |
| Side effects | GASS/LUNSERS, AIMS, Barnes Akathisia Rating Scale, Simpson–Angus | 6-monthly |
| Metabolic | Weight, waist, BP, glucose/HbA1c, lipids | Baseline, 12 weeks, then yearly |
| Endocrine | Prolactin; sexual function; menstrual history in women | Baseline, then if symptomatic [2] |
| Cardiac | ECG (QTc) for high-risk drugs and combinations | Baseline, after dose changes |
| Bone | DEXA if long-term hyperprolactinaemia | As indicated [2] |

### Differential — apparent relapse vs other causes

| Presentation | Distinguishing point |
| --- | --- |
| True relapse | Return of the previous symptom pattern |
| Withdrawal-associated psychosis | Within weeks of abrupt stop, alongside cholinergic or histaminergic withdrawal features [1] |
| Akathisia | Inner restlessness, often mistaken for agitation; worsens if dose is increased |
| Post-psychotic depression | Low mood with insight, suicide risk |
| Substance-induced psychosis | Timing with use; urine toxicology |
| Medical/organic | Delirium, endocrine (hypothyroidism), drug interaction lowering levels (e.g., smoking restarted with clozapine/olanzapine) |

### Management details
- **Dose:** acute-effective dose generally continued; the 5 mg risperidone-equivalent threshold [1]. Use the lowest effective dose in the elderly and in women after menopause (oestrogen loss reduces CYP activity and raises drug need) [2].
- **Women of childbearing age:** avoid prolactin-raising drugs where possible (risperidone, paliperidone, amisulpride, FGAs). Aripiprazole is useful; consider contraception and folate; monitor bone density [2].
- **Pregnancy:** continue the effective antipsychotic rather than switching; relapse risk is high postpartum.
- **LAI practicalities:** test dose for FGA depots; olanzapine pamoate needs 3 hours of observation for post-injection delirium/sedation syndrome; paliperidone 3-monthly only after ≥4 months stable on monthly [1].
- **Clozapine:** blood monitoring; plasma level >350 µg/L target; LAI cannot replace it; continue long-term in responders.
- **Polypharmacy:** generally avoid; clozapine + aripiprazole has observational support (lowest rehospitalisation in Tiihonen 2019).
- **Stopping:** hyperbolic taper (for example 25% reduction of the current dose every 3–6 months), tapering over a year or more reduced relapse hazard 3.5-fold compared with over days [1]. Weigh symptom-free duration, adverse effects, past pattern of illness, previous reduction attempts, social stability, cost of relapse, and capacity to monitor [1].

### Critical appraisal
- **Discontinuation trials may overstate drug benefit.** Most stopped drugs quickly. Withdrawal-associated relapse may inflate placebo-arm relapse [1].
- **Relapse is not outcome.** Functional recovery, cognition and negative symptoms change little with maintenance. Wunderink's data suggest lower long-term exposure may favour function, but the study had flaws [1].
- **Long-term harms:** TD (~5% per year with FGAs), metabolic syndrome, cardiovascular mortality, hyperprolactinaemia and bone loss, and possible brain volume loss [1,2].
- **Heterogeneity:** 18–30% of first-episode patients may not need long-term drugs, and no biomarker identifies them; cannabis use is the only factor linked to outcome after discontinuation [1].
- **Developing-country outcomes** (Madras: high employment and marriage, few hospitalisations despite many off treatment) suggest that social context shapes maintenance needs [3].
- **Ethics:** the patient's right to decline long-term medication (MHCA 2017, advance directives) must be balanced against relapse risk.

### Indian context
- **MHCA 2017:** right to community living, advance directives, and supported admission. Maintenance treatment depends on consent; a nominated representative can support decisions.
- **District Mental Health Programme** and free essential drugs (including LAIs such as fluphenazine decanoate and haloperidol decanoate) improve access. Newer LAIs are costly.
- Family involvement is central in India; family psychoeducation is feasible and effective.
- Indian studies: the Madras 20-year outcome [3]; the Chennai SCARF community care model; COPSI (Community care for People with Schizophrenia in India, Chatterjee 2014), where collaborative community care plus facility care modestly improved outcomes.
- Concerns: irregular supply, out-of-pocket costs, stigma, and limited physical health monitoring.

### Recent advances
- **Established:** paliperidone palmitate 6-monthly; aripiprazole lauroxil and 2-monthly aripiprazole; risperidone subcutaneous monthly formulations (in some markets).
- **Emerging:** muscarinic agonist xanomeline–trospium (approved 2024) offers maintenance options without D2 blockade; long-term extension data so far show sustained effect and no weight gain.
- **Investigational:** prediction models for safe discontinuation; digital relapse prediction using smartphone data; hyperbolic tapering trials.
