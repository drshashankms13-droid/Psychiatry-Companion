## Exam answer

### Introduction
- **Definition [2]:** diabetes mellitus is hyperglycaemia due to defects in insulin production, insulin action, or both.
  - **Type 1:** autoimmune destruction of pancreatic β-cells.
  - **Type 2:** insulin resistance, with dysfunctional receptors and signalling.
- **Burden in India [1]:**
  - about **77 million** adults have diabetes, rising to a projected 101 million by 2030 and 134 million by 2045;
  - prevalence is 19% urban and 15% rural (diabetes and prediabetes);
  - **43.9 million** are undiagnosed, second only to China;
  - about 422 million people have diabetes worldwide.
- **Relationship with psychiatric disorders:**
  - The relationship is **bidirectional** [1, 3].
  - About **one-third** of patients feel unable to self-manage their diabetes [1].
  - Psychological status affects glycaemic control, complications, disability and mortality [1].
  - Psychotropics with metabolic effects increase the incidence of type 2 diabetes in psychiatric patients [1].
  - Psychiatric manifestations are similar in type 1 and type 2 diabetes [2].

### Spectrum of neuropsychiatric problems in diabetes [1, 2, 3]

| Problem | Key points |
| --- | --- |
| **Stress** | Effect on glucose physiology unclear; associated with poor glycaemic control [1] |
| **Depression** | 2–3-fold increase [1, 2]. Up to 3× in type 1 and 2× in type 2 [3]. Prevalence 22% in type 1 and 19% in type 2 [4]. 12.9% in adolescents with type 1; 1 in 4 with type 2 [2] |
| **Diabetes distress** | Emotional response to living with diabetes: the demands of self-management, threat of complications, stigma and cost [3] |
| **Anxiety** | GAD and panic disorder most common; subsyndromal anxiety more common still [1]. 20% increased prevalence [2]; present in 40% [3]. Most frequent just after diagnosis and when complications occur [2]. Fear of hypoglycaemia disrupts glucose monitoring and quality of life [1] |
| **Bipolar disorder** | Diabetes in 10–26% (weight gain from psychotropics, obesity, shared genetic risk, comorbidity) [1] |
| **Schizophrenia** | Higher risk: sedentary lifestyle, nicotine, appetite, high-carbohydrate diet, metabolic effects of atypical antipsychotics [1]. In bipolar disorder and schizophrenia, 2–3-fold risk from shared genes (GSK-3, D2 receptor), lifestyle and drugs [2] |
| **Eating disorders** | Insulin omission or manipulation ("**diabulimia**"), equivalent to caloric purging, in type 1 women. Raises the risk of ketoacidosis and retinopathy. About 7% of type 1 adolescents [1]. ED prevalence 8.2% in type 1; insulin omission in 40%. Suspect it with poor control and repeated DKA [2] |
| **Suicide** | Risk up to 56% higher, stronger in type 1 [2] |
| **Sexual dysfunction** | Men: erectile dysfunction, loss of libido, ejaculatory disturbance (related to age, duration and complications). Women: loss of libido and arousal, reduced lubrication [1] |
| **Cognition** | Type 1 children and adolescents: learning difficulties, slow processing, attention and short-term memory deficits. Long-standing uncontrolled type 2: risk of cognitive decline and **dementia** via micro- and macrovascular brain changes [1] |

### Diabetes and depression in detail

**Direction of association.** There are three possibilities [3]:
1. a common aetiology;
2. diabetes raising the risk of depression;
3. depression raising the risk of diabetes.

Lustman and colleagues found the relationship to be reciprocal, with a strong association between HbA1c and depression [1]. Depression may raise the risk of type 2 diabetes by **60%** [3].

**Mechanisms** [1, 2, 3]:
- **Biological:**
  - insulin resistance increases cytokines, producing a pro-inflammatory state;
  - raised inflammatory markers predict poor antidepressant response;
  - depression activates the HPA axis, raising cortisol and counter-regulatory hormones, which causes insulin resistance;
  - autonomic activation, increased fat and endothelial dysfunction [3];
  - in diabetes, inflammation and oxidative stress reduce brain 5-HT through reduced tryptophan hydroxylase [3];
  - **brain insulin resistance** may be the linking pathway [3];
  - about **500 SNPs** are shared across immune, lipid-metabolism and cell-signalling pathways [3].
- **Lifestyle:** sedentary behaviour, high-calorie food, smoking, drinking [1].
- **Psychological:** the burden of self-management, complications, hopelessness [3].

**Consequences.**
- **Glycaemic control and self-care:**
  - depressive symptoms (low motivation, energy and interest; hopelessness) lead to **non-adherence**, poor glycaemic control and complications [1];
  - patients miss appointments and glucose monitoring [3];
  - depression in type 2 diabetes brings more hospital admissions and complications, and worse diet adherence [4].
- **Outcomes:**
  - poorer quality of life [3];
  - more microvascular complications [3];
  - **mortality up by about 50%** [3].

**Methodological caution [1].**
- **Scales overestimate depression:** many studies use scales such as the PHQ without a clinical interview, which inflates prevalence.
- **INTERPRET-DD (14 countries):** current MDD on the MINI was **10.6%**, but moderate-to-severe PHQ symptoms were reported by 17%.
- **Golden et al.:** minor depression in 13% of patients with diabetes.

### Assessment (IPS CPG Figure 4) [1, 3]
- Detailed psychiatric history, risk factors and family history.
- General examination: BMI, weight.
- Blood tests: fasting and postprandial sugar, HbA1c.
- Screening for cardiac, renal and eye complications.
- Rating scales: **PHQ-9, BDI** [1]; HADS [3].
- Diabetes-specific quality-of-life measures (burden of self-care).
- Evaluation for cognitive decline due to chronic ischaemic change; MRI brain if needed.
- **Maudsley:** screen all patients with depression for diabetes [4].

### Management of depression in diabetes

**Principles** [1, 3]:
- Take a holistic, **integrated** approach to both conditions.
- **Petrak et al.:** treat depression as a **priority**. Antidepressant response appears in 2–4 weeks, whereas HbA1c takes months, and better mood improves diabetes self-care [3].
- **Treatment arms (Figure 5)** [1]:
  - pharmacotherapy;
  - **CBT**;
  - lifestyle modification;
  - **internet-guided self-help**.

**Antidepressants** [1, 2, 4]:
- All antidepressants improve depressive symptoms, which improves self-care and glycaemic control; all classes can be given safely in diabetes [1].
- Antidepressants are effective in diabetes and moderately improve glycaemic control (Cochrane 2012, cited in Maudsley) [4].

| Drug | Effect in diabetes |
| --- | --- |
| **SSRIs** | Preferred for efficacy and low side-effect burden [1]. Favourable effects on diabetic parameters; insulin needs may fall [4] |
| **Fluoxetine** | Better than other SSRIs for hypoglycaemia, weight loss, reduced body fat and glycaemic control; **can be first choice** [1]. Improves HbA1c and insulin sensitivity independently of weight loss [4] |
| Sertraline, escitalopram | May reduce HbA1c and improve control [4] |
| **Bupropion** | Reduces BMI, body fat and HbA1c; can be considered [1] |
| **TCAs** (nortriptyline, imipramine, amitriptyline) | Hyperglycaemic effects; amitriptyline causes weight gain; **regular glucose monitoring needed** [1]. Increased appetite and weight; long-term use may increase diabetes risk; clomipramine reported to precipitate diabetes [4] |
| **SNRIs** | Inconclusive evidence on glucose metabolism; venlafaxine can cause weight loss; duloxetine "regulates body weight, safe in stable diabetic patients but interferes with glucose metabolism" [1]. Maudsley: SNRIs do not appear to disrupt glycaemic control [4] |
| **Mirtazapine** | Does not cause hyperglycaemia and is not contraindicated, but watch weight gain [3]. HbA1c improved short term but worsened at 1 year; BMI increases [4] |
| **Trazodone** | No evidence on glycaemic control [1]. One study showed a 20% increase in type 2 risk [4] |
| **Irreversible MAOIs** | Extreme hypoglycaemic episodes and weight gain [4] |
| Agomelatine, vortioxetine | Probably safe; fewer data [4] |

**Maudsley recommendations** [4]:
- **First line:** SSRIs (sertraline, escitalopram, fluoxetine).
- **Probably safe, fewer data:** SNRIs, bupropion, vortioxetine, agomelatine.
- **Avoid if possible:** TCAs and MAOIs.
- **Monitoring:** glucose and HbA1c when starting, changing the dose and stopping.
- **Diabetes treatment:** metformin may be preferred for its putative mood benefit.

**Psychological and lifestyle treatment** [1, 3]:
- **CBT:**
  - suits mild depression;
  - improves diabetes distress, and depression more than distress, because it was not tailored to self-care problems;
  - gives a **short-term fall in HbA1c**;
  - changes negative illness beliefs, improving self-care;
  - benefits are larger short term than long term [1].
- **IPT** is also helpful [3].
- **Diabetes self-management programmes** improve fitness, weight and sense of control [3].
- **Lifestyle:** healthy eating, exercise, yoga, relaxation, breathing techniques [1].
- **Internet-guided self-help** gives short-term benefit and can be built into self-care programmes [1].

**Anxiety:** early pharmacological and/or non-pharmacological management reduces disease burden and complications. CBT targets maladaptive thoughts and problem-solving [1].

### Special situations [1]

**Schizophrenia (Table 4)** [1]:
- **Before antipsychotics:** screen fasting and postprandial glucose (HbA1c if deranged). Ask about gestational diabetes, obesity and family history; check lipids.
- **Prefer:** **ziprasidone and lurasidone**.
- **Intermediate,** in descending order of metabolic effect: aripiprazole, risperidone, amisulpride, quetiapine, paliperidone, asenapine, haloperidol.
- **Avoid with risk factors:** **olanzapine, clozapine, sertindole**.
- Pre- and probiotics may reduce gut dysbiosis and metabolic syndrome.

**Eating disorders (Table 5)** [1]:
- A multidisciplinary team.
- Medical stabilisation: adequate insulin, more food, a flexible meal plan and regular routine, glucose monitoring.
- CBT for insulin omission; antidepressants.

**Prevention of complications** [1]: annual physical, foot and eye examination in type 2 diabetes.

**Conclusion.**
- **Psychiatric comorbidity:** diabetes carries a 2–3-fold excess of depression, plus excess anxiety, eating disorders (insulin omission), suicide risk, sexual dysfunction and cognitive decline.
- **Reciprocal harm:** depression and diabetes worsen each other through inflammation, HPA dysfunction, insulin resistance and poor self-care.
- **Management:** integrated care, prioritising depression treatment. Use SSRIs (especially fluoxetine) or bupropion, avoid TCAs and MAOIs, add CBT and lifestyle change, and monitor glucose closely.

## In depth

### Diabetes distress versus depression [3]
Features of diabetes distress:
- feeling overwhelmed by the demands of diabetes;
- concern about "failures" in management;
- powerlessness or hopelessness;
- worry about hypoglycaemia or complications;
- frustration that diabetes is unpredictable;
- frustration with caregivers;
- guilt when management goes "off track".

### Biological pathway summary [1, 2, 3]
- Both are **pro-inflammatory** states [2].
- **From diabetes to depression:** hyperglycaemia and adipose dysfunction → inflammation and disordered insulin signalling → reduced tryptophan hydroxylase → reduced 5-HT [3].
- **From depression to diabetes:** HPA dysfunction → raised cortisol → autonomic activation → visceral fat and endothelial dysfunction → insulin resistance → type 2 diabetes [1, 3].
- **Intrauterine factors** may increase the risk of chronic disorders [3].

### Specific risk factors for depression in diabetes [2]
- Insulin therapy.
- Recurrent hypoglycaemia.
- Poor glycaemic control.

### Metabolic syndrome link [1]
- **Definition:** abdominal obesity, insulin resistance or glucose intolerance, high triglycerides, low HDL, hypertension. Definitions: NCEP ATP III 2005, IDF 2005, WHO 1998, EGIR 1999.
- **Risk:** patients with schizophrenia, depression and bipolar disorder are at increased risk.
- **Management:** exercise 30 minutes daily, weight loss ≥7%, healthy diet, no smoking, stress management.

### Counter-view on causation [4]
Antidepressants as a group may themselves contribute to diabetes risk, although the association may disappear after adjusting for comorbidity. Long-term SSRIs may increase the risk of diabetes (including gestational diabetes), but the evidence conflicts.
