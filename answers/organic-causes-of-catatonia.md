## Exam answer

### Introduction [1, 2]
- **Catatonia** is a neuropsychiatric syndrome of motor, speech, affective, behavioural and autonomic signs [2].
- **Prevalence:** about **9%** of psychiatric and medical patients (meta-analysis of 74 studies, over 107,000 people) [1, 2].
- **Not specific to schizophrenia:**
  - up to 35% of people with schizophrenia have it, but **most cases involve depressive or bipolar disorders** [1];
  - Abrams & Taylor: **mania** was the commonest diagnosis [3];
  - it also occurs in **medical conditions** [1].
- **DSM-5-TR "Catatonic disorder due to another medical condition" (F06.1)** [1]:
  - **A.** at least **3 of 12** features: stupor, catalepsy, waxy flexibility, mutism (not if known aphasia), negativism, posturing, mannerism, stereotypy, agitation, grimacing, echolalia, echopraxia;
  - **B.** evidence from history, examination or investigations that it is the **direct physiological consequence** of a medical condition;
  - **C.** not better explained by another mental disorder;
  - **D.** not exclusively during **delirium**;
  - **E.** distress or impairment.
- **Why it matters:** organic causes must be **ruled out before** the catatonia specifier is used for a mental disorder [1]. Organic causes were overlooked initially in published series [3].

### Organic causes [1, 2, 3]

| Category | Causes |
| --- | --- |
| **Structural brain lesions** | **Neoplasms**, **head trauma**, **cerebrovascular disease**, **subarachnoid haemorrhage** [1, 2]. Lesions in the **posterior diencephalon/upper midbrain** (tumours, especially craniopharyngioma; infarction) and **anteromedial frontal / anterior cingulate** (alert "hyperpathic" akinetic mutism) [3]. **Raised intracranial pressure** (the most urgent cause of organic stupor) [3]. **Basal ganglia disorders** [2]. Locked-in and **akinetic mutism** states [2] |
| **Infection / inflammation** | **Encephalitis** [1]: herpes encephalitis first diagnosed as catatonic schizophrenia [3]; acute encephalitis with catatonic features in an 18-year-old [3]; **encephalitis lethargica** (flexibilitas cerea; possible modern cases with catatonic stupor). Mahendra suggested many earlier "catatonic schizophrenia" cases had a viral origin [3]. **Meningitis** (especially tuberculous), **neurosyphilis**, **HIV-associated dementia** [3]; CNS infections generally [2] |
| **Autoimmune** | **Anti-NMDAR (autoimmune) encephalitis** [2]; **limbic encephalitis** (ECT-responsive) [2]; **SLE** [2]; **antiphospholipid syndrome** [2]; rare **autoimmune and paraneoplastic** disorders [1]; demyelination presenting as catatonic schizophrenia [3] |
| **Epilepsy** | **Non-convulsive status epilepticus** [2, 3]; **complex partial status** resembling catatonia [3]; postictal stupor and mutism [3] |
| **Metabolic** | **Hepatic encephalopathy**, **hypercalcaemia**, **homocystinuria**, **diabetic ketoacidosis** [1]; **cerebral folate deficiency** [1]; **uraemia**, **hypoglycaemia**, electrolyte disturbance, water intoxication [3]; **Wilson's disease** [2]; **acute porphyria** [3]; **zinc deficiency** (a catatonic-schizophrenia picture reversed by zinc) [3]; **nicotinic acid deficiency encephalopathy** [3] |
| **Endocrine** | **Cushing's** (catatonic motor phenomena cured by adrenalectomy), **myxoedema**, **Addison's**, **hypopituitarism**, **hyperparathyroidism** [3]; endocrine disorders generally [2] |
| **Drugs: toxic states** | **Neuroleptic-induced catatonia** (negativism, posturing, waxy flexibility in the first weeks; can be mistaken for worsening psychosis) [3]; **NMS** [1, 2, 3]; **lithium toxicity**, excessive flupentixol [3]; **LSD** (recurrent catatonic episodes) [3]; severe alcohol intoxication [3] |
| **Drugs: withdrawal** | Abrupt **clozapine** withdrawal; **benzodiazepine**, **zolpidem** and **alcohol** withdrawal; some non-psychotropic (e.g., oncology) drugs [2] |
| **Neurodegenerative** | **Dementia** (stupor late in senile/presenile dementias) [2, 3] |
| **Developmental / genetic** | **Prader–Willi syndrome**; autism spectrum disorder [2] |
| **Other** | Terminal infections (typhus) [3] |

### Features pointing to an organic cause [3]
- **Neurological signs:**
  - irregular respiration;
  - **abnormal pupils**;
  - roving eye movements, **external ocular pareses** (brainstem);
  - facial weakness;
  - exaggerated jaw jerk.
- **Systemic disturbance:** most organic causes show neurological or systemic signs; pyrexia with raised CK suggests NMS.
- **Level of awareness:**
  - severely reduced awareness and **amnesia for the episode** suggest organic stupor;
  - functional stupor shows **retained awareness**, some help with feeding or toileting, meaningful facial expression, and **resistance to eye opening**.
- **Catatonic signs themselves do not distinguish** organic from non-organic causes.
- **Course:** stupor lasting more than 6 months occurred only in the **severely brain-damaged** (Joyston-Bechal). Organic causes made up **one-fifth** of 100 stupors (Joyston-Bechal), and twice that in a later series with modern imaging (Johnson).

### Evaluation [1, 2, 3]
1. **History:** previous psychiatric illness, drug exposure (antipsychotics, recent **clozapine or benzodiazepine withdrawal**), alcohol, head injury, seizures, fever, systemic illness.
2. **Neurological examination:** essential in every case, with attention to **diencephalic and brainstem signs**.
3. **Exclude NMS** in anyone on antipsychotics with stupor plus **rigidity, pyrexia and raised CK**. NMS cannot be ruled out if a dopamine antagonist has been given. **Malignant catatonia** may be clinically and biochemically indistinguishable from NMS [2, 3].
4. **Exclude delirium** (DSM does not diagnose catatonic disorder exclusively during delirium, though catatonic signs are common in delirium) [1].
5. **EEG:**
   - **non-convulsive status**;
   - triphasic waves (hepatic or renal);
   - spindle coma (brainstem);
   - fast activity (drugs);
   - absent alpha with reduced reactivity is expected in organic stupor [3].
6. **Bloods:** U&E, calcium, glucose, LFT, thyroid and adrenal function, CK, drug levels (lithium), and others as indicated [1, 3].
7. **Neuroimaging; CSF** (infection, encephalitis); autoimmune work-up where indicated (the specific antibody panels are not detailed in the sources).
8. **Abreactive interview** (amobarbital or benzodiazepine) can be informative when a psychogenic cause is suspected [3].

### Management [2, 3]
1. **Treat the underlying condition** (e.g., lupus) [2].
2. **Benzodiazepines:**
   - **lorazepam** 2 mg, a further 2 mg after 3 hours if no effect, up to **4 mg/day**; then **high dose 8–24 mg/day**;
   - **sublingual** (tasteless), IM or IV;
   - 18 of 21 patients with catatonia due to **medical conditions or substances** responded to a lorazepam–diazepam protocol;
   - a **zolpidem 10 mg** test dose may predict response [2].
3. **ECT** if there is no response after 1–2 days, or **immediately if life is in danger**. It is the most successful treatment overall and works in NMS, delirious mania, ASD and **limbic encephalitis**. In malignant catatonia, use liberal stimulus dosing [2].
4. **Avoid antipsychotics** where NMS is possible: they can induce catatonia, and NMS risk is higher [2, 3]. **Stop the offending neuroleptic** in drug-induced catatonia [3]. **Restart clozapine** (± a benzodiazepine) if catatonia followed clozapine withdrawal and NMS is excluded [2].
5. **Physical care:**
   - nutrition and hydration;
   - watch for complications: dehydration, venous thrombosis, PE, pneumonia, pressure ulcers, contractures, hyperpyrexia, death;
   - medical inpatient care when there is **autonomic instability** [1, 2].
6. **Other reported agents** (Table 1.34; experimental): amantadine, memantine, carbamazepine, valproate, lithium, methylphenidate and others. Read the primary literature first [2].
7. **rTMS and tDCS:** promising; RCTs are needed [2].

**Conclusion.** Catatonia (3 or more of 12 DSM signs) is commonest in mood disorders, but it has many organic causes:
- structural lesions (diencephalic, brainstem, frontal/cingulate, basal ganglia, raised intracranial pressure);
- **encephalitis (including anti-NMDAR)**, meningitis, SLE;
- non-convulsive status;
- metabolic disorders (hepatic, calcium, glucose, porphyria, Wilson's) and endocrine disorders;
- drug toxicity, especially **NMS and neuroleptic-induced catatonia**;
- withdrawal of **benzodiazepines, alcohol or clozapine**;
- dementia.

Catatonic signs do not distinguish organic from functional causes, so neurological examination, EEG, bloods, imaging and CSF are needed in every case, with NMS and delirium excluded. Treatment is the cause plus lorazepam and early ECT, avoiding antipsychotics when NMS is possible.
