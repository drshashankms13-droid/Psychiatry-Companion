## Exam answer

### Introduction

Substance use disorder (SUD) involves impaired control, craving, continued use despite harm, and clinically significant impairment. The debate is whether addiction is best understood as a brain disease, learned behaviour, response to adversity, moral failing, or an interaction of these.

### Arguments supporting a disease model

- Repeated exposure alters reward learning, incentive salience, stress systems, habit circuitry, and executive control.
- Some individuals persist despite serious health, family, occupational, or legal consequences, suggesting impaired control rather than simple preference.
- SUD may have a chronic, relapsing course, with recurrence risk persisting after detoxification.
- Genetic, developmental, psychiatric, and environmental vulnerabilities influence risk.
- Evidence-based psychosocial and pharmacological treatments reduce use and harm. A disease framework can reduce moral blame and improve access to care.

### Critiques

- **Heterogeneity:** Most exposed people do not develop addiction; severity and course vary, and some recover without formal treatment.
- **Agency and context:** Poverty, trauma, availability, peer networks, family environment, and commercial determinants shape risk. A brain-only model neglects these.
- **Determinism:** "Hijacked brain" language may imply permanence or absence of agency.
- **Stigma/medicalization:** Disease language may reduce blame but can also portray people as chronically defective.
- **Scientific caution:** Neurobiological changes are not unique to addiction and may be cause, consequence, adaptation, or recovery-related change.

### Integrated formulation

Addiction is most usefully understood as a biopsychosocial disorder. Brain mechanisms interact with learning, stress, psychiatric symptoms, family relationships, drug availability, and social disadvantage. The brain-disease model is a useful corrective to moral condemnation, but incomplete if treated as the entire explanation. Diagnosis rests on a pattern of impaired control and harm, not a scan or biomarker.

### Clinical/public-health implications

Use nonjudgmental assessment of severity, withdrawal/overdose risk, medical complications, comorbidity, trauma, social context, and patient goals. Treatment may combine motivational interviewing, CBT, contingency management, family interventions, harm reduction, mutual-help support, and indicated medications. Public health requires prevention, treatment access, overdose prevention, and action on social determinants.

### Conclusion

Addiction can legitimately be classified as a disorder with biological and psychological mechanisms, but not as a single-cause, irreversible brain defect. Agency, learning, social context, and recovery must remain central.
