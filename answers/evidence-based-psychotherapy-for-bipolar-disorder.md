## Exam answer

**Introduction.** Bipolar disorder is primarily treated with mood stabilisers, but **pharmacotherapy alone leaves high rates of relapse, residual depressive symptoms, non-adherence and psychosocial disability**. Adjunctive psychosocial interventions target these gaps. They are recommended **alongside medication, not instead of it** — cognitive monotherapy is generally not recommended in bipolar disorder [1, 2].

### Rationale — what psychotherapy targets

- Non-adherence (often driven by beliefs such as "If I'm feeling great, I don't have to take meds") [1]
- Early warning signs of relapse
- **Sleep and social rhythm disruption** (triggers for mania)
- Family stress and **expressed emotion**
- Residual depressive symptoms, substance misuse, stigma, functional recovery

### The five evidence-based approaches

| Therapy | Core model | Key components | Best evidence |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Group psychoeducation** (Colom, Barcelona) | Knowledge and self-management | 21 sessions: illness awareness, adherence, early signs, substance avoidance, regular routines | Fewer recurrences and admissions over 2 and 5 years in euthymic bipolar I/II |
| **Family-focused therapy (FFT)** (Miklowitz) | High expressed emotion predicts relapse | 21 sessions over 9 months: psychoeducation, **communication enhancement**, **problem-solving** | Fewer relapses, faster recovery from depression; adolescent trials positive |
| **Interpersonal and social rhythm therapy (IPSRT)** (Frank) | Disrupted social zeitgebers → circadian disruption → episodes | IPT problem areas + **social rhythm metric** to stabilise sleep, meals, activity | Longer time to recurrence when given in the acute phase; faster recovery from depression |
| **CBT for bipolar disorder** (Basco & Rush; Lam; Newman) | Hyperpositive and depressive schemas | Monitoring mood, identifying prodromes, challenging grandiose and depressive cognitions, adherence, activity scheduling | Benefit in less recurrent illness; weaker in patients with many episodes |
| **Structured group programmes** (Life Goals, Bauer & McBride) | Self-management | Psychoeducation + CBT + problem-solving skills | Improves functioning and course [3] |

### Cognitive model of bipolar disorder [1]

- **Depression:** negative views of self, world and future, as in unipolar depression.
- **Mania:** hyperpositive core beliefs ("*I am powerful; I am invulnerable; I am great*"); impulsivity and risk-taking; assumptions such as "*If something seems like a fantastic idea, it must be*" and "*I should follow my impulses immediately*".
- **Euthymia:** "*I have to drive myself hard to make up for how little I accomplished when I was depressed*" → overextension → poor sleep → hypomania.
- Mansell's model: interpretation of internal states and beliefs about affect regulation trigger episodes [1].

### Landmark evidence

| Trial | Finding |
| --- | --- |
| **STEP-BD psychosocial trial (Miklowitz 2007)** | Intensive psychotherapy (FFT, IPSRT or CBT, up to 30 sessions) vs brief collaborative care in bipolar depression: recovery **64% vs 52%** at 1 year, faster recovery |
| **Colom et al., 2003, 2009** | Group psychoeducation reduced recurrences, hospitalisations and time ill; benefits persisted at 5 years |
| **Miklowitz FFT trials** | Lower relapse over 2 years than crisis management |
| **Frank et al., 2005 (IPSRT)** | Acute-phase IPSRT → longer survival without new episode |
| **Lam et al., 2003** | CBT reduced relapse at 1 year (benefit diminished later) |
| **Scott et al., 2006** | CBT not superior to usual care overall; helpful in those with fewer than 12 episodes |

### Practical approach

**Acute phase:** medication first; IPSRT and FFT can begin once the person can engage (especially in depression) → **maintenance phase:** psychoeducation (individual, group or family) for all → add FFT if family stress is high, IPSRT if rhythms are irregular, CBT if residual depression or dysfunctional beliefs → relapse-prevention plan with early warning signs, sleep protection, crisis contacts → address substance misuse and functional rehabilitation.

**Conclusion.** Psychoeducation, family-focused therapy, IPSRT and CBT each reduce relapse and improve recovery when added to mood stabilisers. Psychoeducation is the most robust and practical foundation; the choice of additional therapy depends on the patient's phase, family environment, rhythm disruption and number of previous episodes.

## In depth

### Background

- Early psychodynamic approaches to manic-depressive illness gave way, after lithium (Cade 1949), to purely biological management.
- Recognition of high recurrence despite medication (about 50% relapse within 2 years in many cohorts), poor adherence and the effect of family expressed emotion led to manualised adjunctive therapies from the 1990s.
- Family therapy is indicated whenever serious psychiatric illness such as bipolar disorder affects a family member [4]; cognitive-behavioural family work has been applied to bipolar disorder [4].

### Mechanism in detail

- **Social zeitgeber hypothesis (Ehlers, Frank, Kupfer):** life events disrupt daily routines (social zeitgebers) → circadian disruption → sleep loss → mood episodes, especially mania. IPSRT stabilises routines with the Social Rhythm Metric.
- **Expressed emotion:** criticism, hostility and emotional over-involvement from relatives predict relapse; FFT reduces these through communication and problem-solving training.
- **Cognitive model:** hyperpositive and depressive schemas activated by biological and psychological triggers [1].
- **Adherence:** cognitions about medication (side effects, loss of creativity, "I'm cured") are a major target [1].

### Clinical features relevant to therapy

- **Prodromal signs of mania:** reduced sleep, increased goal-directed activity, irritability, racing thoughts, spending.
- **Prodromal signs of depression:** withdrawal, loss of interest, fatigue, pessimism.
- Patients often detect mania prodromes better than depression prodromes; families can help.

### Management details

- **Psychoeducation content:** nature of illness, causes, triggers, medication and side effects, lithium levels and toxicity, early warning signs, substance avoidance, regular habits, stress management, pregnancy planning.
- **Format:** group psychoeducation is cost-effective; individual for those unable to attend; family sessions where relatives are involved.
- **Booster sessions** help maintain CBT gains [1].
- **Contraindication:** cognitive monotherapy for bipolar disorder is generally not recommended; some evidence for its use only when medical problems preclude medication [1].
- Maudsley lists family therapy, IPT, CBT, psychoeducation and social rhythm therapy as modalities to consider, especially when reducing mood stabilisers [2].

### Critical appraisal

- Effect sizes are modest (small–medium) and smaller in people with many prior episodes or severe illness [5].
- Different therapies share common elements (psychoeducation, early warning signs, adherence) — it is unclear which components drive benefit.
- Most trials are from high-income countries; access to trained therapists is limited elsewhere.
- Benefits may fade after therapy ends; maintenance or booster sessions may be needed.

### Indian context

- Families are usually closely involved — family psychoeducation is feasible and culturally congruent.
- Group psychoeducation sessions in outpatient clinics and inpatient wards are low-cost options; Indian studies have shown benefit of psychoeducation on adherence and knowledge (author's knowledge).
- Brief, task-shared and tele-delivered psychoeducation (including through **Tele-MANAS** and DMHP services) can bridge the therapist shortage.
- Marriage, pregnancy and stigma are common topics in Indian psychoeducation programmes.

### Recent advances

- **Digital self-monitoring** (smartphone mood and sleep tracking, passive sensing) for early relapse detection.
- **Internet-delivered psychoeducation** and app-based IPSRT adaptations.
- **Cognitive remediation and functional remediation** (Barcelona group) to improve psychosocial functioning.
- **Family-focused therapy for youth at high risk** for bipolar disorder (Miklowitz).
