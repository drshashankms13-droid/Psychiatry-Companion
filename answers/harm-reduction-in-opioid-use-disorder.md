## Exam answer

**Definition.** **Harm reduction** means policies, programmes and practices that aim to **reduce the adverse health, social and economic consequences of drug use**, without necessarily requiring abstinence. It meets people "where they are". It is based on the premise that **addressing the harms of drug use is as important as helping people stop**, and it offers an alternative for those **unable or unwilling to give up drugs** through abstinence-oriented approaches [1].

Priority is given to **immediate, easily preventable harms**: HIV, hepatitis C and B, overdose death, abscesses, crime [1].

### Rationale and mechanism
- **Opioid use disorder (OUD)** is a chronic relapsing disorder. Detoxification alone has relapse rates over 80–90%.
- **Injecting** spreads blood-borne viruses: **HIV prevalence in people who inject drugs (PWID) in India is about 7.2%** nationally, and up to 21% in Punjab, 18% in Delhi and about 12% in Manipur and Mizoram [1].
- Harm reduction acts on several mechanisms:
  - **OST** replaces short-acting, injected, illicit opioids with a long-acting, oral, legal, supervised opioid. This stabilises receptor occupancy, removes withdrawal and craving, blocks euphoria at adequate dose, and cuts injecting.
  - **Needle-syringe programmes (NSP)** prevent sharing.
  - **Naloxone** reverses overdose.
  - **Outreach** engages hidden users and links them to care [1].

### The comprehensive package for PWID (WHO/UNODC/UNAIDS: 9 interventions) [1]
1. **Needle-syringe programmes**
2. **Opioid substitution therapy**
3. **Antiretroviral therapy**
4. HIV counselling and testing
5. Prevention and treatment of STIs
6. Condom programmes for PWID and partners
7. Targeted IEC
8. Prevention, diagnosis and treatment of **tuberculosis**
9. Prevention, diagnosis and treatment of **viral hepatitis**

**Additional measures:**
- **Take-home naloxone** and overdose education.
- Drug-checking services.
- Supervised consumption sites (not in India).
- Abscess and wound care; peer outreach and drop-in centres.
- Housing and legal aid.

### Opioid substitution therapy (OST)
| Feature | Buprenorphine | Methadone |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pharmacology | **Partial μ-agonist**, κ-antagonist; high affinity; **ceiling effect on respiratory depression** | Full μ-agonist; NMDA antagonist; long half-life (24–36 h) |
| India (NACP) | Main OST drug: sublingual **0.4 mg and 2 mg** tablets reserved for OST centres; **Daily Observed Treatment** [1] | Introduced in the programme later (selected centres) |
| Induction | First dose **2–4 mg** when in moderate withdrawal (risk of precipitated withdrawal); repeat 2–4 mg; **maximum 8 mg on day 1**, up to about 12 mg on day 2; stabilisation usually by day 3–4 (maximum by day 7) [1] | Start 20–30 mg; increase 5–10 mg every few days; maintenance 60–120 mg |
| Maintenance | **India: 8–12 mg/day usually optimal** (Western guidelines 12–16 mg); maximum about **20–24 mg/day** in India (32 mg elsewhere) [1] | 60–120 mg/day |
| Dose effects | 2–4 mg controls withdrawal; 4–8 mg controls craving; **8–12 mg blocks euphoria** from illicit opioids [1] | — |
| Duration of action | 2 mg about 12 h; 16 mg up to 48 h [1] | About 24 h |
| Safety | Less overdose risk (except with benzodiazepines or alcohol) | QTc prolongation; overdose risk at induction |

### Eligibility under NACP [1]
- **Essential:**
  - Opioid dependence diagnosis (ICD criteria in the past year).
  - Need for OST.
  - No contraindication (the only **absolute** one is **hypersensitivity to buprenorphine**).
  - **Written informed consent.**
  - Willingness to attend **daily** for supervised dosing.
- **Desirable:** age over 18 (adolescents may be considered); failed abstinence attempts; more than 3 years of use or injecting; motivation; feasibility.
- **Caution:** severe alcohol or benzodiazepine dependence; pregnancy (continue OST; do not detoxify); severe liver disease; psychiatric comorbidity.

### Outcomes of OST [1]
- ↓ injecting and sharing; ↓ illicit opioid use.
- **↓ overdose deaths** (methadone patients about **4× less likely to die**).
- ↓ crime; ↓ domestic violence; better child care and family ties; ↑ productivity.
- **↑ adherence to ART, TB and hepatitis treatment.**
- Odds of HIV infection **5.4× higher** among those not in maintenance treatment (Metzger 1993).
- Cost-benefit: **$4–7 return per $1** (crime and justice costs); up to **12:1** including health savings (WHO/UNODC/UNAIDS 2004).

### Harm reduction vs abstinence
| Harm reduction | Abstinence-oriented |
| --- | --- |
| Goal: reduce harm; any positive change | Goal: complete cessation |
| Low threshold; engages the "unwilling" | High threshold; excludes those not ready |
| Pragmatic, public-health, human-rights based | Moral or medical model |
| Evidence-based (OST, NSP, naloxone) | Detox alone has high relapse; naltrexone has adherence problems |

The two are **complementary**, part of a continuum of care. Harm reduction can be the first step towards recovery.

**Conclusion.** Harm reduction is an **evidence-based, pragmatic and humane** response to opioid use disorder. Its core components (OST with buprenorphine or methadone, needle-syringe programmes, naloxone, and linked HIV, TB and hepatitis care) reduce HIV transmission, overdose deaths, crime and suffering, and engage people otherwise unreached. India adopted harm reduction in its **National AIDS Prevention and Control Policy (2002)**, integrated OST into **NACP-III (2007)**, and has expanded it through **NDDTC/AIIMS-supported Drug Treatment Clinics**. Scale-up, adequate dosing, take-home naloxone, prisons and women-focused services are the gaps to close.

## In depth

### Background
- **1960s:** Dole and Nyswander introduced **methadone maintenance** (New York).
- **1980s:** the HIV/AIDS epidemic led to NSPs (Amsterdam, Liverpool) and the "harm reduction" movement.
- **1990s:** buprenorphine maintenance (France, 1996).
- **Endorsement:** WHO/UNODC/UNAIDS position paper (2004); OST medicines on the WHO Essential Medicines List (2005).
- **India:**
  - Early NGO buprenorphine programmes (SHARAN, Delhi, 1998).
  - National AIDS Prevention and Control Policy (2002) endorsed harm reduction.
  - OST formally integrated into NACP (2007).
  - NACO Clinical Practice Guidelines for buprenorphine (Rao, Agrawal, Ambekar; NDDTC; 2014).
  - About 150 OST centres serving about 15,000 PWID by about 2014 [1].

### Epidemiology
- **India (NACO, around 2014):** about 177,000 PWID; concentrated in Manipur, Nagaland, Punjab, Mizoram and Delhi. Over 280 targeted interventions (TIs) reached about 152,000 PWID, which is **over 80% coverage** [1].
- **Magnitude of Substance Use in India (2019):** about 2.1% of the population (about 2.3 crore people) use opioids. About 0.7% (around 77 lakh) need help. Heroin is the commonest opioid, followed by pharmaceutical opioids and opium. Estimated 8.5 lakh PWID.
- **Rising pharmaceutical opioid misuse:** tapentadol, tramadol, pentazocine and buprenorphine injection.

### Classification and criteria
- **ICD-11:** disorders due to use of opioids (harmful pattern; dependence; withdrawal; intoxication).
- **DSM-5-TR:** opioid use disorder (2–3 mild, 4–5 moderate, ≥6 severe); "on maintenance therapy" specifier.
- **NACP diagnosis:** at least 3 ICD dependence criteria within 12 months [1].

### Mechanism in detail
- **Buprenorphine:**
  - High μ affinity displaces other opioids, so it can **precipitate withdrawal** if given too early.
  - Slow dissociation gives a long action and allows **alternate-day dosing** at higher doses.
  - Partial agonism produces a ceiling on respiratory depression.
  - **Sublingual absorption**; poor oral bioavailability.
  - With **naloxone (4:1)**, it deters injection [1].
- **Methadone:** full agonist, oral; long half-life; CYP3A4/2B6 metabolism (interacts with rifampicin and antiretrovirals); QTc prolongation.

### Clinical features in detail: assessment before OST [1]
- **Opioid history:** type, route, quantity, duration, last use; withdrawal (COWS); previous treatment.
- **Other substances:** alcohol, benzodiazepines, cannabis, stimulants.
- **Physical:** injection sites, abscesses, DVT, HIV, HCV, HBV, TB, liver disease.
- **Psychiatric comorbidity.**
- **Urine drug screen** (supportive, not essential).
- **Social:** family, work, legal issues.

### Assessment in detail: dose adequacy [1]
- **Induction goals:** relieve withdrawal, reduce craving, prevent sedation, engage the patient.
- **Stabilisation:** no withdrawal or craving for 24 h, and blockade of euphoria.
- **Maintenance goals:** occupational, financial and family stability; stopping other drugs; treating comorbidities.

### Differential: OUD treatment options
| Option | Role |
| --- | --- |
| Detoxification (buprenorphine or clonidine taper) | Short term; high relapse; overdose risk afterwards (lost tolerance) |
| **OST (buprenorphine, methadone)** | First-line long-term pharmacotherapy [1] |
| Naltrexone (oral or extended-release injectable) | Antagonist; needs detox and motivation; useful in selected patients (e.g., probation) |
| Psychosocial (MI, CBT, contingency management, relapse prevention) | Adjunct to all |
| Residential rehabilitation | Selected patients |

### Management details
- **Daily Observed Treatment:** a nurse dispenses buprenorphine and watches sublingual dissolution. Fractions (0.2 and 0.4 mg) allow fine titration and help patients who perceive "8 mg" as a high dose [1].
- **Take-home doses:** limited under NACP. Many countries allow them for stable patients. COVID-19 prompted take-home flexibility in India.
- **Psychosocial support:** counselling, family sessions, linkage to HIV, TB and hepatitis services [1].
- **Pregnancy:** continue OST (buprenorphine or methadone); neonatal abstinence syndrome is managed; do not detoxify.
- **Adolescents:** may be given OST with caution and guardian involvement [1].
- **Overdose:** naloxone 0.4–2 mg IV/IM/IN, repeated; observe (longer-acting opioids).
- **Prisons:** continuity of OST; overdose risk after release.

### Evidence table
| Study | Design | Result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mattick et al. 2009 (Cochrane) | Methadone vs no OST | Better retention and less heroin use [1] |
| Mattick et al. 2008/2014 (Cochrane) | Buprenorphine maintenance | Effective; at flexible doses slightly less retention than methadone [1] |
| Metzger et al. 1993 | 18-month cohort | HIV infection odds 5.4× higher out of treatment [1] |
| Connock et al. 2007 (HTA) | Systematic review | OST more effective than placebo or none; higher doses better; mortality 4× lower on methadone [1] |
| Dorabjee & Samson 1998 (Delhi) | Community buprenorphine | 33% of 447 stopped injecting; 35% reduced injecting [1] |
| Armstrong et al. 2010 (Manipur/Nagaland) | OST cohort | Retention 73% at 3 months, 63% at 6 months [1] |
| Sordo et al. 2017 (BMJ) | Meta-analysis | All-cause mortality much lower during OST; highest in the first weeks after stopping |

### Critical appraisal
- **Criticisms:** "replacing one drug with another"; diversion of buprenorphine; moral objections; risk of "maintenance without recovery".
- **Responses:** OST improves function and survival, and dependence on a prescribed medicine is not addiction. Supervised dosing and buprenorphine-naloxone reduce diversion [1].
- **Gaps in India:**
  - Low coverage relative to need.
  - Restrictive daily attendance (an employment barrier).
  - Under-dosing.
  - Limited methadone access.
  - **Naloxone** not widely available to communities.
  - Women and prisoners underserved.
  - Stigma; police harassment.
  - Legal grey zones (NDPS).
- **Policy tension:** NDPS Act 1985 (criminalisation) vs harm reduction. **Section 64A** gives immunity to addicts who volunteer for treatment. The **2014 NDPS amendment** recognised "management" of drug dependence and **essential narcotic drugs**, which eased access for medical use.

### Indian context
- **NACP:** targeted interventions with NSP, condoms, abscess care, BCC; OST centres in government hospitals and NGOs [1].
- **Ministry of Health:** **Drug Treatment Clinics (DTC)** scheme with NDDTC AIIMS, providing buprenorphine and naloxone OST in district hospitals. **Since 2019, private psychiatry clinics and nursing homes** may also provide buprenorphine treatment [1].
- **MSJE:** National Action Plan for Drug Demand Reduction (NAPDDR); Integrated Rehabilitation Centres for Addicts (IRCAs); **Nasha Mukt Bharat Abhiyaan (2020)**.
- **Law:**
  - Buprenorphine is a **Schedule H1** drug and a psychotropic under the **NDPS Act** [1].
  - MHCA 2017 includes substance use disorders as mental illness, with rights to treatment.
  - Punjab runs OOAT (Outpatient Opioid Assisted Treatment) clinics at scale.

### Recent advances
- **Established:** buprenorphine-naloxone; take-home naloxone (WHO 2014).
- **Emerging:**
  - **Long-acting injectable buprenorphine** (weekly or monthly; Sublocade, Buvidal).
  - Buprenorphine implants.
  - Telemedicine OST.
  - Micro-induction (Bernese method).
  - Drug checking (fentanyl test strips).
- **Investigational:** opioid vaccines and monoclonal antibodies.
