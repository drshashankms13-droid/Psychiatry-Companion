## Exam answer

**Definitions** [1]
- **Telepsychiatry:** "the use of electronic communication and information technologies to provide or support clinical psychiatric care at a distance" (RANZCP). It is often used as a synonym for video-conferencing.
- **Telemental health:** a broader term. It covers all mental health applications delivered by a wider range of professionals, for preventive, curative, educational and administrative purposes (Millar 2009).
- **Tele-care:** remote delivery of care to people in their homes or communities, with a larger social-care component.
- **E-health:** ICT across the whole range of health functions.
- **Digital psychiatry** goes further still, adding apps, wearables, digital phenotyping, AI and internet-delivered therapies.

**History** [1]
- **1956:** University of Nebraska (education and consultation).
- **1961:** group psychotherapy by video.
- **1973:** the term "telepsychiatry" (Dwyer).
- **1990s:** growth with better technology.
- **India:**
  - 2003 non-binding guidelines;
  - **2020 Telemedicine Practice Guidelines and Telepsychiatry Operational Guidelines** (see `telepsychiatry-guidelines-india-2020`);
  - **Tele-MANAS (2022)**.

### Modes [1]
- **Synchronous:** real-time video, telephone or chat. Mimics a face-to-face consultation.
- **Asynchronous (store-and-forward):** email, recorded video or audio.
- **Delivery models:**
  - direct-to-patient (home-based);
  - clinic-to-clinic;
  - **collaborative / consultation-liaison** (primary care physician or health worker with a psychiatrist);
  - **hub-and-spoke**;
  - **mobile telepsychiatry units**.

### Role of telemedicine in psychiatry
**1. Clinical care:**
- **Consultation, diagnosis and management:**
  - diagnostic reliability by video is comparable to in-person assessment;
  - patient satisfaction 75–100% [1].
- **Follow-up and continuity of care:**
  - aftercare clinics after discharge (NIMHANS tele-aftercare) [2];
  - medication refills;
  - adherence monitoring.
- **Psychotherapy:** CBT, IPT, family therapy, group therapy (see `telepsychotherapy-pros-and-cons`).
- **Emergency and crisis support:** helplines (**Tele-MANAS 14416**), triage.
- **Specialised groups:**
  - **child and adolescent psychiatry** (school-based);
  - **geriatric** (home-bound);
  - **perinatal**;
  - **addiction** (follow-up; OST limited by prescribing rules);
  - **forensic** (prison telepsychiatry);
  - **consultation-liaison** to district hospitals;
  - disaster and pandemic settings (**COVID-19**).
- **Neuropsychological assessment** (some tests can be adapted).

**2. Public health and systems:**
- **Reduces the treatment gap** (NMHS 75–93%) by extending scarce specialists to rural areas [3].
- **Supports DMHP:** supervision, mentoring and monitoring of programme implementation; collaborative care with primary care doctors and health workers [3].
- **Task-sharing support:** specialist back-up for ASHAs, counsellors and AB-HWC staff.
- **Cost-effective:** saves travel and wage loss. In a South Indian study, telepsychiatry was more economical than in-person care [3].
- **Infection control** during pandemics [4].
- Helps implement **MHCA Section 18** (right to access) despite public health spending of about 1% of GDP [3].

**3. Education and capacity building:**
- tele-education (ECHO model; **NIMHANS Digital Academy**);
- the **Primary Care Psychiatry Programme**: Diploma in Primary Care Psychiatry with tele-on-consultation training;
- supervision of trainees.

**4. Research and monitoring:**
- remote data collection; digital outcome monitoring;
- registries; e-monitoring of DMHP.

### Indian models [1]
| Model | Features |
| --- | --- |
| **SCARF STEP (Pudukkottai, Tamil Nadu)** | Base in a peripheral location; NGO collaboration; local staff training; awareness via street plays; teleconsultations; free medication; documentation |
| **PGIMER Chandigarh "Psychiatrist on Web"** | Net-based software with codified knowledge and a decision-support system; adult and child modules; screening in 5 minutes, total 30 minutes |
| **Ganiyari (Jan Swasthya Sahyog, Chhattisgarh)** | Tribal and rural outpatient and emergency consults via Skype |
| **NIMHANS hub-and-spoke** | Hub contacted by spokes (district teams, prisons, rehabilitation centres); scheduled consults |
| **Maharashtra Telemedicine Project (2007)** | Asynchronous; still operational |
| **Tele-MANAS (2022)** | National 24 × 7 helpline (14416); two tiers; 20 languages; linked to DMHP and e-Sanjeevani |
| **e-Sanjeevani** | National telemedicine (AB-HWC hub-and-spoke and OPD) |

### Challenges
**1. Legal and regulatory:**
- pre-2020 ambiguity, which has now eased [1, 3];
- **interstate jurisdiction for grievances** is still unclear [3];
- **prescribing restrictions** (benzodiazepines; buprenorphine OST not prescribable) [3];
- **MHCA compliance**: remote capacity assessment; nominated representatives; records;
- **POCSO and forensic cases** need in-person care [5];
- certificates.

**2. Clinical:**
- **limited non-verbal cues**; no physical examination (EPS, vitals, injuries, signs of substance use);
- **risk assessment** (suicide, violence) is harder;
- **emergency response** depends on knowing the patient's location and on local services;
- **severe illness, poor insight, cognitive impairment, sensory deficits**;
- **children** (consent and assent, attention span);
- reliance on informants;
- **diagnostic accuracy** with audio or text only.

**3. Therapeutic relationship:**
- rapport-building concerns;
- **boundaries** (home settings; patients contacting at odd hours);
- privacy at home (family members listening, especially for women and adolescents);
- dropout.

**4. Technological:**
- **poor connectivity**: the minimum recommended bandwidth is 512 kbps [5];
- power cuts; device access;
- user literacy (**digital divide**: elderly, rural, poor, women);
- platform reliability.

**5. Privacy, security and ethics:**
- consumer platforms (WhatsApp, Zoom);
- data leaks from aggregators [3];
- recording without consent;
- informed consent processes;
- advertising and solicitation;
- commercialisation by app-based platforms.

**6. Provider and system:**
- **clinician scepticism**, inadequate training, discomfort with technology [1];
- workload and burnout (screen fatigue);
- reimbursement and fee norms;
- lack of dedicated staff and rooms [1];
- integration with existing health records (ABDM);
- **evidence gaps**: Indian studies have methodological weaknesses (small, uncontrolled, short follow-up) [1].

**7. Equity:** risk that the "inverse care law" deepens if only urban, literate patients benefit.

### Ways forward [1, 3]
- Clarify the law: jurisdiction, data protection (**DPDP Act 2023**), platform accreditation.
- Train grassroots providers and specialists.
- Use **collaborative hub-and-spoke and mobile telepsychiatry units**.
- Integrate into **NMHP/DMHP**, AB-HWCs and Tele-MANAS.
- Fund dedicated staff.
- Use hybrid (blended) care: in-person first assessment, then tele follow-up.
- Integrate **wearables and adherence apps**.
- Fund implementation research; collect user and provider perspectives.

**Conclusion.** Telemedicine in psychiatry is **feasible, acceptable, accurate and cost-effective** for consultation, follow-up, psychotherapy, education and supervision. In India it is central to closing the treatment gap (Tele-MANAS, DMHP, e-Sanjeevani). The challenges are clinical (risk, examination, rapport), technological, legal (jurisdiction, prescribing, MHCA) and ethical (privacy, equity). It should **complement, not replace**, in-person care and human-resource development.

## In depth

### Background
- **Drivers:** the specialist shortage, geography, stigma (some patients prefer anonymity), and the pandemic.
- **The TPG and TOG (2020)** turned telepsychiatry from experiment into routine practice in India [3, 5].

### Epidemiology / uptake
- About 0.3 psychiatrists per 100,000 people in India; most in cities.
- **Tele-MANAS:** millions of calls since October 2022 [general knowledge; check current figure; see `tele-manas`].
- **Satisfaction** 75–100% in Indian reviews [1].

### Classification: digital psychiatry spectrum (for "emerging trends")
1. **Telepsychiatry:** clinician-delivered remote care.
2. **Internet- and app-delivered interventions:** guided or unguided iCBT; mindfulness apps; **digital therapeutics**.
3. **Digital phenotyping:** passive smartphone and wearable data (sleep, mobility, speech, typing) to detect relapse.
4. **AI and machine learning:** prediction models, chatbots, NLP of records, clinical decision support.
5. **Virtual reality** exposure therapy (phobias, PTSD); avatar therapy for voices.
6. **Electronic health records and interoperability:** ABDM health IDs.
7. **Social media** for psychoeducation; online peer support.

**Emerging trends in India:**
- Tele-MANAS;
- e-Sanjeevani;
- **NIMHANS Digital Academy**;
- the Primary Care Psychiatry Programme (digitally driven diploma);
- startups offering app-based therapy;
- **Kiran helpline (2020)**, later merged into Tele-MANAS [general knowledge];
- DMHP e-monitoring;
- AI research in Indian languages.

### Mechanism: why it works
- It removes **access barriers** (distance, cost, stigma) while preserving the core elements of care: assessment, alliance, evidence-based treatment.
- RCTs and meta-analyses show **equivalence** of video-based care for depression, anxiety and PTSD (e.g., Hubley 2016 review) [general knowledge].

### Clinical features: indications and contraindications
| Suitable | Less suitable (prefer in-person) |
| --- | --- |
| Stable follow-up | First episode psychosis with high risk |
| Depression and anxiety (mild–moderate) | Active suicidality or violence |
| Psychotherapy (CBT) | Need for physical examination (catatonia, NMS, delirium) |
| Rural or homebound patients | Forensic / POCSO / certification [5] |
| Collaborative care with primary care | Severe cognitive or sensory impairment without support |

### Assessment: adapting the mental state examination by video
- Appearance, psychomotor activity, speech, affect: possible.
- **Limits:** smell (alcohol), tremor detail, EPS rigidity, vital signs.
- Use structured scales (PHQ-9, GAD-7, BPRS) and collateral from family or a health worker at the spoke.

### Differential: synchronous vs asynchronous
| | Synchronous | Asynchronous |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Strength | Immediate, rapport, mirrors in-person care | Flexible, low bandwidth, specialist time-efficient |
| Weakness | Bandwidth, scheduling | No interaction; delays; misinterpretation |

### Management: risk protocol for teleconsultations [5]
1. Record the patient's location and an emergency contact at the start.
2. Screen for risk.
3. If imminent risk: keep the patient on the line; involve family; call local police or ambulance; arrange in-person care.
4. Document.

### Critical appraisal
- **Evidence quality** in India is low to moderate. International data are stronger but from different health systems.
- **Over-promise risk:** technology is not a substitute for human resources [3].
- **Equity:** digital-first policies may exclude the most vulnerable.
- **Commercial apps** often lack evidence and privacy safeguards.

### Indian context
- **Laws:**
  - IMC Act / NMC Act;
  - Drugs and Cosmetics Act;
  - Clinical Establishments Act;
  - **IT Act 2000 and SPDI Rules 2011**;
  - Intermediaries Guidelines;
  - TCCP regulations [1];
  - DPDP Act 2023 [general knowledge].
- **Programmes:** Tele-MANAS, e-Sanjeevani, DMHP; prison telepsychiatry in Karnataka; Kerala and Karnataka state initiatives.

### Recent advances
- **Large language model chatbots** for mental health support: unregulated, with safety concerns [general knowledge].
- **Digital phenotyping** for relapse prediction in schizophrenia and bipolar disorder (research).
- **VR therapies.**
- **Blended care** models.
- **Tele-MANAS app** and video consultations (2024–25) [general knowledge; check].
