## Exam answer

**Introduction.**
- Psychosis in epilepsy is best classified by its **temporal relation to seizures** (Fenton) [1]:

| Category | Types |
| --- | --- |
| Ictal | Psychotic-like aura; non-convulsive status |
| Postictal | Postictal psychosis (and delirium) |
| Interictal | Brief, or chronic **schizophrenia-like psychosis** |
| Para-ictal / alternative | **Forced normalisation** |
| Iatrogenic | Antiseizure medication |

- **Prevalence** [1]:
  - psychosis history in **9.25% of epilepsy-clinic patients vs 1.06% of migraine controls**; narrowly defined schizophrenia in 4.72% (Mendez);
  - Danish registers (2.7 million people): **RR of schizophrenia 2.48** and of non-affective psychosis 2.93 after epilepsy.
- Intractable, disabling epilepsy carries a **two- to threefold** risk of schizophrenia [1].

### 1. Ictal psychotic phenomena [1]
- **Temporal-lobe aura:**
  - complex hallucinations (visual, auditory, olfactory, gustatory);
  - illusions (macropsia, micropsia, distortions);
  - **forced thinking**, thought "blocking";
  - derealisation;
  - sudden heightened significance (delusional-mood-like).
  - All are **brief, paroxysmal and stereotyped**, with associated automatisms or dystonia.
- **Complex partial status:**
  - fluctuating delirium with automatisms;
  - may include hallucinations, paranoid ideas or catatonia-like states;
  - **impaired consciousness** gives it away.
- **Simple partial status** ("aura continua") is rare.
- **Absence status:** clouding without true psychosis.
- **Management:** **treat the seizures**; antipsychotics have no role [1].

### 2. Postictal psychosis (PIP) [1, 2, 3]
- **Frequency:** the **commonest psychosis in epilepsy**; about 6% in telemetry series.
- **Setting:** usually TLE (sometimes generalised epilepsy), after **more than 10 years** of epilepsy.
- **Trigger:** a **cluster** of complex partial seizures or a secondarily generalised seizure.
- **Lucid interval:** **up to 24 hours** [1]; 2.5–48 hours [2].
- **Clinical picture:**
  - **sudden onset**, marked **agitation**;
  - **pleomorphic**: paranoid, grandiose and **religious delusions**; auditory, visual and somatic hallucinations; **prominent affective change**;
  - **mainly in clear consciousness** (a true psychosis, not delirium).
- **Duration:** mean **3.5 days** (16 hours to 18 days). EEG shows non-specific postictal slowing.
- **Course:**
  - recurs, often stereotyped;
  - **14–20% progress to chronic interictal psychosis**, often with similar phenomenology.
- **Risk factors:**
  - **bilateral temporal pathology** (bilateral EEG foci; encephalitis or head injury); can appear *de novo* after lobectomy when contralateral seizures arise;
  - seizure clustering and secondary generalisation;
  - **family history of affective (not psychotic) disorder**.
- **Differential:** complex partial status. The **lucid interval** (from an informant) is the key discriminator.
- **Treatment:**
  - **Lishman:** self-limiting, so **antipsychotics are usually not indicated**. Admission and **benzodiazepine sedation** for agitation. If antipsychotics are used, **withdraw promptly**. Improve seizure control. No evidence that prophylactic antipsychotics prevent chronic progression [1].
  - **Maudsley / ILAE:** benzodiazepine or antipsychotic for up to 3 months, then taper; or antipsychotics for 1–2 months after remission [2, 3].
  - ⚠️ **Discrepancy:** Lishman discourages routine antipsychotics; Maudsley and the slides allow short courses.
- **Surgery caution:** a PIP history raises suspicion of **bilateral disease**, so a higher burden of proof of unilaterality is needed before lobectomy [1].

| | Postictal delirium | Postictal psychosis |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Timing | Immediate | After a **lucid interval** |
| Consciousness | Clouded | Mainly clear |
| Duration | Minutes to hours (longer in the elderly or with learning disability) | Days (mean 3.5) |
| Treatment | Observe; EEG if atypical (exclude NCSE) | Sedation (benzodiazepine); ± brief antipsychotic |

### 3. Interictal schizophrenia-like psychosis (p3-1b1ca9db) [1, 2]
- **Definition:** a psychosis that would meet criteria for schizophrenia but for the epilepsy, with no or a variable relation to individual seizures.
- **Slater (1963), n = 69:**
  - **onset at mean age 30, about 14 years after epilepsy onset** (10–15 years in later series);
  - all cardinal schizophrenic symptoms;
  - claimed differences (warmer affect, fewer catatonic features, less premorbid abnormality, friendlier) **were not replicated** by later objective studies.
- **Course:**
  - Slater: one-third remit, one-third improve;
  - Onuma: 64% chronic over 10 years;
  - outcome similar to primary schizophrenia.

**Risk factors** [1]:
- **Epilepsy severity** (most consistent): seizure frequency, multiple seizure types, status, admissions.
- **Localisation-related / TLE:** likely, but confounded by severity.
- **Experiential auras.**
- **Laterality:** the left-sided (Flor-Henry) hypothesis is **not supported** by large studies.
- **Diffuse brain dysfunction:**
  - generalised or multifocal EEG;
  - CNS insult, neurological signs, **lower IQ**;
  - **global volume loss** (not hippocampal);
  - post-mortem: ventricular enlargement, periventricular gliosis, white-matter softening.
- **Developmental lesions** ("alien tissue": hamartomas, gangliogliomas, dysplasia) more than mesial sclerosis in lobectomy series. *De novo* psychosis after lobectomy, usually within 1 year.
- **Family history of psychosis** (an intermediate genetic loading). Epilepsy raises risk even without a family history.
- Age at onset, sex and handedness: inconsistent.

**Aetiology** [1, 2]:
- **Not chance:** although up to half of comorbid cases may be coincidental.
- **Not mainly genetic precipitation.**
- **Not disability alone:** psychosis exceeds that seen in other disabled groups.
- **Not drugs alone:** drug psychoses resolve on withdrawal.
- **Best explanation:** "**symptomatic schizophrenia**", via:
  - (a) **cumulative seizure effects**: severity; PIP progressing to chronic psychosis; **kindling** [2];
  - (b) **static brain pathology**: psychosis persists or arises after successful surgery.
- **Symonds:** disordered limbic activity "between the fits".
- **Stevens:** **aberrant sprouting** and synaptic reorganisation after seizures.
- **Psychological:** delusional elaboration of aura experiences (Pond). This cannot explain hallucinations or thought disorder.

**Treatment** [1, 3]:
- **As for schizophrenia**, with attention to seizure threshold and interactions.
- **Lishman's low-risk antipsychotics:** **sulpiride, haloperidol, risperidone, trifluoperazine**. Amisulpride, olanzapine and quetiapine carry possibly higher (marginal) risk.
- **Maudsley's low-risk list:** amisulpride/sulpiride, aripiprazole, risperidone, high-potency FGAs. Moderate: olanzapine, quetiapine.
- **Avoid:** chlorpromazine (high dose), loxapine.
- ⚠️ **Discrepancy:** amisulpride is "higher risk" in Lishman but "low risk" in Maudsley.
- **Avoid depots** if possible (they cannot be withdrawn quickly if seizures worsen) [1].
- **Start low, go slow.**
- **Refractory psychosis: clozapine.**
  - Most proconvulsant: 2–3% at 300–600 mg; 4% at 600–900 mg [1].
  - Successful case series exist.
  - **Start in hospital.**
  - **Replace carbamazepine** (agranulocytosis).
  - Prefer **lamotrigine** or a newer ASM to valproate (weight, sedation) [1, 3].
- Optimise the epilepsy; review ASMs (levetiracetam, topiramate, vigabatrin, tiagabine, perampanel).

### 4. Alternative psychosis / forced normalisation [1, 2, 3]
- **Phenomenon:** psychosis appearing **as seizures stop and the EEG normalises** (Landolt; Tellenbach). It suggests **biological antagonism** between seizures and psychosis.
- **Settings:**
  - after an effective add-on ASM (e.g., **ethosuximide in children**; 16% of psychoses in Matsuura followed an add-on ASM);
  - rapid seizure control in previously refractory TLE.
- **Management** [3]:
  - shared decision about ASM dose (accepting some seizures);
  - symptomatic antipsychotic or antidepressant.

### 5. Antiseizure-medication-induced psychosis [1, 3]
- Reported with nearly all ASMs, especially **levetiracetam, topiramate, vigabatrin, tiagabine, perampanel**.
- Usually **resolves on withdrawal**.

**Conclusion.** Psychosis is two to three times more common in chronic epilepsy.
- **Postictal psychosis:** the commonest. Abrupt, affective and pleomorphic, after a lucid interval following seizure clusters; associated with bilateral temporal disease; self-limiting but recurrent, and can become chronic.
- **Interictal schizophrenia-like psychosis:** begins about 10–15 years after epilepsy onset; resembles schizophrenia; linked to seizure severity and diffuse or developmental brain pathology.
- **Treatment:** seizure control for ictal psychoses; sedation (± brief antipsychotic) for postictal psychosis; schizophrenia-type treatment with low-seizure-risk antipsychotics (clozapine with care) for interictal psychosis; ASM adjustment for forced normalisation or drug-induced psychosis.

## In depth

### Relevance to epilepsy surgery [1]
- **Preoperative psychosis** usually persists whether or not seizures stop.
- **New psychosis** can follow surgery (developmental lesions, bilateral EEG, small contralateral amygdala).
- **Comorbid dissociative seizures** almost contraindicate surgery.

### Not covered by the sources (deliberately left blank)
- ILAE (2018/2019) classification of psychoses of epilepsy and formal criteria for PIP (e.g., Logsdail & Toone criteria are named but not listed).
- Kindling evidence beyond the seminar summary.
- Newer ASM-specific psychosis rates (brivaracetam, cenobamate).
