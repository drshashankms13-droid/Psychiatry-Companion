## Exam answer

**Case summary.** A 19-year-old man has had a **gradual decline in academic performance and social withdrawal for 2 years**. On enquiry he reports **thoughts about life and death, the meaning of life and supernatural powers**.

**Working formulation.** An insidious, 2-year decline in function with withdrawal and unusual preoccupations in a young man is most consistent with an **evolving psychotic disorder — either a clinical high-risk (prodromal) state or an insidious-onset schizophrenia with predominant negative symptoms** — until proved otherwise. The key task is to decide whether the "thoughts" are **delusions, overvalued ideas, obsessions, depressive ruminations, or culturally normal beliefs** [1].

### 1. Further clarifications needed in the history

**a. Nature of the thoughts (the most important step)**
- Content: what exactly does he think about death, meaning of life, supernatural powers? Does he believe **he has** powers, is **controlled** by them, or is **receiving messages**?
- Form: held with **delusional conviction** (fixed, not amenable to reason) or with doubt? Are they **ego-dystonic and resisted** (obsessions) or **ego-syntonic**?
- Are they shared by his family, religious group or culture?
- Time spent, distress, and action taken (rituals, visits to god-men, self-harm to test powers).
- **Death-related thoughts:** passive wish to die, suicidal ideation, plans, past attempts — essential safety question.

**b. Other psychotic symptoms**
- Hallucinations (voices commenting, commanding), first-rank symptoms (thought insertion, withdrawal, broadcasting, passivity), ideas or delusions of reference and persecution.
- Formal thought disorder: vague, overinclusive, "pseudo-philosophical" speech (**metaphysical preoccupation**), derailment, poverty of content.
- Odd behaviour: talking or smiling to self, neglect of hygiene, bizarre rituals.

**c. Negative and cognitive symptoms**
- Avolition, anhedonia, asociality, blunted affect, poverty of speech; declining concentration, memory, reading ability [1].

**d. Mood symptoms**
- Pervasive low mood, anhedonia, hopelessness, guilt, sleep and appetite change (depression); periods of elevated mood, grandiosity or reduced need for sleep (bipolar disorder).

**e. Course and onset**
- Exact onset and progression; any **stressor** (exam failure, bullying, relationship, migration to college town).
- Premorbid personality and adjustment: shy, odd, isolated (schizoid/schizotypal traits)? Developmental milestones; childhood social communication difficulties (autism).

**f. Substance use**
- **Cannabis** (ganja, bhang, charas), alcohol, stimulants, hallucinogens, inhalants; quantity, timing relative to symptoms.

**g. Medical and neurological**
- Head injury, seizures (déjà vu, absences — temporal lobe epilepsy), tremor, dysarthria, jaundice (**Wilson's disease**), headaches, fever, weight loss.

**h. Family history**
- Psychosis, bipolar disorder, suicide, substance use, consanguinity.

**i. Functioning and risk**
- Academic records, attendance, friendships, self-care; risk of suicide, self-neglect, violence, exploitation.
- **Collateral** from parents, teachers and friends — the patient may lack insight.

**j. Mental status examination** — appearance, rapport, affect, speech, thought form and content, perception, cognition, insight and judgement.

### 2. Differential diagnosis

| Diagnosis | For | Against / what to check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Schizophrenia, insidious onset (negative-symptom predominant)** | 2-year decline, withdrawal, odd preoccupations; peak age | Needs delusions/hallucinations/thought disorder; DSM-5 requires 6 months including prodrome [1] |
| **Clinical high-risk / prodromal state** (attenuated psychosis) | Functional decline + unusual ideas below delusional intensity | Transition to psychosis in about **22% at 1 year and 36% at 3 years** [1] |
| **Schizotypal disorder** | Magical thinking, odd beliefs about supernatural, social withdrawal | Lifelong pattern rather than a clear decline |
| **Simple schizophrenia** (ICD-10 concept) | Insidious negative symptoms, decline, without florid psychosis | Not in DSM-5 or ICD-11; diagnose with caution |
| **Depressive disorder** (including psychotic depression) | Thoughts of death, withdrawal, poor studies | Pervasive low mood, guilt, biological symptoms |
| **Bipolar disorder** | Supernatural/grandiose ideas | Episodic course, elevated mood |
| **OCD with existential/religious obsessions** | Repetitive thoughts on death and meaning | Ego-dystonic, resisted, with anxiety and compulsions; insight present |
| **Substance-induced psychotic disorder** (cannabis) | Common in young men | Temporal link, urine screen |
| **Autism spectrum disorder** | Social withdrawal, restricted preoccupations | Lifelong from early childhood |
| **Social anxiety / avoidant personality** | Withdrawal | Fear of evaluation; no unusual beliefs |
| **Organic** — temporal lobe epilepsy, Wilson's disease, hypothyroidism, frontal lesion, HIV | Must be excluded at first presentation | Neurological signs, seizures, KF rings, labs |
| **Culturally sanctioned belief / normal adolescent existential concern** | Religious or philosophical interest is common | No functional decline, shared by peers, not fixed or idiosyncratic |

### 3. Approach to management

**Step 1 — Assessment and engagement**
- Build trust; a young man with insidious psychosis may not see himself as ill. Involve family with his consent.
- Full MSE; rating scales: **CAARMS/SIPS** (if attenuated symptoms), **PANSS**, SANS, Calgary Depression Scale, Columbia suicide scale.
- **Investigations:** CBC, glucose, lipids, LFT, renal function, TSH, B12, HIV, VDRL, urine drug screen; **serum ceruloplasmin and slit-lamp for KF rings** (Wilson's disease at this age); **EEG** if episodic or seizure-like symptoms; **MRI brain** if neurological signs, atypical features or cognitive decline; baseline weight, BMI, waist, BP, ECG before antipsychotics [2].
- **Cognitive assessment** — deficits are often present at the prodrome stage and predict transition [1].

**Step 2 — Manage according to the diagnosis**

**(a) If clinical high-risk (no established psychosis):**
- Monitoring every 2–4 weeks for 2–3 years; **CBT** for attenuated symptoms; treat comorbid depression, anxiety and substance use; family psychoeducation; educational support.
- **Antipsychotics are not first line** in the at-risk state; consider only if symptoms reach psychotic threshold, or if risk is severe and rapidly worsening.

**(b) If schizophrenia (first episode):**
- **Second-generation antipsychotic at the minimum effective dose**, chosen with the patient for its adverse-effect profile, preferably one available as a long-acting injection [2] — for example **aripiprazole 10 mg**, **risperidone 2 mg**, or **olanzapine 5 mg** (first-episode minimum effective doses) [2].
- Assess over **2–3 weeks**; if no effect, increase or switch; if partial response, continue for a total of about **10 weeks** [2].
- Two adequate failures → **clozapine** early [2].
- Consider an **LAI** before discharge to prevent relapse [2].
- Continue for at least **1–2 years** after remission (first episode); taper slowly if stopping [2].

**(c) If depression, OCD or substance-induced disorder:** treat accordingly (SSRI and CBT; ERP; abstinence and motivational work).

**Step 3 — Psychosocial interventions**
- **Psychoeducation** for patient and family — explain the illness, early warning signs, medication.
- **Family intervention** to reduce expressed emotion.
- **CBT for psychosis**, social skills training, **cognitive remediation** for cognitive deficits.
- **Supported education** — liaison with college, study plan, exam concessions; **IPS** later for work.
- Stop cannabis and other substances.

**Step 4 — Risk management**
- Suicide risk is highest early in psychosis; safety plan, means restriction, frequent follow-up.
- Admission if high risk, severe self-neglect or diagnostic uncertainty requiring observation (MHCA 2017 — independent admission if he has capacity; supported admission under Section 89 if he lacks capacity and is at risk).

**Step 5 — Follow-up and early intervention**
- Refer to an **early intervention in psychosis** service if available; otherwise intensive outpatient follow-up with case management.
- Physical health monitoring: weight, BMI, glucose, lipids at baseline, 6–12 weeks and annually [2].

**Conclusion.** This young man's insidious decline with unusual preoccupations should be treated as a possible emerging psychosis. Careful phenomenology — distinguishing delusions from obsessions, depressive ruminations and cultural beliefs — and a thorough organic and substance work-up decide the path. If he is at clinical high risk, monitor closely and offer CBT without antipsychotics; if schizophrenia is established, start a low-dose SGA with a comprehensive psychosocial and educational package to protect his future.

## In depth

### Background

- **Insidious onset with negative symptoms and poor premorbid adjustment** has long been recognised as a poor-prognosis pattern; Kraepelin's dementia praecox emphasised early decline [1].
- **Metaphysical or pseudo-philosophical preoccupation** — vague, abstract theorising about life, death, the universe or the supernatural — is a classic early feature of schizophrenia in adolescence and young adulthood (author's note).
- The **clinical high-risk (ultra-high-risk)** concept emerged in the 1990s (PACE clinic, Melbourne; North American Prodrome Longitudinal Study) to identify help-seeking young people before the first episode [1].

### Epidemiology

- Schizophrenia affects about **1%** of the population; peak onset in men is late adolescence to mid-20s [1].
- Among people meeting clinical high-risk criteria, transition to psychosis is about **18% at 6 months, 22% at 1 year and 36% at 3 years**; the high false-positive rate is a major concern [1].
- In ultra-high-risk samples, **10–40%** eventually develop the illness; prediction based on cognitive tests alone is weak [1].

### Classification and criteria

- **DSM-5 schizophrenia:** at least 2 of — delusions, hallucinations, disorganised speech, grossly disorganised/catatonic behaviour, negative symptoms (at least one of the first three); functional decline; **6 months** total, including at least **1 month** of active symptoms; prodromal and residual phases count toward the 6 months [1].
- **ICD-10/ICD-11:** require **1 month** of symptoms; prodromal phase not counted; ICD does not require functional deterioration [1].
- **Attenuated psychosis syndrome** is listed in DSM-5 Section III (conditions for further study).
- **Simple schizophrenia** (ICD-10 F20.6): insidious negative symptoms and decline without florid psychosis for at least a year; removed from ICD-11 because of poor reliability.

### Mechanism in detail

Neurodevelopmental vulnerability (genetic load, obstetric complications) → subtle childhood deficits → adolescent synaptic pruning and dopamine dysregulation, worsened by cannabis or stress → prodrome (attenuated positive symptoms, negative symptoms, cognitive decline) → psychosis [1]. Cognitive deficits are already present at the prodrome and at the first episode, at a magnitude similar to established illness [1].

### Clinical features in detail — distinguishing types of thought

| Type | Features | Suggests |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Delusion | Fixed, false, idiosyncratic, not shared culturally, held despite evidence | Psychosis |
| Overvalued idea | Understandable, preoccupying, emotionally charged, not fully fixed | Personality, anorexia, hypochondriasis |
| Obsession | Intrusive, ego-dystonic, resisted, recognised as own thought | OCD |
| Depressive rumination | Mood-congruent (death, futility), with low mood | Depression |
| Magical thinking | Superstitious, partially held | Schizotypal traits, childhood, culture |
| Cultural/religious belief | Shared by community, appropriate context | Normal |

Signs suggesting emerging psychosis: increasing **idiosyncrasy**, **self-reference** ("powers are working on me"), **perplexity**, odd behaviour, social withdrawal, and decline in function.

### Assessment in detail

| Tool | Use |
| --- | --- |
| CAARMS / SIPS (SOPS) | Clinical high-risk assessment |
| PANSS, SAPS/SANS | Positive and negative symptom severity [1] |
| Calgary Depression Scale | Depression in psychosis |
| Y-BOCS | If obsessions suspected |
| MoCA / neuropsychological testing | Cognition — attention, memory, executive function [1] |
| Social and occupational functioning scale (SOFAS/GAF) | Functional decline (required in CHR criteria) |

### Management details

- **Antipsychotic choice in a young man:** weight gain, metabolic effects, sexual dysfunction and hyperprolactinaemia drive non-adherence; aripiprazole or low-dose risperidone are common first choices; olanzapine is effective but metabolically costly [2].
- **Dose escalation:** increase only after 1–2 weeks of clear poor response [2].
- **FGAs** should probably be reserved for second- or third-line because of higher risk of movement disorders, particularly TD [2].
- **Clozapine** should be offered as soon as treatment resistance is apparent; delay reduces response [2].
- **LAIs** reduce relapse and readmission in first-episode patients, who will accept them [2].
- **Physical health:** baseline and repeat glucose, lipids, weight, BP, prolactin if symptomatic; ECG [2].

### Evidence table

| Evidence | Finding |
| --- | --- |
| CHR meta-analyses (cited in K&S) | Transition 18% / 22% / 36% at 6 months / 1 year / 3 years [1] |
| Prodrome neurocognition studies | Cognitive impairment present at prodrome; greatest impairment → highest risk of psychosis [1] |
| First-episode treatment data | Improvement usually apparent within 2–3 weeks of an effective dose [2] |
| LAI studies in first-episode schizophrenia | Relapse and readmission markedly reduced [2] |

### Critical appraisal

- **Over-diagnosis risk:** labelling a young person as "prodromal" when most will not develop psychosis causes stigma and unnecessary medication [1].
- **Under-diagnosis risk:** insidious psychosis without florid symptoms is often missed for years in India, attributed to laziness, "bad company" or spiritual causes, lengthening DUP.
- Distinguishing culturally normal supernatural beliefs from delusions requires knowledge of the patient's community — ask family and use the "shared by others in his culture?" test.

### Indian context

- Supernatural explanations (black magic, possession, planetary influence) are common in Indian families; patients may first be taken to religious healers, delaying care.
- Duration of untreated psychosis in India is often long; early detection through colleges, primary care and the **District Mental Health Programme** helps.
- Academic pressure and competitive examinations may mask or precipitate decline; liaison with colleges and use of disability provisions (**RPwD Act 2016**) can protect education.
- **MHCA 2017:** an adult of 19 has the right to confidentiality and to consent; family involvement needs his agreement unless he lacks capacity or there is serious risk.

### Recent advances

> Not covered in the uploaded sources; from author's knowledge — check before marking `reviewed`.

- Transition rates from CHR to psychosis appear to be **declining** in recent cohorts, reinforcing caution about early antipsychotics.
- **Individualised risk calculators** (NAPLS-2 risk calculator) combine symptoms, cognition and function to estimate transition risk.
- Large ongoing trials of psychosocial and novel pharmacological interventions in CHR (e.g. omega-3 fatty acids failed to replicate early benefit).
