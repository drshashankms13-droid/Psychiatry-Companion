## Exam answer

**Definition.** Negative symptoms are the loss or reduction of normal functions — drive, pleasure, social interest, emotional expression and speech — as opposed to positive symptoms, which add abnormal experiences to normal mental life [1]. They account for much of the long-term disability in schizophrenia, predict poor functional outcome better than positive symptoms, and remain the largest unmet treatment need [1, 2].

### Concept and classification

- **History:** Kraepelin's "emotional dullness" and Bleuler's "emotional deterioration" placed them at the core of the illness. Schneider's first-rank symptoms shifted attention to positive symptoms, and negative symptoms became under-recognised [1]. **Crow (1980)** proposed Type I (positive, drug-responsive) and Type II (negative, poorly responsive, linked to structural brain change) [1].
- **NIMH-MATRICS consensus (2005): five domains** grouped into **two factors** [1, 2]:

| Factor | Domains | How assessed |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Avolition–amotivation** (motivation and pleasure) | Avolition, anhedonia, asociality | Interview — patient's inner experience and behaviour |
| **Diminished expression** | Blunted affect, alogia | Observed by the clinician |

- **DSM-5** lists "diminished emotional expression or avolition" as a Criterion A symptom [1]. **ICD-11** rates negative symptoms as a separate symptom domain specifier.
- **Anhedonia in schizophrenia is anticipatory, not consummatory:** patients enjoy pleasant activities as much as controls while doing them but anticipate less pleasure, so they lack motivation to seek them [1].
- Only **reduced speech output** is alogia; normal amount of speech with poor content is a disorganisation symptom. Inattention also belongs with disorganisation [1].

### Frequency and course

- About **60%** of people with established schizophrenia have clinically relevant negative symptoms; up to **20%** have persistent primary negative symptoms [2]. In **CATIE**, 40% had significant negative symptoms [1].
- Present in the prodrome and at onset (anhedonia in about half at first episode); may increase over the first years [1].
- Associated with male sex, early onset, poor premorbid function and long duration of untreated illness [1].

### Primary versus secondary — the central clinical step

| Secondary cause | Clue | Remedy |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Positive symptoms** (fearful withdrawal, distracting voices) | Withdrawal tracks psychosis | Optimise antipsychotic |
| **Depression** | Low mood, guilt, hopelessness | Antidepressant, psychological therapy |
| **Drug-induced parkinsonism / sedation** | Bradykinesia, rigidity, high dose | Reduce dose, switch to low-EPS drug |
| **Environmental deprivation** (institution, poverty) | Improves with stimulation | Rehabilitation, activity |
| **Substances, social anxiety, sleep apnoea, pain** | History, examination | Treat the cause |

Secondary symptoms usually resolve within about a year once the cause is treated; symptoms that persist beyond a year and do not change with medication adjustment are likely primary [1].

- **Deficit syndrome (Carpenter; Schedule for the Deficit Syndrome):** at least 2 of 6 primary negative symptoms present for the past 12 months and during clinical stability. Seen in about **10–30%** (meta-analysis about 30%). It carries poor functional prognosis but less dysphoria and suicidality [1].
- **Persistent negative symptoms (Buchanan):** at least 2 negative symptoms for at least 12 months during stability, not explained by comorbidity or medication. This is a pragmatic alternative when primary and secondary cannot be separated [1].

### Assessment

History from patient and family (initiative, pleasure, relationships, self-care) → mental state examination (affect, speech, eye contact) → screen for each secondary cause (depression scale, EPS examination, dose review, substances, sleep) → rating scales:
- **SANS** (Andreasen): affective flattening, alogia, avolition–apathy, anhedonia–asociality, attention [1].
- **PANSS negative subscale**; second-generation scales **BNSS** and **CAINS** separate anticipatory and consummatory pleasure.
- Calgary Depression Scale for Schizophrenia (avoids overlap with negative symptoms); Simpson–Angus Scale for parkinsonism.

### Management

**Step 1 — Prevent:** treat psychosis early; earlier effective treatment may reduce later negative symptoms [2].

**Step 2 — Treat secondary causes:** control positive symptoms; treat depression; reduce dose or switch for EPS and sedation; reduce anticholinergics; enrich the environment [1, 2].

**Step 3 — Choose the antipsychotic wisely** (lowest effective dose):
- No consistent superiority of SGAs over FGAs; apparent SGA benefit may be only reduction of EPS-related secondary symptoms [1, 2].
- Best evidence for a specific drug: **cariprazine** (superior to risperidone in predominant negative symptoms, Németh 2017); also **amisulpride** (low dose) and **aripiprazole** [2].
- **Clozapine**: superior for treatment resistance, but its effect on negative symptoms is uncertain [2]. Adding cariprazine to clozapine may help residual negative symptoms [2].

**Step 4 — Augmentation (modest, inconsistent evidence):**
- **Antidepressants** — SSRIs (fluvoxamine, citalopram), mirtazapine, mianserin. Meta-analyses show a small effect, possibly mainly on secondary symptoms [1, 2].
- **Memantine** (three meta-analyses positive) [2].
- Minocycline was **not effective** in a large RCT, and the BeneMin trial was also negative [2].

**Step 5 — Psychosocial treatment (the mainstay for primary symptoms):** social skills training, CBT targeting defeatist beliefs and low expectancy of pleasure, cognitive remediation, **supported employment (IPS)**, family psychoeducation, exercise.

**Step 6 — Neuromodulation:** rTMS to left DLPFC — mixed but promising; tDCS — limited evidence [2].

### Key evidence

- **CATIE (2005):** 40% had significant negative symptoms; no antipsychotic differed meaningfully on cognition [1].
- **Leucht 2009 / Fusar-Poli 2015 meta-analyses:** SGAs reduce negative symptoms statistically, but below the threshold for clinically meaningful change [2].
- **Németh 2017 (Lancet):** cariprazine beat risperidone on PANSS negative factor at 26 weeks in predominant negative symptoms [2].
- **BAP 2020 and EPA 2021 guidance:** no pharmacological treatment has convincing, enduring benefit for primary negative symptoms [2].

**Conclusion.** Most negative symptoms seen in practice are partly secondary and treatable, so the key skill is detecting and treating their causes. For persistent primary negative symptoms, no drug works well; the realistic strategy is the lowest effective dose of a well-chosen antipsychotic (cariprazine or amisulpride where appropriate), a cautious trial of augmentation, and sustained psychosocial rehabilitation aimed at function rather than symptom scores.

## In depth

### Background

- Negative symptoms were downgraded when the DSM-III emphasised reliable positive symptoms; flat affect returned in DSM-III-R through the work of Strauss, Carpenter, Crow and Andreasen [1].
- Adding negative symptoms to diagnostic criteria did not increase the incidence of schizophrenia, which suggests they had been present all along but unrecognised [1].
- Factor analyses of PANSS across cultures consistently show **five** symptom dimensions — psychotic, disorganisation, negative, mood/anxiety and agitation — rather than the simple positive/negative split [1].

### Epidemiology

- Mild negative symptoms occur in **5–10% of the general population** without psychiatric illness and after brain injury, but a significant enduring negative syndrome is far more common in schizophrenia than in mood disorders [1].
- In chronic populations about half have at least one overt negative symptom [1].
- Alogia is present in up to 25%, and up to 25% develop significant negative symptoms within 2 years of onset [1].
- Negative symptoms are **more heritable than psychotic symptoms but less heritable than disorganisation symptoms**; relatives of patients with negative symptoms show more negative symptoms [1].
- The deficit syndrome shows an excess of **summer births**, the reverse of the winter–spring excess seen in schizophrenia overall [1].
- Substance users with psychosis tend to have fewer negative symptoms, probably because cannabis-associated psychosis occurs in people with fewer neurodevelopmental risk factors rather than through a drug effect [2].

### Classification and criteria

**Schedule for the Deficit Syndrome — the six negative symptoms** [1]:
1. Restricted affect
2. Diminished emotional range
3. Poverty of speech
4. Curbing of interests
5. Diminished sense of purpose
6. Diminished social drive

At least two must be present for the preceding 12 months, and not be better explained by other symptoms, another psychiatric illness or medication. Reliable rating requires training [1].

**Items of the SANS** [1]:

| Subscale | Items |
| --- | --- |
| Affective flattening | Unchanging facial expression, decreased spontaneous movement, paucity of gestures, poor eye contact, affective non-responsivity, lack of vocal inflection |
| Alogia | Poverty of speech, poverty of content, blocking, increased response latency |
| Avolition–apathy | Grooming and hygiene, impersistence at work or school, physical anergia |
| Anhedonia–asociality | Recreational interests, sexual interest, intimacy, relationships with friends |
| Attention | Social inattentiveness, inattentiveness during testing |

Note that poverty of content and attention are now regarded as disorganisation items [1].

**Avolition vs apathy:** avolition is a deficit in the ability to initiate and persist in goal-directed activity (in neurology, abulia); apathy is a loss of concern for an idea or task. They are closely related [1].

### Mechanism in detail

- **Reward processing:** reduced anticipatory pleasure → reduced effort to seek reward → avolition. This explains the strong correlation between anhedonia and avolition in rating scales [1].
- **Dopamine:** hypodopaminergic mesocortical transmission (prefrontal) is proposed to underlie negative and cognitive symptoms, alongside mesolimbic hyperdopaminergia for positive symptoms.
- **Neuropathology:** negative symptoms correlate with measures of frontal lobe function and reduced cerebral tissue volume [1].
- **Cognition:** negative symptoms and cognitive impairment correlate modestly but are separate domains; emotion recognition is the one cognitive deficit counted as negative [1].
- **Social cognition:** asociality is associated with deficits in theory of mind [1].
- **Iatrogenic phenocopy:** antipsychotics at excessive doses cause blunted affect, reduced movement, poor self-care and anhedonia. EPS rating scales may not detect this, so a dose reduction trial is the test [1].

### Clinical features in detail

- **Affective blunting** includes both reduced emotional expression and reduced recognition of others' emotions. It is more common in men, early onset and poor premorbid function, and predicts lower quality of life [1].
- **Social withdrawal** is passive — indifference to relationships, reduced sexual interest, inability to feel closeness — unlike the active, fearful withdrawal driven by persecutory delusions [1].
- **Akinesia at first admission** predicts later significant negative symptoms [1].
- Negative symptoms often **rise and fall with acute exacerbations**, and then improve when the positive symptoms are treated [1].
- Patients with prominent negative symptoms have poorer insight yet often rate their own quality of life higher than clinicians do [1].
- **Deficit syndrome patients:** lower IQ, less education, worse work history, more often unmarried men, insidious onset, more disorganisation; fewer severe social delusions, less suspiciousness, less dysphoria and fewer suicide attempts [1].

### Assessment in detail

| Tool | Use |
| --- | --- |
| SANS | Detailed 5-domain rating (Andreasen) [1] |
| PANSS negative subscale / Marder negative factor | Trial outcome measure |
| BNSS (Brief Negative Symptom Scale) | 13 items covering all 5 MATRICS domains, separates anticipatory and consummatory pleasure |
| CAINS | Clinical Assessment Interview for Negative Symptoms; motivation/pleasure and expression scales |
| Schedule for the Deficit Syndrome | Identifies primary enduring negative symptoms [1] |
| Calgary Depression Scale for Schizophrenia | Depression without overlap with negative items |
| Simpson–Angus Scale | Drug-induced parkinsonism [2] |
| Personal and Social Performance scale | Functional outcome |

Practical test of secondary causes: observe whether negative symptoms change after reducing the antipsychotic dose, treating depression, or changing the environment over several months [1].

### Differential diagnosis

| Condition | Distinguishing point |
| --- | --- |
| Depression in schizophrenia | Subjective low mood, guilt, hopelessness, suicidal ideation; negative symptoms lack inner distress |
| Drug-induced parkinsonism | Rigidity, tremor, bradykinesia; dose-related; improves with dose reduction or anticholinergic |
| Akinesia / bradyphrenia | Slowed thinking with motor slowing, often with high-potency drugs [2] |
| Positive symptoms | Withdrawal explained by fear or hallucinations |
| Institutionalism | Improves with stimulation and community living |
| Catatonia (retarded) | Mutism, posturing, negativism, waxy flexibility |
| Frontal lobe syndrome | Late onset, neurological signs, imaging findings |
| Autism spectrum disorder | Lifelong social difficulty, restricted interests, from early childhood |

### Management details

**Pharmacological evidence** [2]:
- A 2015 meta-analysis of 38 RCTs: SGAs reduced negative symptoms significantly, but the effect fell below the "minimally detectable clinical improvement" threshold.
- Single trials suggest olanzapine and quetiapine may be more effective than risperidone; a 2023 review listed **amisulpride and cariprazine** as the most promising for primary negative symptoms.
- Clozapine studies are confounded because clozapine causes little parkinsonism, which overlaps with expressive deficits.
- **Antidepressant augmentation:** a 2006 Cochrane review found possible benefit on affective flattening, alogia and avolition; one meta-analysis found benefit only when added to FGAs; benefit is reported for fluvoxamine, citalopram, mirtazapine, mianserin and possibly reboxetine.
- **Other agents with some evidence:** memantine, topiramate, Ginkgo biloba, COX-2 inhibitors (small effect), selegiline, pramipexole, ondansetron, granisetron, pimavanserin, roluperidone; experimental — pregnenolone, raloxifene (women), xanomeline–trospium.
- **Failed:** minocycline (large RCT and BeneMin); inconsistent evidence for lamotrigine augmentation of clozapine.
- When augmenting, choose agents that minimise pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic interactions [2].

**Antipsychotic polypharmacy:** no high-quality evidence supports it, except possibly adding a partial D2 agonist to a dopamine antagonist for negative symptoms — and it is unclear whether this helps primary or secondary symptoms [1].

**Psychosocial approaches:**
- **Psychiatric rehabilitation** combines creating opportunities, providing supports and increasing skills [1].
- **IPS supported employment:** more than 20 RCTs show employment rates about **three times higher** than traditional vocational programmes, without causing relapse; pre-employment skills training alone does not improve vocational outcome [1].
- Social skills training, CBT for psychosis adapted to negative symptoms, cognitive remediation, family interventions and exercise programmes are recommended in the European Psychiatric Association guidance (Galderisi 2021) [2].

| Study | Design | Main finding |

| MATRICS consensus, 2005 | Expert consensus | Five domains in two factors [1] |

| Fusar-Poli et al., 2015 | Meta-analysis, 38 RCTs | SGA effect statistically significant but below clinical threshold [2] |
| Németh et al., 2017 | RCT, n = 461 | Cariprazine > risperidone for predominant negative symptoms [2] |
| Deakin et al., 2018 (BeneMin) | RCT | Minocycline did not prevent or improve negative symptoms [2] |
| Aleman et al., 2017 | Review of meta-analyses | Modest evidence for some antidepressants, psychosocial interventions [2] |
| IPS trials (Drake, Bond) | More than 20 RCTs | Employment about 3× higher than standard vocational rehabilitation [1] |

### Critical appraisal

- **Primary vs secondary is often unknowable in routine practice**; time constraints and limited resources prevent sequential drug trials and environmental change [1]. The "persistent negative symptoms" construct is a pragmatic workaround [1].
- **Trial design problems:** most data come from sub-analyses of acute trials in which negative symptoms improve alongside positive ones; few trials recruit patients with predominant or persistent negative symptoms; two-factor subdomains are rarely analysed separately [2].
- **The SGA promise was overstated:** apparent advantages largely reflect less EPS rather than direct action on primary symptoms [1, 2].
- **Overtreatment is common:** excessive antipsychotic doses produce a phenocopy of negative symptoms, so higher doses can make the problem worse [1].
- **Measurement:** older scales mix negative, cognitive and disorganisation items; the newer BNSS and CAINS improve validity.
- **Function, not symptoms:** functional recovery depends more on negative and cognitive symptoms than on positive symptoms, so rehabilitation outcomes should be the main target [1].

### Indian context

- Families often interpret avolition as laziness or lack of discipline; psychoeducation reduces criticism and expressed emotion.
- Large treatment gaps and long duration of untreated psychosis increase the risk of persistent negative symptoms.
- Formal vocational rehabilitation is scarce; community-based rehabilitation and family-run small businesses are common routes back to work. The COPSI trial (2014) showed a community-based intervention delivered by lay health workers improved disability modestly.
- The **Rights of Persons with Disabilities Act 2016** recognises chronic mental illness as a benchmark disability; disability certification (IDEAS) can give access to benefits and reservation in employment.
- Cariprazine and amisulpride are available in India and are relatively affordable generics.

### Recent advances

- **Xanomeline–trospium** (muscarinic M1/M4 agonist, FDA 2024) — signals on negative symptoms are being studied [2].
- **Roluperidone** (5-HT2A/σ2 antagonist) — positive phase 3 signal on negative symptoms but not yet approved [2].
- **Digital phenotyping** (passive smartphone activity, voice and facial analysis) to measure motivation and expression objectively.
- **Non-invasive brain stimulation:** rTMS (mixed but promising) and tDCS (limited evidence) [2].
- Second-generation scales (BNSS, CAINS) now used as primary outcomes in negative-symptom trials.
