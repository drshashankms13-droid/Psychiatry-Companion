## Exam answer

**Introduction.**
- Irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) is the **most frequently encountered functional GI disorder** [1].
- **Functional GI disorders** (Rome consensus): "variable combinations of chronic or recurrent GI symptoms which are not explained by structural or biochemical abnormalities". There are more than 25 such disorders [1].
- **Prevalence:** more than **10%** of adults. It peaks in the 2nd, 3rd and 4th decades, with a **female preponderance** [1].
- **Burden:**
  - more than one-third of new secondary-care GI patients have a functional GI disorder;
  - functional GI disorders cause absenteeism of about **13 days a year** vs 5 in controls;
  - they reduce work productivity and quality of life, but carry **no mortality** per se [1].

### Clinical features [1]
- **Core:** recurrent abdominal pain or discomfort, with altered stool frequency or consistency, relieved by defecation.
- **Extra-GI features:** lassitude, headache, dysmenorrhoea, dyspareunia.
- Symptoms wax and wane. Pain or discomfort occurs on about 3 days a week, often in clusters.
- **Subtypes** by predominant bowel habit: **IBS-C** (constipation), **IBS-D** (diarrhoea), **IBS-M** (mixed/alternating), **IBS-U** (unclassified).

**Rome III criteria [1]:**
- recurrent abdominal pain or discomfort **at least 3 days a month in the past 3 months**;
- with **two or more** of: improvement with defecation; onset with a change in stool frequency; onset with a change in stool form;
- criteria fulfilled for the past 3 months, with **onset at least 6 months** before diagnosis;
- "discomfort" means an uncomfortable sensation not described as pain.

### Psychiatric comorbidity [1]
- **Depression, anxiety and hypochondriasis** affect **more than half** of patients with IBS.
- Up to **75%** of patients at tertiary referral centres meet criteria for anxiety or depression.
- **15–45%** of patients with functional GI disorders also meet criteria for **somatisation disorder**. They often have fibromyalgia, chronic fatigue and chronic generalised pain, and are typically "non-responders" in drug trials.
- **"Presentation bias":** patients presenting only with somatic complaints are often not diagnosed:
  - only 22% of patients with solely somatic complaints were correctly diagnosed with anxiety or depression, vs 77% of those presenting with psychosocial problems;
  - the **more physical symptoms**, the more psychological distress (Kroenke: mood or anxiety disorder in 4% with 0–1 symptoms, rising to **78% with 9 or more**).

### Aetiopathogenesis: the biopsychosocial model and gut–brain axis [1]
- **Gut–brain axis:** bidirectional communication between gut and brain through the CNS (spinal and cerebral), ANS and HPA axis.
- **Neurotransmitters** that modify gut motility and sensation: **5-HT, noradrenaline, CRF, opioids**.
- **Dysregulation at any level** (peripheral, spinal, cerebral) can cause chronic symptoms. **Dysmotility alone** does not explain the symptoms.
- **Predisposing:**
  - adverse early life events (maternal separation in animal models);
  - **childhood sexual abuse**, which alters visceral pain sensitivity;
  - deprivation, personal losses, separation.
- **Precipitating:** infection (**post-infective IBS**: distress at the time of gastroenteritis independently predicts it); abdominal surgery; life events.
- **Perpetuating:**
  - **catastrophising** and maladaptive illness behaviour, which maintain **visceral hypersensitivity**;
  - alexithymia; ongoing work stress;
  - repeated examinations and poor or over-technical explanations, which increase fear.
- **Physiological effects of stress:** it aggravates motility, lowers pain thresholds and increases gut inflammation.
- **Central sensitisation** in severe, refractory cases.
- The importance of psychosocial factors is **proportional to severity**.

### Psychiatric evaluation [1]
1. **Build the therapeutic relationship:** elicit beliefs, concerns and expectations; empathise; clarify misunderstandings; educate; negotiate a plan.
2. **Validate the physical symptoms.** Many patients have been told, in effect, "it must be in your head".
3. **Avoid presentation bias:** "going slow is going fast" and "don't do something, stand there".
4. **Symptom diary:** bowel symptoms, stool frequency and consistency, menstruation, diet, lifestyle and stressors. This links symptoms to psychosocial factors and prepares for CBT.
5. **Screen for** depression, anxiety and panic, somatisation, history of abuse, and functional impairment.

### Management [1]
**General approach:**
- **Reassurance** once the patient's specific concerns are understood. Stress the overall favourable prognosis.
- **Accept the adaptations of chronic illness:** explain the illness as a troublesome symptom cluster, not an undiagnosed pathology.
- Focus on **function rather than cure**.
- **Minimise investigations** and symptomatic treatments; reinforce health-promoting behaviour.

**Specific psychiatric approach:** treat
- psychiatric disorders (major depression, panic disorder);
- abuse that interferes with adjustment;
- serious functional impairment (coping skills);
- somatisation.

**Pharmacotherapy:**
- **Antidepressants:**
  - an odds ratio of **2.6–4.2** for overall improvement; **NNT 3.2**;
  - efficacy is **independent of mood change** in at least five studies;
  - **TCAs and SNRIs** for pain, especially **diarrhoea-predominant** IBS;
  - **SSRIs** for somatisation with anxiety or phobias, especially **constipation-predominant** IBS;
  - **mirtazapine** for nausea and poor appetite.
- **Low-dose antipsychotics** (risperidone, olanzapine, quetiapine) for visceral pain with high arousal.
- **Carbamazepine, valproate, pregabalin** for chronic visceral pain.
- Start with monotherapy. Combinations at lower doses may be better tolerated.
- **Benzodiazepines:** only short-term; long-term use only after other options fail.

**Psychological therapies** (effective as a class; no single therapy is superior):
- **CBT:** in Lackner's trial, therapist-delivered CBT gave pain relief in **60.9%** and self-administered CBT in **72%**, vs **7.4%** with usual care.
- **Gut-focused hypnotherapy:**
  - more efficacious than standard care (Cochrane review);
  - cost-effective in severe refractory cases;
  - best in younger patients without serious psychopathology;
  - availability is limited.
- **Relaxation therapy:** progressive muscle relaxation, biofeedback, autogenic training, **MBSR**, Tai-Chi, meditation.
- **Interpersonal therapy:** grief, role transitions, relationship discord.
- **Psychodynamic therapy.**
- **Biofeedback** (mainly for constipation or incontinence).

**Conclusion.** IBS is a common functional GI disorder of disordered gut–brain axis regulation. More than half of patients have psychiatric comorbidity, often hidden by presentation bias. The psychiatrist's role is to:
- validate and engage;
- identify and treat depression, anxiety, somatisation and abuse;
- use antidepressants (TCA or SNRI for pain and diarrhoea; SSRI for anxiety and constipation; NNT about 3);
- use psychological therapies (CBT, hypnotherapy);
- limit investigations and focus on function.

## In depth

### Psychosomatic history [1]
- Phrases such as "butterflies in my stomach" and "gut wrenching" reflect the link between anxiety and the gut.
- **William Beaumont** (1785–1853) described the influence of emotions on gastric acid secretion.
- **Peptic ulcer** was considered psychosomatic before *Helicobacter pylori* was identified. This illustrates the limits of the "organic" vs "functional" split.
- **IBD and IBS may lie on one spectrum**, because many IBD patients remain symptomatic after mucosal healing.

### Related functional GI disorders [1]
- **Functional abdominal pain (FAP):**
  - continuous or near-continuous pain, unrelated to physiological events, with some loss of function;
  - the pain is real, not feigned;
  - prevalence 0.5–2%;
  - no consensus on treatment.
- **Functional chest pain of presumed oesophageal origin (FCP):**
  - recurrent midline chest pain;
  - **up to 61%** have psychological comorbidity;
  - proposed mechanisms: visceral hypersensitivity, stress systems (ANS, HPA), psychological comorbidity.

### Neuroplasticity rationale [1]
- Imaging shows **reduced neuronal density** in emotion- and pain-regulating cortex in pain disorders and IBS, as in depression and PTSD.
- Antidepressants raise **BDNF** and stimulate neurogenesis, offering a rationale for centrally acting treatment.

### Prescribing patterns [1]
- Up to **1 in 8** patients with a functional GI disorder are offered an antidepressant.
- UK data: patients on long-term antispasmodics are **2–4 times** more likely to receive CNS drugs: antidepressants 35.4%, anxiolytics 27.8%, antipsychotics 9.8%, hypnosedatives 32.7%.

### Clinical case (K&S) [1]
- A 35-year-old woman had 3 years of pain, distension and alternating bowel habit. She denied affective symptoms.
- After validation and rapport, she disclosed fatigability, anhedonia, sleep disturbance, weight loss, low mood, marital and work stress, and lifelong perfectionism.
- **Learning points:**
  - visceral hypersensitivity makes colonoscopy painful;
  - standard medical therapies often fail;
  - patients are often unwilling to engage with psychiatry;
  - chronic functional symptoms are frequently associated with psychopathology.

### Why psychiatrists have done poorly [1]
- Gastroenterologists often report unsatisfactory outcomes after psychiatric referral.
- Patients resist referral when the gastroenterologist does not acknowledge psychosocial relevance; they perceive stigma or rejection.
