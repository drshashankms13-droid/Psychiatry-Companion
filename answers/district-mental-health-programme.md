## Exam answer

**Introduction.** The **National Mental Health Programme (NMHP, 1982)** aimed to make basic mental health care available and accessible to all, especially the most vulnerable, by **integrating mental health into general health care**. The **District Mental Health Programme (DMHP)** is NMHP's operational arm:
- **1985:** the NIMHANS **Bellary model** piloted it.
- **1996–97:** launched in **4 districts**.
- **By 2024:** **738 districts approved, 716 functional** [1, 2].

The statement is **partly true**. DMHP has achieved **massive geographic expansion** and proved that **district-level, primary-care-integrated** mental health care is feasible. But **coverage is not the same as effective care**: quality, staffing, drug supply, monitoring and outcomes lag far behind, and the treatment gap (70–92%) remains huge [1, 2, 3].

### NMHP: aims and strategies [1]
- **Aims:**
  - Prevention and treatment of mental and neurological disorders and their disabilities.
  - Use of mental health technology in general health services.
  - Application of mental health principles in national development.
- **Strategies:**
  - Integration with primary health care.
  - Tertiary institutions.
  - Rights protection through Central and State Mental Health Authorities.
  - Human resource development.

### Bellary model (1985) [1]
- **Setting:** a district of about 20 lakh people.
- **Components:**
  - Training of all PHC staff.
  - **Six essential drugs** at PHCs: chlorpromazine, amitriptyline, trihexyphenidyl, fluphenazine decanoate, phenobarbitone, phenytoin.
  - Simple case records.
  - Monthly reporting and monitoring by a district team (psychiatrist, clinical psychologist, PSW, statistical clerk).
  - A 10-bed unit.
- **Conclusion:** primary care doctors can be trained to deliver mental health care with specialist supervision [1].

### DMHP components and team
- **Components:** service provision (OPD, 10-bed inpatient, outreach clinics, emergencies); capacity building; IEC; targeted interventions (life skills and school counselling, college counselling, workplace stress management, **suicide prevention**); day-care and long-term care (PPP); disability certification [1].
- **Team:** psychiatrist, clinical psychologist, PSW, psychiatric/community nurse, programme manager, case-registry assistant, record keeper [1, 2].
- **Levels:**
  - District hospital: OPD/IPD, child services, perinatal, certification.
  - CHC/taluk: OPD and counselling.
  - PHC: common mental disorders, emergencies, follow-up of severe mental disorders, community case finding, access to benefits [1].

### Evidence for "great strides" (FOR)
| Achievement | Detail |
| --- | --- |
| **Expansion** | 4 districts (1996) → 27 (9th Plan) → 123 (11th) → **738 approved / 716 functional** (2024) [1, 2] |
| Workforce under DMHP | About 722 psychiatrists or trained MOs, 579 psychologists, 994 social workers, 3,551 nurses (2024; mapping of figures to cadres inferred from slide layout, so verify) [2] |
| Institutional strengthening | **25 Centres of Excellence**; **47 PG departments** supported; CIP Ranchi and LGBRIMH Tezpur upgraded [1, 2] |
| Integration | Mental, neurological and substance use (MNS) services in **Ayushman Arogya Mandirs** (2018 onward); **Tele-MANAS** (2022) linked to DMHP [2] |
| State innovations | Karnataka: Manochaitanya ("Super Tuesdays" at taluk hospitals), Manasadhara day-care, Manasakendra halfway homes, home-care, tele-psychiatry (hub-and-spoke from 2013), school and taluk mental health programmes [1]. Kerala: full DMHP coverage [3] |
| Legal and policy alignment | National Mental Health Policy 2014; MHCA 2017 right to care; NMHS 2016 data [2] |

### Evidence against (AGAINST)
| Problem | Detail |
| --- | --- |
| **Treatment gap persists** | NMHS: **70–92%**, about 85% for CMDs, about 91% for SUD [3] |
| **Uneven implementation** | Skewed distribution; some districts functional only on paper. Coverage in Punjab under 15% vs Kerala full [1, 3] |
| **Human resources** | Contractual posts, high attrition, frequent transfers, vacancies. Specialists 0.2–1.2 psychiatrists per lakh [1, 2] |
| **Drugs** | **Irregular drug inflow** (regular supply in only about 25% of DMHPs in one evaluation); supply-chain and quality issues [1, 2] |
| **Primary-care integration weak** | MOs and CHOs lack confidence prescribing psychotropics; most contacts happen at district hospitals (61%) rather than CHCs (12.7%) or PHCs (11.5%) [1] |
| **Monitoring** | Weak M&E; no real-time data; poor databases in some states [1, 2] |
| **Urban mental health neglected** | Low use of public facilities and high out-of-pocket costs in cities [2] |
| **Funding** | Mental health under 1% of the health budget; funds released late and underused [3] |
| **Community involvement** | Stigma; little trust in non-specialists; weak IEC [1] |
| **Curative focus** | NMHP criticised for emphasising cure over promotion and prevention; short-term goals; unclear administrative structure [1] |

### The response: DMHP 2.0 (2024 redesign) [2]
Its pillars:
1. Service delivery: a **mental health unit at the CHC level** and services at Ayushman Arogya Mandirs, with assured drugs.
2. Community support (support and carer groups).
3. **Urban mental health:** a 3-tier system with strengthened UPHCs.
4. Training and capacity building.
5. **Vertical linkages** (sub-centre/PHC to Centres of Excellence).
6. **Lateral linkages** with other programmes.
7. IEC.
8. Technology: Tele-MANAS and e-Sanjeevani integration, a smart drug supply chain, digital patient tracking, HMIS outcome indicators.
9. M&E.
10. **Ring-fenced financing.**

**Conclusion.** DMHP has made **great strides in reach and in proving feasibility**: from a single Bellary pilot to almost every district, with Tele-MANAS and primary-care integration. It has **not yet made great strides in effectiveness**: the treatment gap, staffing, drug supply, monitoring and quality remain serious failures. The statement is true for **coverage** and false for **outcomes**. DMHP 2.0's focus on CHC-level units, ring-fenced funds, urban services, digital monitoring and outcome indicators is the right direction, but it must be implemented and evaluated.

## In depth

### Background
- **Bhore Committee (1946):** found prevalence of mental illness of 2 per 1,000 and only 10,000 psychiatric beds for 400 million people [1].
- **WHO Expert Committee (1974, Sartorius):** integrate basic mental health care into general health services, delivered by non-specialists. **Alma Ata (1978):** "Health for All by 2000" [1].
- **NMHP:** first draft February 1981; final draft adopted by the Central Council of Health and Family Welfare in **August 1982**. India was among the first major countries to adopt such a national programme [1].
- **Pilots:** Sakalwara (NIMHANS, 1976) and Raipur Rani (PGIMER, 1975) rural mental health centres demonstrated primary care integration.
- **Bellary (1985)** → DMHP launched **1996** [1].
- **Re-strategised 2003:** modernising mental hospitals and upgrading general hospital psychiatry units.
- **2009:** manpower development schemes (Schemes A and B: Centres of Excellence and PG departments).
- **2014:** National Mental Health Policy.
- **2017:** MHCA.
- **2018:** MNS services in Ayushman Arogya Mandirs.
- **2022:** Tele-MANAS.
- **2024:** DMHP redesign [1, 2].

### Epidemiology underpinning the programme
- NMHS 2015–16: current prevalence 10.6%; treatment gap 70–92% [3].
- Human resources: psychiatrists about 0.3 per lakh nationally (WHO Atlas); far below need.

### Classification: programme components by plan period
| Plan | DMHP districts | Additions |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 9th (1997–2002) | 27 | Pilot expansion |
| 10th (2002–07) | 110 | Upgrading psychiatric wings, modernising state hospitals, IEC, M&E [1] |
| 11th (2007–12) | 123 | Workforce schemes, Centres of Excellence, support for Mental Health Authorities, central MH team [1] |
| 12th (2012–17) | +325 districts planned | Targeted interventions (life skills, suicide prevention) |
| 2017 onward | Under NHM flexipool | Near-universal approval; DMHP 2.0 (2024) [2] |

### Mechanism in detail: the theory of change
Train non-specialists → provide essential drugs → refer upwards and supervise downwards → bring care closer → shorter duration of untreated illness, better adherence and follow-up → less disability and less pressure on mental hospitals. **Failure points:** drug supply, supervision, staff turnover and data systems break the chain [1, 2].

### Clinical features in detail: an evaluation snapshot
- **Site of contact:** district hospital 61%, CHC 12.7%, PHC 11.5%. 18% of respondents had been referred to the district level [1].
- **Interpretation:** DMHP remains largely a district-hospital psychiatry service, not true primary-care integration [1].

### Assessment in detail: how to evaluate DMHP
- **Process indicators:** number of districts, staff positions filled, outreach camps, trainings.
- **Outcome indicators** (largely missing): treatment coverage, continuity, relapse, disability, suicide rates, cost.
- **DMHP 2.0** proposes a central mental health information system with defined indicators [2].

### Differential: comparison with other models
| Model | Feature |
| --- | --- |
| mhGAP (WHO) | Task-sharing with an intervention guide; evidence-based protocols |
| Kerala DMHP | Full coverage; community psychiatry and palliative-care linkages [3] |
| Karnataka innovations | Manochaitanya, Manasadhara, Taluk MHP, tele-psychiatry [1] |
| Tamil Nadu | State Mental Health Programme; rehabilitation partnerships |
| Lay-counsellor models (MANAS trial, Goa; PRIME) | Task-sharing for CMDs, effective in trials |

### Management details: what would make DMHP work
1. **Ring-fenced, timely funding** (not merged invisibly into NHM pools) [2].
2. **Permanent cadres**, career paths, retention incentives; train CHOs and MOs with on-consultation training [1, 2].
3. **Assured drug supply** through essential drug lists, e-procurement and the smart supply chain [2].
4. **True task-sharing:** ASHAs for case finding, PHQ-2 screening (CBAC), lay counsellors.
5. **Continuity of care:** line-listing of patients, tele-follow-up (Tele-MANAS), home care [2].
6. **Urban strategy** [2].
7. **Monitoring with outcome indicators** and independent evaluation [2].
8. **Community engagement:** carer groups, faith-healer partnerships, anti-stigma campaigns [1, 2].

### Critical appraisal
- **Coverage vs quality:** expansion has outpaced capacity. Many districts have only a psychiatrist and a few staff, with irregular outreach [1, 2].
- **Lack of outcome evaluation** makes "great strides" claims hard to verify.
- **Policy-law mismatch:** MHCA registration requirements and resource demands are not matched by funding [3].
- **Positive signal:** DMHP has created a district-level platform on which Tele-MANAS and Ayushman Arogya Mandirs now build [2].

### Indian context
- **Integration under NHM:** NMHP and DMHP now operate under the National Health Mission (NCD flexipool) [2].
- **Linked initiatives:** Tele-MANAS (14416); the National Suicide Prevention Strategy (2022); RKSK (adolescents); NPHCE (elderly); Nasha Mukt Bharat Abhiyaan.
- **MHCA 2017, Section 18:** a right to access mental health care, with services in every district. The government must reimburse care elsewhere if it is unavailable [3].

### Recent advances
- **DMHP 2.0 (2024)** [2]:
  - CHC-level mental health units.
  - Urban 3-tier services.
  - Smart supply chain.
  - Digital academy for training.
  - Tele-MANAS integration.
  - HMIS outcome indicators.
  - Accreditation through Centres of Excellence.
- **Tele-MANAS** has handled more than 30 lakh calls (2026) and is linked to DMHP for in-person care.
