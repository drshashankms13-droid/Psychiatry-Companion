## Exam answer

**Introduction.**
- **Definition (Chadwick):** an epileptic seizure is an intermittent, **stereotyped** disturbance of consciousness, behaviour, emotion, motor function or sensation, believed on clinical grounds to result from cortical neuronal discharge [1].
- **Misdiagnosis is common:** **26%** of patients already on ASMs referred to a specialist were misdiagnosed (Smith 1999).
- **The two commonest errors:** **syncope** and **psychogenic (dissociative) attacks**.
- **Consequences of misdiagnosis:**
  - missed treatment (including **fatal cardiac arrhythmia**);
  - ASM toxicity and teratogenicity;
  - emergency "status" interventions;
  - driving and job restrictions;
  - cost (an estimated £125 million a year to the NHS).
- **A "trial of treatment" is seldom justified.**

### Differential diagnosis of seizures [1]

| Group | Conditions |
| --- | --- |
| **Syncope** (Table 6.12) | **Reflex**: vasovagal; situational (micturition, defecation, Valsalva, eating); carotid sinus hypersensitivity. **Orthostatic**: autonomic failure (MSA, diabetic or amyloid neuropathy), drugs (antihypertensives, diuretics, alcohol), volume depletion. **Cardiac**: tachy- or bradyarrhythmias (VT, SVT, **long QT**, AV block, sinus node disease); structural (valve disease, HOCM, myxoma, PE). **Other**: subclavian steal; intermittent obstructive hydrocephalus (third-ventricle lesion) |
| **Psychiatric** | **Dissociative (psychogenic non-epileptic) seizures**; **panic attacks** (vs ictal fear); hyperventilation; psychosis or derealisation (vs aura); factitious disorder or malingering; attention problems (vs juvenile absence epilepsy) |
| **Paroxysmal neurological** (Table 6.14) | **TIA** (negative symptoms; longer; consciousness preserved except vertebrobasilar); **migraine** (slow-evolving aura over tens of minutes; acephalgic migraine); **vertigo** (BPPV, Ménière's, cerebellar stroke); **hyperekplexia** (startle disease; GLRA1); **paroxysmal kinesigenic dyskinesia** (brief, movement-triggered, consciousness preserved; responds to low-dose ASMs) and non-kinesigenic forms; periodic paralysis; periodic ataxia |
| **Sleep disorders** | **Narcolepsy** and **cataplexy** (emotion-triggered atonia, consciousness preserved, recovery within a minute); **NREM parasomnias** (stage 3–4 sleep, 1.5–2 hours after sleep onset, less clustering); **REM sleep behaviour disorder** (dream enactment; older; rousable); *versus* nocturnal **frontal lobe epilepsy** (stage 2, within 30 minutes of sleep onset, bizarre and clustered) |
| **Transient global amnesia** | Dense anterograde amnesia for about 4 hours, otherwise intact; usually single. Vs **transient epileptic amnesia** (prominent retrograde amnesia; partial recall; recurrent) |
| **Endocrine and metabolic** | **Hypoglycaemia** (insulin, alcohol, insulinoma); hypocalcaemia; phaeochromocytoma; carcinoid |
| **Provoked (acute symptomatic) seizures** | About 40% of incident seizures. Fever (children); head injury, stroke, **alcohol withdrawal**, CNS infection; metabolic (Na⁺, Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺, glucose), renal or hepatic failure, hypertensive encephalopathy (PRES); **drugs** (psychotropics 35% of drug-related seizures, isoniazid, bronchodilators, stimulants, insulin); clozapine (4% at 600–900 mg); overdose of antidepressants or antipsychotics |
| **Other ictal mimics within epilepsy** | Non-convulsive status presenting as delirium or psychiatric disorder; **reflex epilepsy** (photic, reading, musicogenic, startle) misread as functional |

### Seizure vs syncope (p3-1ec0f698) [1]

| Feature | **Vasovagal syncope** | **Epileptic (tonic–clonic) seizure** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Posture and trigger | **Standing**; **emotional or pain triggers** (injection, blood, distressing sight); crowded, hot places; micturition or cough | Any posture, including **sleep**; triggers less obvious (sleep loss, alcohol, flicker in some) |
| Prodrome | **Presyncope**: light-headedness, detachment, blurred vision, sounds receding, tinnitus, **cold sweat**, nausea (may "rise" from the epigastrium) | **Aura** (if focal): stereotyped, brief, e.g., epigastric, fear, déjà vu; or none |
| Onset and fall | **Flaccid crumple** | **Tonic "rigid" crash** |
| Colour | **Marked pallor** ("ashen") | Not stated in the source |
| Movements | **Brief myoclonic jerks in up to 90%** (provoked) and reported by about 15% of witnesses: **irregular, multifocal, asynchronous, asymmetrical** | **Tonic phase, then fine, symmetrical, synchronous clonic jerks**, increasing in amplitude and slowing before stopping |
| Duration of unconsciousness | **Seconds** | Longer: a tonic phase, then a clonic phase (convulsive movements lasting more than 1–2 minutes suggest dissociative seizures) |
| Recovery | **Rapid; no confusion** | **Slow, with several minutes of confusion**; drowsiness, headache |
| Incontinence / injury | Possible, less common (non-specific) | Common; **lateral tongue bite**; **posterior shoulder dislocation and vertebral fractures** strongly suggest epilepsy |
| Prolactin (10–20 minutes) | May rise (non-specific) | **Twofold rise** after GTCS (less reliable after CPS; absent after SPS or status) |

**Cardiac syncope pointers ("red flags"):**
- **no warning**, or palpitations or chest pain;
- syncope **on exertion** or **lying down**;
- **personal or family history of heart disease or early cardiac death**.
- **Orthostatic syncope:** on standing, often without prodrome (drugs, autonomic failure).
- **Carotid sinus:** neck pressure; elderly fallers.

**Overlap:**
- **Convulsive syncope:** myoclonic jerks during syncope are commonly mistaken for epilepsy.
- **Ictal bradycardia/asystole** (under 1% of partial seizures; under-recognised) can cause **syncope during a seizure** and may contribute to **SUDEP**.

### Clinical approach to "is this epileptic?" [1]
1. **Eyewitness account is the cornerstone.** For each attack type ask:
   - before, during and after;
   - what happens, how long, and is it always the same?
2. **Before:**
   - prodrome (hours) vs aura (under 1–2 minutes) vs presyncope vs migraine aura (slow);
   - triggers and posture.
3. **During:**
   - awareness (check with the informant);
   - **response to interaction** (attack changes or stops: dissociative);
   - subtle focal features (oral automatisms, dystonic posturing, fumbling);
   - **type of fall** (tonic crash vs flaccid crumple with pallor vs "swoon");
   - movement character (synchronous vs thrashing);
   - eyes open or closed.
4. **After:** speed of recovery (slow and confused: epilepsy; rapid: syncope or dissociative); postictal dysphasia; injury pattern; tongue examination.
5. **Ask specifically about absences and myoclonic jerks**, which are often unreported.
6. **Timing:** nocturnal-only attacks (parasomnia, nocturnal FLE); early-morning attacks (JME).
7. **Aetiology clues by age:**
   - perinatal or congenital (infancy);
   - febrile convulsions, infection, idiopathic generalised epilepsy (childhood/adolescence);
   - trauma, tumour, vascular, degenerative (with age).

### Investigations [1]
- **Interictal EEG:**
  - **supportive only**. Epileptiform changes occur in **0.3–0.5% of healthy adults** (10–30% with brain injury);
  - **sensitivity about 50%** on the first record, about 80% with sleep;
  - **over-reading of non-specific changes** is a major source of misdiagnosis;
  - an entirely normal EEG in someone with daily attacks suggests non-epileptic attacks.
- **Video-EEG telemetry:**
  - **gold standard**;
  - epileptic features: ictal discharge, postictal slowing, or **preserved alpha rhythm in an unresponsive patient** (non-epileptic);
  - frontal or simple partial seizures may show no scalp change.
- **Provocation by suggestion:**
  - induces dissociative attacks in 50–90%;
  - placebo methods are ethically problematic;
  - disclosed suggestion with photic stimulation or hyperventilation works in about two-thirds.
- **MRI:** for all except definite idiopathic generalised epilepsy or benign rolandic epilepsy.
- **Cardiac work-up** (if not clearly vasovagal, frequent, or red flags):
  - **ECG, echocardiography, 24-hour ECG**;
  - **tilt-table testing**: sensitivity about 50%, false positives up to 15%;
  - carotid sinus massage (elderly; one-third false positives);
  - **implantable loop recorder** (diagnosis in about one-third; up to 18 months).
- **Serum prolactin:**
  - sample 10–20 minutes post-event against a baseline;
  - a **twofold rise** supports GTCS or CPS;
  - also rises in syncope and modestly in dissociative attacks;
  - **a negative result after an apparent GTCS is still useful**.
- **Bloods** (glucose, electrolytes, calcium, magnesium), toxicology for provoked seizures.

**Conclusion.** The differential diagnosis of seizures is broad. **Syncope** and **dissociative seizures** account for most errors. The diagnosis is **clinical**, built on a detailed eyewitness history of the sequence of events, supported (not made) by EEG.

**Syncope** is distinguished by:
- posture and emotional triggers;
- presyncope and pallor;
- a flaccid fall;
- brief asynchronous jerks;
- **rapid recovery without confusion**.

Cardiac syncope (no warning, exertional, cardiac history) must never be missed. Video-EEG telemetry, cardiac monitoring and prolactin help in doubtful cases.

## In depth

### Ictal fear vs panic disorder [1]
**Ictal fear:**
- arises "of itself" (no situational trigger);
- has a **unique, intense quality**;
- lacks catastrophic cognitions (fear of dying or suffocating);
- is brief and stereotyped, with other ictal features.
