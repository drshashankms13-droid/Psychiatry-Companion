## Exam answer

**Definition.** Bad news is **any information that changes a person's view of the future in a negative way** (Buckman). The clinician **cannot judge** what constitutes bad news for a particular person [1].

**Why it matters** [1]:
- it is a core, frequently used communication skill, not only in end-of-life care;
- it requires two simultaneous tasks:
  - finding kind words and understandable terms;
  - assessing the patient's and family's reactions and distress, and tailoring information accordingly;
- the conversation must then move patient and family **from the news to future plans, with realism and hope**;
- **a protocol helps** ensure quality and empathy, especially in busy, sub-optimal clinical settings.

**Cancer-specific finding:** patients with cancer report that the **most important component is the content itself**: the clinician's expertise and the specific detail provided. The clinician must therefore be **fully prepared for the informational needs** of patient and family (Parker et al. 2001) [1].

### The SPIKES protocol (Baile, Buckman et al.; MD Anderson) [1]

| Step | What to do |
| --- | --- |
| **S – Setting up** | **Prepare** what to say and the vocabulary to use; know the facts, their implications and the care plan. Agree a **unified, consistent message** across the team. Choose a **private, quiet space**; ask for phones to be silenced. **Sit**, keep an open posture, maintain **eye contact**. Hide signs of nervousness (feet flat, hands palms-down on the lap) |
| **P – Perception** | Ask what patient and family **already know**, what the results mean to them, and **what they want to know**. Look for **denial** (wishful thinking, omitting unfavourable details, unrealistic expectations). Denial may be an important coping strategy; **do not shatter it**, but ensure enough understanding for **informed consent** |
| **I – Invitation** | Ask **permission** to share the news; the patient or family grants it. Respect the level of information desired |
| **K – Knowledge** | **"Fire a warning shot"**: "Unfortunately I have some bad news…", "I'm sorry to tell you…", "Things are not going in the direction we had hoped". This lets them brace emotionally and marks the clinician as an advocate. Give information **slowly**, in **non-technical language** and in understandable amounts, matched to understanding and wishes. Use concrete aids (lab trends, scans). **Choose words carefully** when prognosis is poor |
| **E – Emotions with Empathy** | Expect anything from silence to sobbing. Recognise the emotion and **respond with empathy and respect**; this reduces the clinician's own awkwardness |
| **S – Strategy and Summary** | Ask whether they are **ready to discuss a plan**. Check understanding frequently. End with a **clear plan**: next steps, who does what, which tests are arranged, and **how results will be communicated** |

**The seven goals** behind SPIKES [1]:
1. an appropriate setting;
2. the patient's perception;
3. how much information is known and wanted;
4. knowing the medical facts beforehand;
5. exploring emotions;
6. responding with empathy;
7. a strategy for support.

### Practical points [1]
- **Use the relationship:** a prior relationship makes perception easy to assess and allows planning for emotion, family and follow-up. With a new patient, perception is harder to judge.
- **Be flexible:** not every conversation can follow the protocol exactly, but its considerations help (Eggly et al. 2006).
- **Preparatory information** reduces distress at any illness stage.
- **Train for it:** this is best **taught experientially**, as a psychomotor skill: didactic teaching, then simulated practice, then supervised clinical use. Communication-skills training programmes for oncologists and nurses have been tested in trials (cited: Fallowfield 2002; Wilkinson 2003).

**Conclusion.** Breaking the news of cancer is a structured, empathic process:
- prepare and set up;
- assess perception;
- seek an invitation;
- give knowledge after a warning shot, in plain, careful language;
- respond to emotion with empathy;
- close with a clear strategy and summary.

In cancer, patients value **expert, detailed content** most, so preparation is essential.

## In depth

### Denial and informed consent: the central tension [1]
Denial protects patients facing overwhelming loss, but they must know enough to consent to further tests and treatment. The clinician balances respect for the information desired against the need for adequate understanding.

### Role of the clinician's non-verbal behaviour [1]
- **Signs of nervousness** undermine rapport.
- **A neutral posture and eye contact** convey attentiveness.
