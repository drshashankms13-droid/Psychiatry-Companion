## Exam answer

**Introduction.** Huntington's disease (HD; Huntington, 1872) is an **autosomal dominant** neurodegenerative disorder with a triad of:
- **chorea** (motor);
- **subcortical dementia** (cognitive);
- **psychiatric disturbance** [1, 2].

**Prevalence:** 4–8 per 100,000 in Europe [1]; 4.88 per 100,000 worldwide [2]. There are geographic foci (Tasmania; Moray Firth: 560 per 100,000).

### Aetiopathogenesis [1]
- **Genetics:**
  - **CAG (polyglutamine) repeat expansion** in exon 1 of ***HTT*** (huntingtin);
  - **≤36 repeats normal; ≥40 causes HD**;
  - longer repeats mean earlier onset and greater severity.
- **Anticipation:**
  - expansion during meiosis, **especially in male gametes**;
  - so **juvenile onset is usually paternally transmitted**.
- **Mechanism:**
  - **toxic gain of function**: nuclear aggregates and possibly impaired autophagy;
  - excitotoxicity (NMDA neurones lost early);
  - mitochondrial dysfunction (the 3-NP model).
- **Pathology:**
  - **caudate atrophy** (flattened heads, losing their bulge into the frontal horns), then putamen;
  - frontal cortical loss; small-neurone loss; gliosis; intranuclear inclusions.
- **Neurochemistry:**
  - **↓ GABA and GAD**, ↓ ACh in the striatum;
  - **dopamine spared or relatively excessive**, producing chorea; mesolimbic excess may underlie psychosis.

### Clinical features [1]
- **Onset:** mean mid-40s (childhood to old age). Earlier onset means a more severe course and more prominent emotional prodrome.
- **Presentation:** **neurological and psychiatric presentations are about equally common** (Heathfield).
- **Psychiatric changes often precede chorea or dementia by years.**

**Motor:**
- **chorea**: random, brief, unpredictable jerks; starts in the face, hands or shoulders; often "fidgety" or disguised as purposive acts; ceases in sleep;
- **dance-like gait**;
- dysarthria, dysphagia, **slow saccades**;
- disordered rhythm and speed of fine voluntary movements, with conspicuous slowness (Folstein).
- **Westphal (rigid) variant** (up to 10%; young onset): akinesia, rigidity, tremor, more seizures.
- Maudsley: a **biphasic** course, hyperkinetic (chorea) then **hypokinetic** (bradykinesia, dystonia, gait) [2].

### Psychiatric and neuropsychiatric manifestations (p3-0e868548) [1, 2]

| Domain | Features |
| --- | --- |
| **Personality change** (often the earliest) | Morose, quarrelsome, **irritable**, explosive or **apathetic**, neglectful. Behaviour or personality change in **42%** (Oxford): **aggression to the spouse** (especially men), suspiciousness, temper outbursts; quarrelsomeness in women. **Sexual disturbance**, promiscuity, offending, alcoholism |
| **Depression** | Major affective disorder **41%** (32% depressive, 9% bipolar; Folstein). **It preceded HD by 2–20 years in two-thirds.** It clusters in certain families (genetic heterogeneity?). It responds to drugs or ECT. Maudsley: **30–70%** prevalence |
| **Suicide** | Stressed by Huntington himself. **7% of deaths** among non-hospitalised patients. Highest **early, while insight is retained**. Also high in unaffected relatives |
| **Anxiety** | Early. Lifetime 16.7–24% [2] |
| **Irritability / agitation** | 38–73% [2] |
| **Obsessive-compulsive features / perseveration** | Perseveration and OCB [2] |
| **Apathy** | Early and characteristic; worsens with progression; drives everyday inefficiency and, unlike depression, cognitive deficits |
| **Psychosis** | **Schizophrenia-like or paranoid**: "delusional–hallucinatory states" emerging from a **delusional mood** (McHugh & Folstein); persecutory delusions, religiosity, grandiosity, ideas of reference. **May precede chorea by years** and is the commonest misdiagnosis. Responds to neuroleptics. Less prevalent than mood disorder [2] |
| **Dementia (subcortical)** | Insidious; **inefficiency rather than amnesia**; **psychomotor slowing**; **executive dysfunction** (planning; correlates with caudate atrophy); **retrieval** memory deficit (impaired free recall, better recognition); flat retrograde amnesia; **impaired procedural (skill) learning with preserved priming** (the reverse of AD); severe **verbal fluency** loss; poor visuospatial skills; **no aphasia, apraxia or agnosia**; **insight retained** long; distractibility; terminal akinetic mutism |
| **Terminal** | Apathy or fatuous euphoria, inertia, self-neglect |
| **Pre-manifest carriers** | Anxiety, paranoid ideation, depression; memory deficits before executive and motor ones (>2 years before onset); cingulate activation changes. Some attribute mood change to family stress (Berrios) |

**Juvenile HD (onset before 20):**
- **rigidity and tremor more than chorea**;
- rapid cognitive decline; **seizures in over 50%**;
- school failure; personality change or conduct problems;
- shorter survival;
- often misdiagnosed (learning disability, Wilson's, Friedreich's ataxia, psychopathy).

**Family and psychosocial burden:**
- In Dewhurst's series of 102 patients:
  - attempted suicide 10; self-mutilation 13;
  - alcoholism 19;
  - serious convictions 18;
  - **divorce 38%**;
  - child neglect.
- **Unaffected siblings** also suffer: 93 of 150 died young or developed psychosis, psychopathy, alcoholism or criminality.
- One-third of first-degree relatives are depressed, mainly reactively.

### Misdiagnosis [1]
**Over one-third are initially misdiagnosed.**
- **Psychiatric** (commonest): schizophrenia or paranoid psychosis (chorea blamed on mannerisms or **tardive dyskinesia**), affective psychosis, anxiety, personality disorder.
- **Neurological:** MS, Wilson's, PD, neurosyphilis, cerebellar disease.
- **In the elderly:** senile or vascular chorea.
- **Other choreas:**
  - benign hereditary chorea (no dementia);
  - Sydenham's chorea;
  - **neuroacanthocytosis** (atypical features, seizures, areflexia: check a fresh blood film).

### Investigations [1]
- **Genetic test** (CAG repeat).
- **CT/MRI:** caudate atrophy; dilated frontal horns.
- **PET/SPECT:** **caudate hypometabolism** (even pre-symptomatic).
- **EEG:** loss of alpha; low-voltage, eventually flat.

**Predictive testing** (international protocol):
- counselling by **two professionals**;
- sessions **at least 3 months apart**;
- results given **in person**;
- only after the parent's diagnosis is confirmed;
- **within clinical genetics**.

Wellbeing generally improves after testing, but some who receive **"good news" do worst** (survivor adjustment) [1].

### Management (p3-980df8ac) [2]

**General principles (Box 10.3):**
- treat when symptoms become bothersome or stigmatising;
- **check existing drugs first**;
- prioritise the most troublesome symptom;
- **start low**: patients are sensitive to motor and cognitive adverse effects;
- review regularly.
- **No disease-modifying treatment exists.**

**Motor (Table 10.9):**
- **Chorea:**
  - aim to reduce, not abolish;
  - **tetrabenazine** (licensed; best evidence) is first line **if there is no depression or suicidality** (it causes sedation, depression and parkinsonism);
  - deutetrabenazine or valbenazine where available;
  - **SGAs** (aripiprazole, risperidone, olanzapine) are preferred **when depression, aggression or psychosis coexist**, or adherence is poor;
  - prefer monotherapy;
  - watch QTc.
- **Hypokinetic rigidity:** reduce antipsychotic or tetrabenazine; levodopa (may worsen behaviour); amantadine.
- **Myoclonus:** valproate or clonazepam; levetiracetam.
- **Dystonia:** low-dose tetrabenazine; botulinum toxin (focal); clonazepam or baclofen.

**Psychiatric (Tables 10.10–10.11):** psychiatric symptoms are **the most amenable to drugs**. Choose drugs as in other conditions, but with greater adverse-effect sensitivity.

| Symptom | First line | Alternatives / notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Depression / suicidality** | **SSRIs** (fluoxetine and citalopram trials); mirtazapine; bupropion; venlafaxine | **Avoid TCAs** (anticholinergic: worsen chorea and cognition; overdose toxicity). Lithium for suicidality (limited). Avoid MAOIs with VMAT2 inhibitors. **ECT** if life-threatening. **CYP2D6 inhibitors (fluoxetine, paroxetine) raise VMAT2-inhibitor levels** |
| **Anxiety** | SSRIs/SNRIs | Mirtazapine, pregabalin; SGAs; benzodiazepines; buspirone; propranolol |
| **Irritability / aggression** | **Non-drug first** (triggers: pain, akathisia; behavioural approaches). Then **SSRIs**, then SGAs (olanzapine, risperidone, quetiapine; preferred with chorea or acute aggression) | Benzodiazepines (adjunct); mirtazapine or mianserin (with insomnia); mood stabilisers (valproate, lamotrigine, carbamazepine); propranolol; buspirone; ECT in refractory cases |
| **OCB / perseveration** | SSRIs | Clomipramine; olanzapine or risperidone for ideational perseveration with irritability |
| **Apathy** | None established | Reduce sedatives. Bupropion RCT negative; stimulants and others little success; cariprazine (small open study) |
| **Psychosis** | **Olanzapine, risperidone** (low dose) | Quetiapine, aripiprazole, amisulpride, haloperidol (if chorea troublesome), sulpiride, tiapride, LAIs; **clozapine** for refractory or akinetic-rigid forms. Antipsychotics may worsen the movement disorder |
| **Cognition** | **No effective drug**: insufficient evidence for cholinesterase inhibitors | — |

**Non-pharmacological and social care** [1, 2]:
- **genetic counselling** and predictive-testing support;
- family support (high family psychopathology, child neglect);
- suicide-risk monitoring, especially early;
- forensic and sexual-behaviour issues;
- long-term care (survival **15–20 years**).

**Conclusion.** HD is an autosomal dominant CAG-repeat disorder in which:
- **psychiatric symptoms** (depression, irritability, apathy, personality change, paranoid psychosis) often **precede chorea**;
- **subcortical dementia** and high **suicide** risk follow;
- **misdiagnosis** (schizophrenia, TD) is common.

Management is symptomatic and multidisciplinary:
- tetrabenazine or SGAs for chorea (choice guided by depression or psychosis);
- SSRIs for depression, anxiety, irritability and OCB;
- SGAs (clozapine if refractory) for psychosis;
- avoid TCAs;
- genetic counselling and family support throughout.

## In depth

### Background
- **George Huntington (1872)** described the triad of heredity, chorea and "insanity with a tendency to suicide" [1].
- **New England cases** were traced to emigrants from **Bures, Suffolk (1630)**, with 11 generations affected (Vessie 1932). Later work cast doubt on some of the genealogies [1].
- **The Venezuelan Lake Maracaibo kindreds** (Wexler) led to the localisation of the gene to **4p16.3** (Gusella 1983) and its identification (1993) [general knowledge].
- HD is "an archetypal example of best practice" in the use of genetics in clinical practice: predictive testing and counselling [1].

### Epidemiology
- **4–8 per 100,000** in Europe [1]; 4.88 per 100,000 worldwide [2]. Rare in Japan; very high in Tasmania; **Moray Firth, 560 per 100,000** [1].
- **Equal sex incidence** (autosomal dominant; 50% risk to each child) [1].
- **Mean onset in the mid-40s**, from infancy to extreme old age. Siblings' ages at onset correlate, but not closely enough for counselling [1].
- **Duration 15–20 years** after definite onset, longer than most primary dementias [1].
- **Juvenile HD (onset before 20)** accounts for about 5–10% of cases. Onset before 10 years: about 4 times more likely to have an **affected father** [1; general knowledge for the percentage].
- *Note:* the user's resident notes give a frequency of "4–5 per million". This is an error; the figure is per **100,000** [1, 3].

### Classification
**By CAG repeat length** [1; general knowledge for the intermediate bands]:
| CAG repeats | Category | Consequence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ≤26 | Normal | No risk |
| 27–35 | **Intermediate** (mutable normal) | No HD in the carrier; **paternal expansion can cause HD in offspring** |
| 36–39 | **Reduced penetrance** | May develop late-onset, milder HD [3: "late onset, mild"] |
| ≥40 | **Full penetrance** | HD will develop in a normal lifespan [1] |
| >60 | | Usually juvenile onset |
- Lishman gives "≤36 normal; ≥40 causes HD" [1]. The user's notes give "11–34 normal; 35–39 late onset; >42 classical" [3]. **Use the ACMG/EMQN bands above in exams** [general knowledge].

**By phenotype [1, 3]:**
- **Adult choreic** (typical);
- **Westphal (akinetic–rigid) variant** (up to 10%; young onset; seizures 16% vs 3%) [1];
- **juvenile**;
- **late-onset**: chorea predominant, milder cognitive and behavioural change; often mistaken for "senile chorea" [1, 3].

**Clinical staging** [general knowledge]:
- **Shoulson–Fahn** stages I–V, based on Total Functional Capacity (UHDRS);
- **HD Integrated Staging System (HD-ISS, 2022)**: stage 0 (gene-positive), 1 (biomarker change), 2 (clinical signs), 3 (functional decline).

### Mechanism in detail
**Genetics [1]:**
- **CAG codes for glutamine**, so HD is a **polyglutamine** disorder. Others include DRPLA, SCA1/2/3/6/7/17 and SBMA.
- *Note:* the user's notes say "CAG codes for glutamate, leading to excitotoxicity". **This is wrong**: CAG codes for glutamine. Excitotoxicity is a separate downstream mechanism [1, 3].
- **Anticipation:** CAG instability in **spermatogenesis** produces longer repeats and earlier onset in offspring of affected fathers [1].
- **Toxic gain of function:** expressing the expanded region alone causes disease in models [1].
  - Mutant huntingtin forms **nuclear aggregates**, which disrupt transcription.
  - Inhibiting aggregation or **stimulating autophagy** reverses toxicity in models [1].

**Neurochemistry [1]:**
- **↓ GABA and GAD** in the striatum, globus pallidus and substantia nigra. GABA receptors are depleted early.
- **↓ ACh** (ChAT, cholinergic receptors) in the striatum.
- **Dopamine and noradrenaline spared or raised.** Normal nigral dopamine acting on a reduced population of striatal neurones produces **relative dopaminergic overactivity**, which causes **chorea**. Mesolimbic excess may underlie **psychosis** (Spokes; Marsden).
- **Excitotoxicity:** NMDA-receptor-bearing neurones are lost early. **3-nitropropionic acid** (a mitochondrial complex II toxin) produces an HD-like syndrome in animals [1].
- **Mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress** [1].
- **Protein-clearance failure** (ubiquitin–proteasome, autophagy), shared with AD, DLB, CJD and PD [1].

**Circuit model** [general knowledge]:
- Early loss of **indirect-pathway medium spiny neurones** (enkephalin/D2, projecting to GPe) disinhibits the thalamocortical drive. The result is **hyperkinesia (chorea)**.
- Later loss of **direct-pathway** neurones (substance P/D1, projecting to GPi) produces **hypokinesia and rigidity**. This explains the biphasic course [2].

**Pathology [1]:**
- small atrophic brain;
- **caudate reduced to a rim** along the dilated frontal horns, then putamen; **globus pallidus largely spared**;
- frontal cortical loss with gliosis;
- **loss of small (medium spiny) neurones**; white-matter loss; thin corpus callosum;
- **intranuclear huntingtin inclusions**;
- the **nucleus accumbens is relatively spared** [3].

**Why the psychiatric syndromes arise:**
- **Caudate–frontal disconnection** (dorsolateral, orbitofrontal and anterior cingulate circuits) produces executive dysfunction, disinhibition and irritability, and apathy, respectively [1; general knowledge for circuit mapping].
- **Insight is preserved** because the cortex is relatively spared early. This feeds **reactive depression and suicide** [1].
- **Familial clustering of affective disorder** suggests genetic modifiers (Folstein) [1].

### Clinical features in detail
**Motor [1, 3]:**
- **Early chorea:**
  - finger twitching, facial grimaces passing as mannerisms;
  - "fidgetiness", **parakinesia** (incorporating jerks into purposeful acts such as smoothing hair);
  - slight dysarthria.
- **Established chorea:**
  - **"milkmaid grip"** (cannot sustain grip);
  - **motor impersistence** (cannot keep the tongue protruded);
  - **"piano-playing" fingers**;
  - **dancing, prancing gait**: weight on heels, toes dorsiflexed, a foot suspended, wide base, lordosis, zig-zag, backward steps [1, 3].
- **Hemichorea** may occur.
- **Eye movements:** difficulty initiating saccades (head thrust, blinking), impaired pursuit and fixation [1, 3].
- **Bulbar:** jerky breathing, **explosive or staccato speech**, dysphagia (aspiration pneumonia is a common cause of death).
- **Voluntary movement:** slow, dysrhythmic (Folstein) [1].
- Movements largely **cease in sleep** [1].

**Cognition: details [1]:**
- **inefficiency rather than amnesia**; everyday performance more slipshod than testing predicts;
- **planning but not execution** deficits (Watkins);
- **mental rigidity** and difficulty set-shifting;
- **retrieval deficit**: impaired free recall, near-normal recognition;
- **flat retrograde amnesia** without a Korsakoff-type temporal gradient;
- **preserved lexical priming but impaired motor and perceptual skill learning**, the reverse of AD;
- **verbal fluency severely affected early**;
- poor visuospatial performance;
- **no dysphasia, dyslexia, apraxia or agnosia**;
- orientation preserved until late;
- **insight retained long**;
- distractibility;
- terminal **akinetic mutism**.

**Psychosis phenomenology [1]:** a **delusional mood** ("uncanny change in reality laden with meaning") from which delusions and hallucinations distil suddenly and last months (McHugh and Folstein). Ideas of reference are accentuated by the attention the movements attract. Auditory hallucinations outnumber visual [1, 3].

**Pre-manifest carriers [1]:**
- clusters of anxiety, paranoid ideation and psychoticism, or depression;
- memory deficits before executive and motor changes;
- cognitive change more than 2 years before onset;
- **reduced left anterior cingulate activation** on fMRI.
- Berrios argued mood and personality changes reflect **family stress** rather than the gene.

### Assessment
**History:**
- **three-generation pedigree**; paternity; early parental death; concealment within families [1];
- psychiatric history (often years of "schizophrenia", depression or personality disorder) [1];
- forensic, sexual and alcohol history [1];
- **suicide risk** at every visit, highest early when insight is preserved and around diagnosis or testing [1].

**Examination:** chorea; saccades; motor impersistence; tandem gait; dysarthria.

**Scales** [general knowledge]:
- **UHDRS** (motor, cognitive, behavioural, functional; Total Functional Capacity);
- **Problem Behaviours Assessment-short (PBA-s)**;
- **MoCA**, Symbol Digit Modalities Test, Stroop, verbal fluency;
- **HADS / BDI**; C-SSRS;
- Irritability Scale; Apathy Evaluation Scale.

**Investigations [1, 3]:**
- **Diagnostic genetic test** (CAG count) in symptomatic patients, with consent and counselling.
- **MRI/CT:** caudate atrophy with loss of the convex bulge into the frontal horns; increased bicaudate ratio; frontal ventricular dilatation; thalamic and medial temporal reductions.
- **PET:** **caudate and putamen hypometabolism**, before atrophy. It was seen in 31% of at-risk individuals, close to the expected 34% risk (Mazziotta) [1].
- **SPECT:** caudate hypoperfusion.
- **EEG:** loss of alpha, low voltage, eventually flat (but may be normal).
- **Phenocopy work-up if the gene test is negative:**
  - acanthocytes on a fresh blood film; CK;
  - copper studies;
  - thyroid; autoantibodies (SLE, antiphospholipid);
  - genes for **HDL2 (JPH3)**, **SCA17 (TBP)**, **C9orf72**, **DRPLA (ATN1)**, McLeod (XK), PKAN [general knowledge; 3].

**Predictive testing protocol [1]:**
- two professionals;
- sessions at least 3 months apart;
- results in person;
- parent's diagnosis confirmed first;
- within a clinical genetics unit.

**Also consider** [general knowledge]:
- **psychiatric assessment before testing**: suicide risk, current mood, coping, supports;
- a **"partner in the process"**;
- **no testing of minors** for adult-onset disease;
- **prenatal options**: PGD, and **exclusion testing**, which avoids revealing the parent's status.

### Management details
**Multidisciplinary model** [1, 2; general knowledge]:
- neurologist, psychiatrist, genetic counsellor, speech and swallowing therapist, dietitian (high calorie needs), physiotherapist and OT, social worker;
- **HD associations** (patient and family support).

**Drug interactions and cautions [2]:**
- **Tetrabenazine:**
  - **depression and suicidality** (boxed warning);
  - parkinsonism, akathisia, sedation; QTc;
  - **CYP2D6 metabolism**: **fluoxetine and paroxetine raise levels**; genotype before more than 50 mg/day where available [general knowledge for genotyping];
  - **contraindicated with MAOIs**.
- **Antipsychotics** worsen the hypokinetic phase and dysphagia. Use low doses.
- **Avoid TCAs** (anticholinergic effects worsen chorea and cognition; overdose risk) [2].
- *Note:* the user's notes rank chorea drugs "aripiprazole > risperidone > olanzapine > clozapine > haloperidol". **Maudsley ranks tetrabenazine first unless depression or suicidality coexists**, then SGAs [2, 3]. Use Maudsley's order in exams.

**Psychotherapy and psychosocial care** [general knowledge]:
- supportive and **CBT** for depression and anxiety;
- **behavioural strategies for irritability**: predictable routine, avoid confrontation, offer choices, identify triggers such as hunger, pain, akathisia and over-stimulation;
- **carer education and respite**;
- **children in HD families**: young carers, risk disclosure, neglect [1].

**Ethical and legal issues** [general knowledge]:
- **confidentiality vs duty to relatives** (genetic information);
- **insurance and employment discrimination**;
- **advance directives** before capacity is lost (MHCA 2017 allows advance directives);
- **driving**;
- **criminal responsibility** for disinhibited behaviour [1].

**End of life:**
- PEG decisions (dysphagia);
- **aspiration pneumonia, falls, suicide** are the common causes of death [1; general knowledge].

### Critical appraisal
- Old series (Dewhurst, Oliver) **mix disease effects with social disadvantage and assortative mating**. Causal inference about psychopathology in relatives is weak [1].
- **Prevalence of psychiatric syndromes varies** with method. The NPI and PBA give higher figures than clinical diagnosis.
- **Drug evidence for psychiatric symptoms in HD is mostly open-label or case series**; Maudsley extrapolates from other conditions [2].
- The user's resident notes contain **several factual errors** (prevalence units; CAG codes glutamine, not glutamate; repeat bands; drug order). Verify secondary notes against primary texts [3].

### Indian context [general knowledge]
- HD is reported from all regions. Indian series (NIMHANS, AIIMS) describe **high rates of psychiatric presentation**, often after years of psychiatric treatment.
- **Genetic testing** for CAG repeats is available at tertiary centres (NIMHANS, CCMB Hyderabad, AIIMS), but **counselling services are limited**.
- **Tetrabenazine is available**. Deutetrabenazine and valbenazine are not widely available.
- Families face **stigma**, marriage and disclosure dilemmas, and **consanguinity** in some communities, which is relevant for recessive phenocopies.
- **MHCA 2017:** advance directives and nominated representatives are useful in progressive disease.
- **RPwD Act 2016:** chronic neurological conditions and mental illness are listed disabilities for certification.

### Recent advances [general knowledge]
- **Huntingtin-lowering therapies:**
  - antisense oligonucleotides (tominersen: halted 2021; renewed trial in younger, lower-burden patients);
  - **AMT-130 gene therapy** (AAV-delivered microRNA; reported slowing in 2025; verify);
  - small-molecule splicing modulators (PTC518, votoplam).
- **CSF mutant huntingtin and plasma neurofilament light** as biomarkers.
- **HD-ISS staging (2022)** enables trials in the pre-manifest phase.
- **Valbenazine** (FDA 2023) for HD chorea.
- **Somatic CAG instability (MSH3, FAN1 modifiers)** as a therapeutic target.
