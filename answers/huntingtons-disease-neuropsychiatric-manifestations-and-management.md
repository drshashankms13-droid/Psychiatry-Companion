## Exam answer

**Introduction.** Huntington's disease (HD; Huntington, 1872) is an **autosomal dominant** neurodegenerative disorder with a triad of:
- **chorea** (motor);
- **subcortical dementia** (cognitive);
- **psychiatric disturbance** [1, 2].

**Prevalence:** 4–8 per 100,000 in Europe [1]; 4.88 per 100,000 worldwide [2]. There are geographic foci (Tasmania; Moray Firth: 560 per 100,000).

### Aetiopathogenesis [1]
- **Genetics:**
  - **CAG (polyglutamine) repeat expansion** in exon 1 of ***HTT*** (huntingtin);
  - **≤36 repeats normal; ≥40 causes HD**;
  - longer repeats mean earlier onset and greater severity.
- **Anticipation:**
  - expansion during meiosis, **especially in male gametes**;
  - so **juvenile onset is usually paternally transmitted**.
- **Mechanism:**
  - **toxic gain of function**: nuclear aggregates and possibly impaired autophagy;
  - excitotoxicity (NMDA neurones lost early);
  - mitochondrial dysfunction (the 3-NP model).
- **Pathology:**
  - **caudate atrophy** (flattened heads, losing their bulge into the frontal horns), then putamen;
  - frontal cortical loss; small-neurone loss; gliosis; intranuclear inclusions.
- **Neurochemistry:**
  - **↓ GABA and GAD**, ↓ ACh in the striatum;
  - **dopamine spared or relatively excessive**, producing chorea; mesolimbic excess may underlie psychosis.

### Clinical features [1]
- **Onset:** mean mid-40s (childhood to old age). Earlier onset means a more severe course and more prominent emotional prodrome.
- **Presentation:** **neurological and psychiatric presentations are about equally common** (Heathfield).
- **Psychiatric changes often precede chorea or dementia by years.**

**Motor:**
- **chorea**: random, brief, unpredictable jerks; starts in the face, hands or shoulders; often "fidgety" or disguised as purposive acts; ceases in sleep;
- **dance-like gait**;
- dysarthria, dysphagia, **slow saccades**;
- disordered rhythm and speed of fine voluntary movements, with conspicuous slowness (Folstein).
- **Westphal (rigid) variant** (up to 10%; young onset): akinesia, rigidity, tremor, more seizures.
- Maudsley: a **biphasic** course, hyperkinetic (chorea) then **hypokinetic** (bradykinesia, dystonia, gait) [2].

### Psychiatric and neuropsychiatric manifestations (p3-0e868548) [1, 2]

| Domain | Features |
| --- | --- |
| **Personality change** (often the earliest) | Morose, quarrelsome, **irritable**, explosive or **apathetic**, neglectful. Behaviour or personality change in **42%** (Oxford): **aggression to the spouse** (especially men), suspiciousness, temper outbursts; quarrelsomeness in women. **Sexual disturbance**, promiscuity, offending, alcoholism |
| **Depression** | Major affective disorder **41%** (32% depressive, 9% bipolar; Folstein). **It preceded HD by 2–20 years in two-thirds.** It clusters in certain families (genetic heterogeneity?). It responds to drugs or ECT. Maudsley: **30–70%** prevalence |
| **Suicide** | Stressed by Huntington himself. **7% of deaths** among non-hospitalised patients. Highest **early, while insight is retained**. Also high in unaffected relatives |
| **Anxiety** | Early. Lifetime 16.7–24% [2] |
| **Irritability / agitation** | 38–73% [2] |
| **Obsessive-compulsive features / perseveration** | Perseveration and OCB [2] |
| **Apathy** | Early and characteristic; worsens with progression; drives everyday inefficiency and, unlike depression, cognitive deficits |
| **Psychosis** | **Schizophrenia-like or paranoid**: "delusional–hallucinatory states" emerging from a **delusional mood** (McHugh & Folstein); persecutory delusions, religiosity, grandiosity, ideas of reference. **May precede chorea by years** and is the commonest misdiagnosis. Responds to neuroleptics. Less prevalent than mood disorder [2] |
| **Dementia (subcortical)** | Insidious; **inefficiency rather than amnesia**; **psychomotor slowing**; **executive dysfunction** (planning; correlates with caudate atrophy); **retrieval** memory deficit (impaired free recall, better recognition); flat retrograde amnesia; **impaired procedural (skill) learning with preserved priming** (the reverse of AD); severe **verbal fluency** loss; poor visuospatial skills; **no aphasia, apraxia or agnosia**; **insight retained** long; distractibility; terminal akinetic mutism |
| **Terminal** | Apathy or fatuous euphoria, inertia, self-neglect |
| **Pre-manifest carriers** | Anxiety, paranoid ideation, depression; memory deficits before executive and motor ones (>2 years before onset); cingulate activation changes. Some attribute mood change to family stress (Berrios) |

**Juvenile HD (onset before 20):**
- **rigidity and tremor more than chorea**;
- rapid cognitive decline; **seizures in over 50%**;
- school failure; personality change or conduct problems;
- shorter survival;
- often misdiagnosed (learning disability, Wilson's, Friedreich's ataxia, psychopathy).

**Family and psychosocial burden:**
- In Dewhurst's series of 102 patients:
  - attempted suicide 10; self-mutilation 13;
  - alcoholism 19;
  - serious convictions 18;
  - **divorce 38%**;
  - child neglect.
- **Unaffected siblings** also suffer: 93 of 150 died young or developed psychosis, psychopathy, alcoholism or criminality.
- One-third of first-degree relatives are depressed, mainly reactively.

### Misdiagnosis [1]
**Over one-third are initially misdiagnosed.**
- **Psychiatric** (commonest): schizophrenia or paranoid psychosis (chorea blamed on mannerisms or **tardive dyskinesia**), affective psychosis, anxiety, personality disorder.
- **Neurological:** MS, Wilson's, PD, neurosyphilis, cerebellar disease.
- **In the elderly:** senile or vascular chorea.
- **Other choreas:**
  - benign hereditary chorea (no dementia);
  - Sydenham's chorea;
  - **neuroacanthocytosis** (atypical features, seizures, areflexia: check a fresh blood film).

### Investigations [1]
- **Genetic test** (CAG repeat).
- **CT/MRI:** caudate atrophy; dilated frontal horns.
- **PET/SPECT:** **caudate hypometabolism** (even pre-symptomatic).
- **EEG:** loss of alpha; low-voltage, eventually flat.

**Predictive testing** (international protocol):
- counselling by **two professionals**;
- sessions **at least 3 months apart**;
- results given **in person**;
- only after the parent's diagnosis is confirmed;
- **within clinical genetics**.

Wellbeing generally improves after testing, but some who receive **"good news" do worst** (survivor adjustment) [1].

### Management (p3-980df8ac) [2]

**General principles (Box 10.3):**
- treat when symptoms become bothersome or stigmatising;
- **check existing drugs first**;
- prioritise the most troublesome symptom;
- **start low**: patients are sensitive to motor and cognitive adverse effects;
- review regularly.
- **No disease-modifying treatment exists.**

**Motor (Table 10.9):**
- **Chorea:**
  - aim to reduce, not abolish;
  - **tetrabenazine** (licensed; best evidence) is first line **if there is no depression or suicidality** (it causes sedation, depression and parkinsonism);
  - deutetrabenazine or valbenazine where available;
  - **SGAs** (aripiprazole, risperidone, olanzapine) are preferred **when depression, aggression or psychosis coexist**, or adherence is poor;
  - prefer monotherapy;
  - watch QTc.
- **Hypokinetic rigidity:** reduce antipsychotic or tetrabenazine; levodopa (may worsen behaviour); amantadine.
- **Myoclonus:** valproate or clonazepam; levetiracetam.
- **Dystonia:** low-dose tetrabenazine; botulinum toxin (focal); clonazepam or baclofen.

**Psychiatric (Tables 10.10–10.11):** psychiatric symptoms are **the most amenable to drugs**. Choose drugs as in other conditions, but with greater adverse-effect sensitivity.

| Symptom | First line | Alternatives / notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Depression / suicidality** | **SSRIs** (fluoxetine and citalopram trials); mirtazapine; bupropion; venlafaxine | **Avoid TCAs** (anticholinergic: worsen chorea and cognition; overdose toxicity). Lithium for suicidality (limited). Avoid MAOIs with VMAT2 inhibitors. **ECT** if life-threatening. **CYP2D6 inhibitors (fluoxetine, paroxetine) raise VMAT2-inhibitor levels** |
| **Anxiety** | SSRIs/SNRIs | Mirtazapine, pregabalin; SGAs; benzodiazepines; buspirone; propranolol |
| **Irritability / aggression** | **Non-drug first** (triggers: pain, akathisia; behavioural approaches). Then **SSRIs**, then SGAs (olanzapine, risperidone, quetiapine; preferred with chorea or acute aggression) | Benzodiazepines (adjunct); mirtazapine or mianserin (with insomnia); mood stabilisers (valproate, lamotrigine, carbamazepine); propranolol; buspirone; ECT in refractory cases |
| **OCB / perseveration** | SSRIs | Clomipramine; olanzapine or risperidone for ideational perseveration with irritability |
| **Apathy** | None established | Reduce sedatives. Bupropion RCT negative; stimulants and others little success; cariprazine (small open study) |
| **Psychosis** | **Olanzapine, risperidone** (low dose) | Quetiapine, aripiprazole, amisulpride, haloperidol (if chorea troublesome), sulpiride, tiapride, LAIs; **clozapine** for refractory or akinetic-rigid forms. Antipsychotics may worsen the movement disorder |
| **Cognition** | **No effective drug**: insufficient evidence for cholinesterase inhibitors | — |

**Non-pharmacological and social care** [1, 2]:
- **genetic counselling** and predictive-testing support;
- family support (high family psychopathology, child neglect);
- suicide-risk monitoring, especially early;
- forensic and sexual-behaviour issues;
- long-term care (survival **15–20 years**).

### Update from Continuum 2022 [3]
**Genetics:**
- HD is the **commonest cause of adult-onset hereditary chorea**, affecting about **5–12 per 100,000**.
- **Autosomal dominant CAG expansion** in ***HTT*** on **chromosome 4p16.3**. Mutant huntingtin forms nuclear aggregates, with loss of striatal **medium spiny neurons**.
- **About 5%** are **de novo** or arise from expansion of a mutable intermediate allele, so a negative family history does not exclude HD.

**CAG repeat categories (Table 8-1):**

| CAG repeats | Classification | Disease status | Risk to offspring |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 26 or under | Normal | Not affected | None |
| 27–35 | **Intermediate** | Not affected (rare symptomatic reports) | **Increased** (paternal **anticipation**) |
| 36–39 | **Reduced penetrance** | May or may not be affected | 50% |
| 40 or more | **Full penetrance** | Will be affected | 50% |

- Repeat length explains only **50–70%** of the variation in **age at motor onset** (inverse correlation).
- **DNA-repair modifier genes** (GeM-HD) act through somatic expansion. The **uninterrupted** CAG length matters more than polyglutamine length.

**Course and phenotype:**
- **Mean onset** in the **third or fourth decade**; survival **15–20 years** after onset.
- **Prodromal HD:** subtle signs for about **10 years** before clinical diagnosis.
- **Diagnostic criteria:** historically motor-based. **Revised criteria** (Reilmann; MDS task force) add cognitive features.
- **Juvenile (Westphal) HD:** onset **before 20**, CAG usually **over 55**, with akinesia, parkinsonism and **seizures** rather than chorea.
- **Motor clues:** **forehead or upper-face chorea** (less common in tardive syndromes); **delayed saccade initiation and slowed saccades**; also ataxia, dystonia, parkinsonism, tics, dysarthria, dysphagia.
- **Cognition:** decline in **all**, mainly executive (planning, working memory, attention).
- **Psychiatric:**
  - depression, **irritability**, anxiety and impulsivity are commonest, contributing to the **high suicide rate**;
  - OCD symptoms; **apathy, which uniquely worsens over time**;
  - psychosis is rare;
  - **anosognosia** for chorea is common.
- **Assessment:** **UHDRS** total maximal chorea score (seven body regions); AIMS.

**Genetic testing:** **genetic counselling must come before testing.**

**HD phenocopies** (1–7% of HTT-negative genetic choreas; most stay undiagnosed):
- **C9orf72 expansion** is the commonest phenocopy to test next (1.95%; may include ALS or FTD);
- **HDL2** (Huntington disease–like 2); **DRPLA** (*ATN1*; dentate, red nucleus and pallidal MRI change);
- **SCA17**;
- **chorea-acanthocytosis** (*VPS13A*: orobuccolingual and feeding dystonia, acanthocytes) and **McLeod syndrome** (X-linked);
- brain mineral disorders: **NBIA** (iron), **Fahr** (calcium), **Wilson's** (copper; "face of the giant panda" on MRI; treatable).

**Treatment of chorea:**
- **Treat only troublesome chorea.** Judge its impact with the care partner, because of anosognosia.
- **VMAT2 inhibitors** are the **only FDA-approved class** for HD chorea:
  - **tetrabenazine:** TETRA-HD, Class I. Adverse effects: **depression and suicidal ideation**, sedation, parkinsonism;
  - **deutetrabenazine:** FIRST-HD. A **deuterated** form with a longer half-life, less frequent dosing, steadier levels and probably fewer mood and sedation effects, but still monitor mood, suicidality and parkinsonism;
  - **valbenazine** (once daily) is approved for TD and was under study for HD.
- **Antipsychotics (off-label):**
  - stronger D2 blockers (typicals, **risperidone, olanzapine**) suppress chorea better than quetiapine or clozapine;
  - also useful for behaviour, weight and sleep;
  - watch for metabolic effects, sedation, parkinsonism and TD.
- **Amantadine** has limited evidence in HD. **Antiseizure drugs** are used in juvenile HD with seizures. **Botulinum toxin** helps focal dystonia.

**Drugs that can induce chorea (Table 8-2):**
- **SSRIs** (fluoxetine, fluvoxamine, paroxetine) and **TCAs**;
- antiseizure drugs (carbamazepine, lamotrigine, phenytoin, valproate);
- antihistamines;
- levodopa, dopamine agonists, anticholinergics;
- calcium-channel blockers; **dopamine antagonists**; psychostimulants; **lithium**; methadone; oral contraceptives; steroids.

⚠️ **Discrepancy (not resolved):**
- Lishman (above) gives "**≤36 normal**". Continuum gives **26 or under** as normal, with 27–35 intermediate and 36–39 reduced penetrance.
- Continuum's table prints "<26" while its text says "26 or lower".
- **Onset:** Lishman gives a mean in the **mid-40s**; Continuum gives the **third or fourth decade**.

**Conclusion.** HD is an autosomal dominant CAG-repeat disorder in which:
- **psychiatric symptoms** (depression, irritability, apathy, personality change, paranoid psychosis) often **precede chorea**;
- **subcortical dementia** and high **suicide** risk follow;
- **misdiagnosis** (schizophrenia, TD) is common.

Management is symptomatic and multidisciplinary:
- tetrabenazine or SGAs for chorea (choice guided by depression or psychosis);
- SSRIs for depression, anxiety, irritability and OCB;
- SGAs (clozapine if refractory) for psychosis;
- avoid TCAs;
- genetic counselling and family support throughout.

## In depth

### Why HD is a model "subcortical dementia" [1]
- Caudate-frontal disconnection explains apathy, slowing, executive and retrieval deficits.
- Language and praxis are spared.
- Insight is preserved, which explains early depression and suicide.

- Investigational disease-modifying therapies (Continuum names none for HD).
