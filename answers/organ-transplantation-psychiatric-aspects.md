## Exam answer

**Introduction [1].**
- **History:**
  - first successful transplant in **1954** (Joseph Murray, a kidney from an identical twin);
  - chemical immunosuppression in the 1960s;
  - **cyclosporine** approved in 1983, which made transplantation standard care;
  - the US **NOTA (1984)** prohibited buying or selling organs.
- **The core problem is organ shortage:**
  - more than 40% of wait-listed candidates wait 2 years or more, and **10–18% die on the list**;
  - teams therefore select candidates with the best chance of a good outcome.
- **Why psychiatry is involved:** the process makes heavy demands on patients and caregivers (severe illness, waiting, surgery, recovery, lifelong regimen). Psychiatrists are involved in **evaluation, selection, treatment and long-term care**.

### Role of the psychiatrist across the transplant timeline [1]
Transplantation is a continuum. Each phase brings different stressors: advanced organ disease, then evaluation, waiting, surgery and perioperative care, early recovery, and long-term life as a recipient.

**1. Pretransplant psychosocial evaluation.** Goals:
1. Develop an individualised treatment plan, including listing requirements.
2. Establish a working relationship.
3. Assess coping strengths and vulnerabilities.
4. Diagnose and treat active psychiatric disorders.
5. Assess past **adherence** and the ability to change behaviour.
6. Evaluate the amount, stability and availability of **social support**.
7. Ensure **informed consent**.
8. Educate and prepare the patient.
9. Give input to candidacy decisions.

**Content of the evaluation:**
- a full psychiatric assessment;
- transplant-specific areas:
  - illness course and understanding;
  - attitude to and knowledge of transplant;
  - treatment adherence;
  - coping with illness (acceptance vs denial, active vs passive);
  - substance use in detail (insight, abstinence, rehabilitation, 12-step involvement);
  - family and social history;
  - the support system;
  - MSE including cognition and executive function.
- **Process:**
  - usually a two-stage screen: a social worker first, then psychiatry for flagged cases;
  - structured tools: **PACT, TERS, SIPAT, SIRT**;
  - collateral from family, records, pharmacy and **toxicology**.

**Special areas:**
- **Social support:** caregivers should attend. The best evidence of support is consistent behaviour, not a self-presentation.
- **Adherence:**
  - non-adherence to immunosuppressants drives **late acute rejection and graft loss**;
  - rates: **28%** self-reported non-adherence in kidney recipients; **23 cases per 100 recipients per year** across organs (Dew meta-analysis);
  - best pretransplant predictors: **past non-adherence, substance use and poor support**.
- **Substance use:**
  - ask all candidates, not only those with alcohol-related disease;
  - **tobacco cessation** is required for heart and lung listing and increasingly for abdominal organs;
  - marijuana policies vary.
- **Informed consent:** desperation and cognitive impairment (encephalopathy) limit understanding. Identify gaps and supply information.
- **Emergency evaluations** (fulminant liver failure, acute cardiomyopathy):
  - rely on family and records;
  - surrogate understanding is the key consent issue;
  - in **paracetamol overdose**, decide whether it was accidental or a suicide attempt, and assess impulsivity, stressors, character pathology and future risk.
- **Living donors**:
  - assess mental health, **motives** (guilt, urgency, wish to be a "hero", ambivalence), relationship, **coercion or inducement**, knowledge, finances and capacity;
  - an **independent living donor advocate** is mandatory;
  - active psychiatric disorder or SUD is usually a contraindication, with exceptions (for example, a parent donating to a critically ill child).

**Dual role:** the evaluator serves both the team and the patient:
- advocates for the patient;
- interprets team requirements back to the patient;
- educates the team on the prognosis of psychiatric disorders.
- Reports should be **plain and jargon-free**, and comments on suitability made cautiously (insurers read them).
- Most teams **defer rather than reject**: they allow the problem to be addressed, then re-evaluate.
- Teams vary: **heart** programmes are the strictest about psychosocial contraindications; **kidney** programmes are the most tolerant.

**2. Preparation and contracting:**
- Refer for psychotherapy **early**, before encephalopathy limits participation.
- **Behavioural/contingency contracts** for SUD, non-adherence or psychiatric disorder:
  - specify expectations, how they will be monitored and the consequences;
  - adherence leads to listing; non-adherence leads to removal from the list.
- **SUD:**
  - stable abstinence is measured in years, but many liver programmes use **6 months** as the benchmark;
  - relapse risks: short abstinence, poor support, family history, polysubstance use, failed rehabilitation;
  - **addiction counselling** is a prerequisite if abstinence is under 1 year without prior therapy; use motivational interviewing; random toxicology.
- **Methadone maintenance:** forcing a taper for listing may precipitate relapse and **could be unethical**.
- **Reassess wait-listed candidates** periodically (mood, coping, cognition, adherence, substance use, support).

**3. Waiting period:**
- Often perceived as **more stressful than evaluation**.
- Patients grieve role loss, independence and finances, with uncertainty about deterioration and loss of eligibility.
- Counselling prepares patients for either **transplantation or death**.

**4. Perioperative period:**
- Pain control, education and reassurance.
- Watch for **delirium**, which is the commonest cognitive disorder and affects most patients at some point.
- Typical stay: kidney 3–4 days, liver and heart 7–10 days, lung about 2 weeks.

**5. Early and long-term adaptation:**
- Complex regimens and frequent clinic visits.
- **Maladaptive illness behaviour** takes two forms:
  1. the chronically ill patient attached to the **sick role**, with difficulty becoming well, somatic complaints, non-adherence and fear of fewer visits;
  2. the **emergency recipient**, with denial, overexertion, resentment of restrictions and non-adherence.
- **Long term:** immunosuppressant complications (renal failure in 10–20% of non-renal recipients within 5 years, diabetes, cancer). Quality of life improves but stays below that of healthy people.

### Psychiatric disorders in transplantation [1]
- **Mood disorders:** MDD, dysthymia, adjustment disorder.
  - Rates: **kidney up to 20%, liver 30%, heart 63%** in the first years.
  - Risk is **highest in the first year** after transplant.
  - Depression before or after transplant **raises mortality risk by 65%** (Dew 2015 meta-analysis) and graft loss. Screen early after transplant.
- **Anxiety:**
  - affects up to one-third of recipients; panic is commoner in lung disease;
  - **PTSD** follows critical events and delirium experiences. After heart transplant, first-year PTSD predicted mortality.
- **Psychosis/mania:**
  - rare, and after recovery almost confined to those with a prior history;
  - about **37%** with a prior history relapsed (Coffman);
  - patients do well if stable, supported and adherent;
  - a prior history does **not clearly predispose to steroid psychosis**;
  - do not stop antipsychotics inadvertently.
- **Alcohol and SUD (liver):**
  - after liver transplant, 30–60% drink at all but only **10–25%** drink harmfully;
  - survival equals that of other indications;
  - illicit drug relapse: **3.7 per 100 patient-years**;
  - monitor, never condone, and refer early.
- **Tobacco:**
  - immunosuppression multiplies cancer risk (lung ×4, oropharyngeal ×25 in alcoholic liver disease recipients);
  - resumption: 10 per 100 patient-years after a substance history vs 3 overall.
- **Cognitive disorders:**
  - separate reversible delirium from dementia or static deficits before transplant;
  - deficits may take months to years to resolve.

### Psychopharmacology [1]
- **Before transplant:** altered perfusion, protein binding and metabolism. Start **low** and titrate **slowly**.
- **After transplant:** tacrolimus, cyclosporine, sirolimus and mycophenolate are **CYP3A4 substrates**. Avoid strong 3A4 inhibitors:
  - order of inhibition: **fluvoxamine, nefazodone > fluoxetine > sertraline, TCAs, paroxetine > venlafaxine**;
  - nefazodone has caused toxic immunosuppressant levels.
- **Preferred antidepressants:** **citalopram and escitalopram**, because of few interactions.
- **Lithium:**
  - unstable levels from fluid shifts, and additive nephrotoxicity;
  - **cyclosporine reduces lithium clearance**;
  - monitor closely and stop if renal insufficiency develops.
- **Carbamazepine and oxcarbazepine** induce 3A4, lower immunosuppressant levels and risk **rejection**.
- **Antipsychotics:** for delirium, steroid-induced mood change or psychosis; **ECG monitoring** for QT.
- **Benzodiazepines:** prefer **lorazepam, oxazepam or temazepam**, which need conjugation only. **Buspirone** helps in lung disease and ventilator weaning.
- **Anti-craving drugs:**
  - not studied in transplant;
  - **naltrexone** is unsuitable in liver disease;
  - **disulfiram** needs hepatic activation and its metabolite inhibits 3A4.

### Neuropsychiatric effects of immunosuppressants [1]
- **Calcineurin inhibitors (tacrolimus > cyclosporine):**
  - mild effects in **40–60%**: tremor, headache, insomnia, vivid dreams, paraesthesia, mood change;
  - severe effects in up to **20–30%** early on: delirium, seizures, cortical blindness, catatonia;
  - can occur at therapeutic levels; liver recipients are the most susceptible;
  - associated with high levels, IV use, **low cholesterol and low magnesium**.
- **Corticosteroids:** euphoria, depression, psychosis and delirium. Usually transient and early.
- **PRES:** seizures, headache, visual change, confusion; bilateral parieto-occipital FLAIR lesions. Resolves when the CNI or sirolimus is stopped.
- **PML:** JC virus reactivation.

### Renal transplantation: specific psychiatric issues [1]
- **Indications (US list):** diabetes 34%, hypertensive nephrosclerosis 25%, glomerulonephritis 14%, polycystic disease 8%.
- **Dialysis as the bridge:**
  - most candidates are on dialysis; median wait is **3–5 years** without a living donor;
  - dialysis survival is **80% at 1 year and 35% at 5 years**.
- **Adherence to dialysis** (sessions, diet, fluids) is a **useful predictor** of adherence after transplant:
  - explore missed or shortened sessions;
  - treating **depression improves dialysis adherence**.
- **Uremic encephalopathy:**
  - fluctuating irritability, insomnia, attention and memory deficits, progressing to delirium, psychosis, catatonia and coma;
  - EEG shows generalised slowing; exclude non-convulsive status;
  - thiamine deficiency on haemodialysis can mimic it (**Wernicke's**);
  - associated with PRES;
  - treated with dialysis and correction of electrolytes, anaemia and nutrition.
- **Dialysis dementia** (aluminium) is now rare.
- **Living kidney donation:**
  - paired exchanges and domino chains are used;
  - laparoscopic nephrectomy means a 2–3 day stay and **4–6 weeks** before returning to work;
  - donor quality of life is generally good. At about 17 years, donors' QoL exceeded population norms, but **13–20%** had impairment in some area, and a psychiatric history raised the risk.
- **Programme stance:** kidney programmes are the **most tolerant** of psychosocial issues, given their experience and the cost advantage over dialysis.
- **Rates:**
  - mood disorder in **up to 20%** of kidney recipients;
  - self-reported immunosuppressant non-adherence **28%**;
  - after transplant, quality of life improves clearly over pre-transplant levels.
- **Prescribing in renal insufficiency**:
  - lithium: 50–75% dose, with a 300 mg supplement after each dialysis session;
  - gabapentin: reduce in moderate or severe renal insufficiency;
  - desvenlafaxine: at most 50 mg/day;
  - duloxetine: avoid in severe renal insufficiency;
  - paliperidone and risperidone: lower doses;
  - acamprosate: contraindicated in severe renal insufficiency;
  - varenicline: 0.5 mg/day to start in severe renal insufficiency.

### Psychological treatment [1]
- **Before transplant:** CBT and education RCTs mostly improve QoL and distress.
- **After transplant**, individual therapy addresses three themes:
  1. **fear of rejection**;
  2. **paradoxical loss** despite a successful transplant;
  3. **psychological integration of the organ**.
- **Group therapy:** less negative affect, better QoL, fewer calls to coordinators.
- **Other approaches:**
  - peer mentoring (Toronto);
  - MBSR (Gross);
  - Dew's Internet intervention: less depression and anxiety, better adherence.
- **Adherence interventions:**
  - no single intervention is superior; combine them;
  - pharmacist education, problem-solving and CBT;
  - treat depression and SUD;
  - booster sessions may be needed.

**Conclusion.** The psychiatrist is involved across the whole timeline:
- evaluates candidates and living donors;
- prepares and contracts;
- supports the waiting period;
- manages delirium, depression, PTSD, SUD and immunosuppressant neurotoxicity;
- prescribes with attention to CYP3A4 and organ impairment.

This matters because depression and non-adherence predict graft loss and death.

### Cognition in organ failure [1]
- **Heart failure:** low output, hypoperfusion and inotropes. VAD microemboli lead to poorer cognition after transplant.
- **Lung disease:** high PCO₂ and low PO₂.
  - impairment was 57% at discharge vs 45% before transplant;
  - 80% of those impaired before transplant remained impaired.
- **Liver transplant:** hepatic encephalopathy-type deficits resolved, but new impairment appeared in 70% by 1 year.
- **Minimal hepatic encephalopathy:**
  - affects up to 60% of cirrhotic patients and impairs driving;
  - detected with Trail-Making A/B, Digit Symbol and Block Design;
  - precipitants: GI bleed, sedatives, diuretics, protein load, dehydration;
  - avoid anticholinergics, narcotics and sedatives.

### VAD patients [1]
- Body-image damage, vulnerability, and fear of malfunction or infection.
- Psychotherapy for patient and caregiver.

### Living donor case (K&S) [1]
- A 30-year-old estranged sister with a history of drug misuse wished to donate to her "model son" brother, hoping to restore her family standing.
- **Explore:** her expectations of the post-donation relationship, guilt, and the wish to restore status.

### Contracting case (K&S) [1]
- A 58-year-old man with ischaemic cardiomyopathy tapered his own medicines and hated being a "walking pharmacy".
- Listing required consistent adherence and psychotherapy.
- Therapy uncovered vulnerability, loss of control and anger at providers.
