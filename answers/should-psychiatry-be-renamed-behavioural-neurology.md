## Exam answer

**Introduction.**
- **"Behavioural neurology"** would imply that mental disorders are **brain diseases** best described in neurological terms.
- Lishman draws a related distinction [1]:
  - **organic psychiatry:** disorders where investigation will probably uncover cerebral or systemic pathology causing or contributing to the mental state;
  - **neuropsychiatry:** a narrower discipline at the **interface of neurology and psychiatry**, dealing with disorders due "to brain malfunction of a clearly identifiable nature".
- The question is whether **all** of psychiatry fits this model.

### Arguments FOR renaming
1. **Brain abnormalities exist in "functional" disorders.**
   - **Schizophrenia** shows enlarged ventricles, reduced cortical and hippocampal volume, smaller neurons, first-episode deficits and developmental anomalies [1].
   - Depression and anxiety are strongly linked with migraine, a brain disorder of neuronal hyperexcitability [1].
2. **Subtle brain dysfunction probably underlies even non-organic presentations.** Disorientation in acute schizophrenia, cognitive impairment in late-life depression, and delirium-like features in mania probably reflect "**subtle perturbations of brain function** … not yet identifiable by routine clinical investigation" [1].
3. **The organic/non-organic line is blurred.** "The line of demarcation between organic and non-organic psychiatric disorders is **not hard and fast**." Some commentators call the distinction a "primitive throwback to **Cartesian dualism**" [1].
4. **Psychiatric presentations of brain disease.** Many brain diseases present with **psychological symptoms alone**, long before neurological signs: neurotic symptoms, personality change, even schizophrenia-like pictures [1].
5. **History shows "psychosomatic" labels change with knowledge.** **Peptic ulcer** was considered psychosomatic until *H. pylori* was identified [2].

### Arguments AGAINST renaming
1. **Individual and environmental factors shape psychiatric illness.** In most psychiatric illness the picture is "profoundly coloured and sometimes decisively shaped by factors specific to the individual and his environment". Separate disease processes are hard to identify and **tissue pathology is lacking** [1].
2. **The distinction remains clinically useful.** Neuropsychiatry is "the embodiment of an integrated approach to mind and body" but "not an excuse for **woolly thinking** or for ignoring the very real distinctions between classes of disorder. These distinctions remain **valid and useful in practice** for the great majority of cases" [1].
3. **Brain changes are non-specific and do not translate into treatment.** Even where subtle brain dysfunction exists, "the possibility of their presence has **little practical implication for treatment**" [1].
   - Schizophrenia brain changes are group findings with overlap, and some are **medication-related** (caudate enlargement with antipsychotics) [1].
4. **Psychological symptoms are hard to measure objectively.** They "can rarely be measured accurately". Difficulty "increases abruptly" from motor and sensory processes to emotion, personality and complex behaviour [1].
5. **Psychiatry must stay broad, even within neuropsychiatry.** Neuropsychiatry "must capitalise on all that psychiatry has to offer", including **psychodynamic, social and cultural** aspects. "Neuropsychiatric practice requires a **widening not a narrowing** of psychiatric skills" [1].
6. **"Biological psychiatry" is an approach, not a discipline.** It is "rightly reserved for the approach (rather than the clinical discipline)" concerned with biological pathophysiology across psychiatric disorders [1].
7. **Conversion and functional disorders.** These look neurological but "**psychological and social factors predominate** in the clinical formulation" [1]. A purely neurological framing would misdescribe them.
8. **Psychological factors matter even in neurological disease.**
   - **Migraine:** stress, sustained emotional tension and personality influence attacks. Relaxation, hypnosis, behavioural therapy and treatment of depression are part of management [1].
   - **Chronic GI disorders:** symptoms persist despite mucosal healing, leading to a spectrum model with **central and peripheral** factors [2].
9. **Dualism in practice harms patients.** An "organic is real, functional is psychiatric" split can lead patients to feel dismissed [2]. Relabelling psychiatry as neurology risks the same split in reverse: the psychosocial content gets dropped.
10. **Testing is not objective.** Even psychometry depends on the patient's cooperation and motivation [1].

### Balanced view
- **Neuropsychiatry and behavioural neurology are valid sub-areas** at the interface, where brain lesions explain behaviour (dementia, epilepsy, head injury, movement disorders) [1].
- **Psychiatry as a whole** treats disorders shaped by **individual history, environment, personality and culture**, which a neurological name would leave out [1].
- The right goal is **integration**: an integrated mind–body approach that keeps the organic/non-organic distinction where it is useful [1], and keeps biological psychiatry as one approach among several.

**Conclusion.** Psychiatry should **not** be renamed behavioural neurology. Brain science informs every psychiatric disorder, and neuropsychiatry is a legitimate interface discipline. But most psychiatric illness is decisively shaped by individual and environmental factors, has no identifiable tissue pathology, and needs psychological, social and cultural skills. As Lishman's text puts it, practice needs "a **widening, not a narrowing**" of psychiatric skills.

## In depth

### Lishman's definitions [1]
| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Organic psychiatry | High probability that examination and investigation will uncover cerebral or systemic pathology responsible for, or contributing to, the mental condition |
| Neuropsychiatry (Lishman, narrow) | Interface of neurology and psychiatry; disorders from clearly identifiable brain malfunction; excludes endocrine, toxic and metabolic causes (these belong to general medicine) |
| Neuropsychiatry (4e usage, broad) | Synonymous with organic psychiatry, plus **conversion disorders** |
| Biological psychiatry | An **approach**, not a clinical discipline |

### Features that separate organic from non-organic disorders [1]
- **Cognitive impairment** is the feature that distinguishes neuropsychiatric disorders from the rest of psychiatry.
- **Bonhoeffer's "exogenous reactions":** different brain insults produce similar syndromes (delirium, dementia), so the syndromes point to cerebral disorder whatever the pathology.
