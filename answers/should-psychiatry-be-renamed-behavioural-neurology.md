## Exam answer

### Introduction: the question behind the question [1, 2]
- Renaming psychiatry "behavioural neurology" assumes that mental disorders are **simply brain disorders**, so the specialty boundary is outdated.
- There have been **recent calls to abandon the distinction** between neurological and psychiatric disorders on **philosophical and moral grounds**. White, Rickards and Zeman's 2012 *BMJ* call to end it is cited [2].
- The debate moves from **philosophy** (is the distinction real?), through **biology** (is there a pathological process?), to **sociology, culture and prejudice** (whose job is it? who gets the credit or blame?) [2].

### How the split arose [1]
- Neurology and psychiatry share **a common root**.
- Disorders with **behavioural disturbance** came to be seen as **"functional"**, without a structural basis. Neurology took symptoms with an **organic** origin.
- Neurology matured into a biological specialty close to internal medicine. Psychiatry added **socioeconomic, familial and interpersonal** dimensions. The two **drifted apart**.

### Arguments FOR merger or renaming [1, 2]
1. **A shared neuroscience base.**
   - fMRI, imaging, genetics and molecular medicine (signal transmission) show **how closely connected** the fields are [1].
   - "Psychiatry and neurology share a common basis in neuroscience" [1].
2. **The structural/functional divide has blurred.**
   - Many **dystonias once thought "functional" and psychogenic turned out to be biological** [1].
3. **Behavioural neurology has "effaced the border".**
   - Imaging relates personality traits and neuropsychiatric symptoms to genetic, biochemical and **receptor** characteristics, which can **expand and modify classification** [1].
4. **Psychiatric disorders are physical too.**
   - Schizophrenia shows **quantitative regional brain change** on structural MRI, and affective disorder to a lesser degree [2].
5. **Overlapping clinical territory.**
   - **Epilepsy and dementia** are areas of cross-fertilisation [1].
   - **Alzheimer's disease** is a neurological disorder, yet **core business for old-age psychiatrists** [2].
6. **Public health.**
   - Brain disorders cause **35% of the disease burden in Europe**, and **62% of that is mental disorders**.
   - WHO cares about public health, not specialties, so integration serves society [1].

### Arguments AGAINST renaming [1, 2]
1. **The disorders are empirically different.**
   - **Crossley et al. 2015** meta-analysed whole-brain **voxel-based morphometry** studies across neurological and psychiatric diagnoses. They found a **clear, statistically robust difference** between the two classes [2].
   - **Neurological side:** **basal ganglia, insula**, primary sensory and motor networks, and, unexpectedly, the **dorsal prefrontal "executive" region**.
   - **Psychiatric side:** the **medial prefrontal cortex**, part of the **default mode network**, involved in **self-reflection and social cognition**. Also, oddly, **visual association cortex** (lingual gyrus; face perception).
   - **Contested:** the **temporal cortex** (hippocampus, limbic system, mesial temporal sclerosis).
   - David and Nicholson conclude the two are **"quite subtly, different"**, with **no hierarchy** between them [2].
2. **"Pathology" is ill-defined.**
   - One proposed rule: a disorder is neurological if it is reliably associated with a recognisable CNS pathological process.
   - That begs questions. Does "reliably" mean necessary and sufficient? Is pathology microscopic or macroscopic? Does a **group-level statistical difference** from controls count?
   - Schizophrenia's brain changes are detectable **only at group level, against an arbitrary statistical threshold** [2].
3. **Psychiatry is defined by its domain, not by dualism.**
   - Quoting McHugh and Slavney: psychiatrists focus on **thoughts, perceptions, moods and behaviours**, and causal explanation spans "from intermediary metabolism (a 'body' issue) to interpersonal misunderstanding (a 'mind' issue)" [2].
   - A "neurology" label would **drop the social and interpersonal level** that psychiatry added [1, 2].
4. **The skill sets differ.**
   - **Neurologists will not manage schizophrenia and depression**, and **psychiatrists will not take over multiple sclerosis, epilepsies or hereditary ataxias** [1].
   - **Mental-status examination is traditionally the weakest part of the neurological examination**, and psychiatrists neglect the sensory and motor examination [1].
   - Neurologists show little interest in seeing schizophrenia patients despite their brain changes [2].
5. **The specialties are diverging and subspecialising** in practice [1].
6. **Methodological caveats** in the biological evidence [2]:
   - **medication confounds structure** (antipsychotics may increase volume loss; typical antipsychotics enlarge the basal ganglia);
   - **grey matter is not the whole story** (white matter and connectivity studies, e.g., DTI, are awaited);
   - the **insula** is "psychiatric" in Goodkind 2015 but "neurological" in Crossley 2015.

### A middle path: integration without absorption [1, 2]
- **Joint working:** psychiatrists in neurology teams and vice versa [1].
- **Cross-training:** in some countries psychiatry is mandatory in neurology training and vice versa. Europe is **harmonising** specialty content [1].
- **WFN and WPA:** work towards an **integrated definition** of each specialty's content [1].
- **Shared research platforms** in basic neuroscience [1].
- **Keep asking** "what makes a psychiatric disorder" using every available method [2].

**Conclusion.** [Likely, a synthesis of the two sources] **No.** Psychiatry should not be renamed behavioural neurology. The evidence:
- psychiatric disorders are brain disorders, but **not the same brain disorders**: Crossley's VBM meta-analysis separates them, with psychiatric conditions centred on **self-referential and social-cognitive networks** (medial prefrontal and default mode);
- psychiatry's subject matter runs from **molecules to interpersonal and social relationships**, which a neurological label would shrink;
- neither specialty is equipped to absorb the other's core work.

The right response is **integration, not renaming**: shared neuroscience training and research, joint clinics in overlap areas (epilepsy, dementia, functional disorders, neuropsychiatry), and harmonised curricula. In Aarli's Kipling metaphor, two strong specialties should "stand face to face".
