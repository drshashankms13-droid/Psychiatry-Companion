## Exam answer

**Introduction.**
- **What it is:** Alzheimer's disease (AD) is the **commonest dementia**: about **60%** of cases; up to two-thirds [1, 2].
- **Hallmarks:**
  - insidious, progressive **amnesia** with **aphasia, apraxia and agnosia**;
  - neuropathology of **neuritic (amyloid) plaques and neurofibrillary (tau) tangles** with neuronal and synaptic loss.
- **Early vs late onset:** the same disease; age 65 is a social, not biological, cut-off [1].

### Aetiopathogenesis [1, 3]

**1. Neuropathology:**
- **Neuritic plaques:**
  - extracellular **Aβ** amyloid (Congo red, birefringent); diffuse plaques are precursors;
  - correlate less with dementia than tangles.
- **Neurofibrillary tangles:**
  - intraneuronal **hyperphosphorylated tau** in **paired helical filaments**;
  - begin in the **entorhinal cortex**, then hippocampus, then neocortex (**Braak**); the cerebellum is spared;
  - **tangles correlate best with severity**;
  - "ghost" tangles remain after neuronal death.
- **Also:** synaptic and neuronal loss, granulovacuolar degeneration, **amyloid angiopathy**, vascular change.
- **Macroscopic:** atrophy, especially **hippocampal and temporoparietal**.

**2. Molecular biology:**
- **APP processing:**
  - α-secretase cleavage is **non-amyloidogenic**;
  - **β-secretase (BACE) then γ-secretase** (presenilin complex) yields **Aβ40/42**;
  - **Aβ42 is more fibrillogenic** and inhibits LTP.
- **Tau:**
  - normally stabilises microtubules for **axonal transport**;
  - hyperphosphorylation (e.g., **GSK-3**, which lithium inhibits) causes detachment, aggregation and transport failure.
- **Amyloid cascade hypothesis (Hardy & Higgins 1992):** APP dysregulation → ↑Aβ → oligomers → tau phosphorylation → tangles → neuronal death → dementia.
  - **Support:** all FAD mutations raise Aβ or Aβ42; APP mutations inside the Aβ region instead cause haemorrhagic angiopathy.
  - **Gap:** transgenic mice get plaques without tangles.

**3. Neurochemistry:**
- **Cholinergic hypothesis:**
  - loss of the **nucleus basalis of Meynert** and of choline acetyltransferase;
  - anticholinergic drugs hasten decline.
- Equally marked loss in the **locus coeruleus (noradrenaline)** and **dorsal raphe (serotonin)**; less clear glutamate deficit.

**4. Genetics:**

| Type | Genes | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Early-onset familial (autosomal dominant)** | **APP** (chromosome 21), **PSEN1** (chromosome 14; over 70 mutations; earliest onset), **PSEN2** (chromosome 1; Volga Germans) | About 5 per 100,000 at risk; nearly all onset before 55. **Down's syndrome**: trisomy of APP means AD pathology is invariable. Predictive testing via clinical genetics (Huntington's protocol; counselling over 3–6 months; do not test 25%-risk grandchildren) |
| **Late-onset susceptibility** | **APOE ε4** | Heritability 60–70%. ε4: 3–4× risk (heterozygote), about 10× (homozygote); **ε2 protective**. Shifts **age at onset** ("when, not if"). **Not recommended as a clinical test** [1, 3]. A chromosome 10 locus; over 100 unreplicated genes |

**5. Risk and protective factors** [1]:
- **Risk:**
  - **age** (the overwhelming factor);
  - female sex (2–3:1);
  - **head injury** (OR about 1.6, men; dementia pugilistica);
  - **vascular**: hypertension (treatment may reduce risk), smoking, homocysteine;
  - **diabetes and insulin resistance** (OR 1.3–1.9);
  - **prior depression** (likely prodrome);
  - low education.
- **Protective:**
  - education and **cognitive reserve** (Nun study; head size);
  - physical and mental activity;
  - **NSAIDs** (observational only; trials negative);
  - fish oils.
- **Disproved:** HRT (RCTs negative); aluminium (excluded except early dialysis).

### Clinical features [1, 3]
- **Onset:** insidious, usually after 40; most cases are elderly.
- **Three classic stages:**
  1. **Stage 1 (2–3 years):** failing **recent memory**, muddled inefficiency, **spatial disorientation**; partial insight early.
  2. **Stage 2:** rapid intellectual and personality decline; **parietal features**: **dysphasia, apraxia, agnosia, acalculia**.
  3. **Stage 3:** profound apathetic dementia, bedridden, doubly incontinent; rigidity, primitive reflexes (snout 41%), myoclonus (5%), **seizures** (late), wasting.

**The "four As":**
- **Amnesia** (invariable and early):
  - encoding and storage deficit with rapid forgetting;
  - recent before remote (temporal gradient);
  - semantic memory affected later (verbal fluency).
- **Aphasia:** word-finding difficulty, circumlocution, then receptive problems, then echolalia and perseveration.
- **Apraxia:** ideomotor; dressing apraxia (OT assessment).
- **Agnosia:** **prosopagnosia**, **autoprosopagnosia**, "**mirror sign**".
- Plus **executive dysfunction** (serial 7s, Stroop).
- **Klüver–Bucy features:** hyperorality, hypermetamorphosis, hyperphagia (in over three-quarters of early-onset cases).

**Function (FAST stages 1–7):**
- IADLs are lost before basic ADLs;
- function predicts institutionalisation;
- **MMSE correlates only modestly with function**.

**BPSD (Table 9.1)** [1]:

| Domain | Frequency |
| --- | --- |
| Delusions (**theft**, secondary to amnesia) | 20–30% |
| Hallucinations (visual > auditory) | 20–30% |
| Misidentification syndromes | 10–20% |
| Major depression | Up to 10%; minor up to 30% |
| Anxiety | Over 50% |
| Apathy | 15–40% |
| Agitation | 30–70% |
| Wandering | 15–40% |
| Aggression | 20–40% |
| Sleep/circadian disturbance | 30–80% |

BPSD are the **main determinant of carer burden and institutionalisation**.

**Suicide risk:** three to eight times higher after a hospital dementia diagnosis (Danish study) [3].

### Diagnosis [1, 3]
- **Criteria:**
  - **NINCDS-ADRDA** (probable, possible, definite; sensitivity 88–93%, specificity 55–61%) [1];
  - **DSM-5-TR probable AD:** causative mutation, **or** clear memory decline plus another domain, steady progression without plateaus, and no mixed aetiology [3].
- **Features against AD:**
  - sudden onset;
  - early focal signs;
  - early seizures or gait disturbance.
- **Investigations:**
  - **bloods**: FBC, U&E, LFTs, TFTs, B12/folate, ± syphilis serology; CXR;
  - **MRI**: **hippocampal/medial temporal atrophy** (sensitivity 85%, specificity 88%); atrophy rate 2.4%/year vs 0.4%;
  - **FDG-PET/SPECT**: temporoparietal hypometabolism (86%/86%);
  - **EEG**: early loss of alpha, later diffuse theta/delta (useful vs FTD, where EEG stays normal);
  - **biomarkers** [3]: CSF ↓Aβ42, ↑total and phospho-tau; **amyloid and tau PET**;
  - **ECG** before cholinesterase inhibitors [1, 2].

**Course:** progressive; death in about **2–8 years** (longer in some).
- **Faster decline with:** worse baseline cognition, aphasia or apraxia, psychosis, EPS, early onset.

### Management [1, 2]

**1. Diagnosis and information:**
- most patients with mild dementia want to be told;
- skilled, honest, patient-led disclosure;
- address driving, work, **lasting power of attorney and advance directives** while capacity remains;
- Alzheimer's Society and carer groups.

**2. Physical health:**
- treat infection, heart failure, anaemia, hypertension;
- avoid iatrogenic toxicity;
- **minimise anticholinergic burden**;
- exercise and nutrition.

**3. Cognitive enhancers (Maudsley Table 6.1):**

| Drug | Mechanism | Start → usual dose | Key adverse effects |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Donepezil** | Selective reversible AChE-I | 5 mg → **10 mg daily** (4-week steps) | Diarrhoea, nausea, headache, nightmares, **bradycardia** |
| **Rivastigmine** | AChE + BuChE inhibitor | 1.5 mg bd → 3–6 mg bd; **patch 4.6 → 9.5 (→ 13.3) mg/24 hours** | GI effects (fewer with the patch); minimal interactions |
| **Galantamine** | AChE-I + nicotinic modulator | 8 mg XL → 16–24 mg | Nausea, vomiting, bradycardia |
| **Memantine** | NMDA antagonist | 5 mg → **20 mg daily** (weekly steps) | Dizziness, headache, constipation; renal dosing |

- **Efficacy:** AChE-Is give **modest** cognitive, functional and global benefit (NNT 4–12). About 68% have a period of stabilisation, then decline at the prior rate. Memantine gives small benefit in moderate–severe AD.
- **NICE (Box 6.2):**
  - **AChE-Is for mild–moderate AD**;
  - **memantine** for moderate AD if AChE-Is are not tolerated, or for severe AD;
  - **add memantine** to an AChE-I in moderate (consider) or severe (offer) disease;
  - **do not stop for severity alone** (benefit even at MMSE 3; stopping increases nursing-home placement).
- **Cautions:**
  - bradycardia, sick sinus syndrome, conduction block, β-blockers or digoxin (**pulse checks**; ECG if cardiac history or rate-lowering drugs);
  - asthma/COPD, peptic ulcer, seizures, urinary obstruction.
- **Switching:** if one AChE-I isn't tolerated, up to 50% tolerate another (switch after side-effects resolve).
- **Stopping:** taper; monitor for 1–3 months; restart if decline.
- **Not recommended:** statins, NSAIDs, vitamin E, Ginkgo (evidence inconsistent), omega-3, trazodone for cognition.
- **Anti-amyloid antibodies:**
  - **lecanemab**: 27% slowing on CDR-SB over 18 months; **ARIA in 21%**; FDA-approved;
  - **donanemab**: about 35% slowing; ARIA-E 24%;
  - require amyloid confirmation (PET/CSF) in MCI or mild AD;
  - Maudsley notes no disease-modifying agent recommended by NICE at the time of writing [2].

**4. BPSD:**
- **non-pharmacological first** (most effective and safest);
- treat depression with an **SSRI** (avoid anticholinergic antidepressants);
- antipsychotics **only in extremis**, for psychosis or severe risk, at low dose with regular review (**increased serious adverse effects and mortality**; in one large multicentre trial (Schneider 2006), benefits were often outweighed by adverse effects) [1].
- See the separate BPSD answer.

**5. Psychosocial and care:**
- **carer support and respite** (a quarter of carers have significant stress);
- day centres; home care; key worker;
- cognitive stimulation therapy, reality orientation, validation therapy (variable evidence);
- **occupational therapy**;
- timely, well-supported transition to residential care.

### Case: 70-year-old woman with 1 year of progressive memory loss, disorientation, ADL difficulty, apraxia and word-finding difficulty (p3-760d8b63)

**Probable diagnosis:** **major neurocognitive disorder due to probable Alzheimer's disease** (late onset), mild–moderate severity [1, 3].
- insidious and progressive over 1 year;
- **short-term memory impairment** (amnesia);
- **word-finding difficulty** (early aphasia);
- **apraxia**;
- **disorientation** (spatial and temporal);
- **functional decline** (ADLs).

**Differential diagnosis** [1]:
- **vascular or mixed dementia:** stepwise course, focal signs, vascular risk, imaging;
- **DLB:** fluctuation, visual hallucinations, parkinsonism, neuroleptic sensitivity;
- **FTD:** early behavioural or language change with preserved memory and orientation (less likely);
- **depressive pseudodementia:** "don't know" answers, affective symptoms; even so, late-life depression often heralds dementia;
- **reversible/secondary causes:**
  - hypothyroidism, B12/folate deficiency, neurosyphilis;
  - subdural haematoma, NPH (gait, incontinence), tumour;
  - drugs (anticholinergics), alcohol;
- **delirium superimposed:** fluctuation, clouding (look for infection, e.g., UTI).

**Assessment:**
- **History from an informant:**
  - onset, course, first symptom;
  - ADLs and IADLs;
  - BPSD (delusions of theft, wandering, sleep), risk (falls, cooking, finances, driving, abuse);
  - vascular risk, alcohol, drugs, family history, mood.
- **Examination:** physical and neurological (focal signs, parkinsonism, gait, primitive reflexes), hearing and vision.
- **Cognitive testing:** MMSE as a severity measure; tests of frontal and parietal function; neuropsychology if borderline.
- **Investigations:**
  - FBC, U&E, Ca²⁺, LFT, TFT, B12/folate, glucose, ± VDRL/HIV;
  - urine;
  - **CT/MRI** (atrophy, hippocampal volume, vascular lesions);
  - ± SPECT/FDG-PET, EEG;
  - **ECG** before AChE-Is.
- **OT functional assessment** (kitchen or home); carer burden.

**Management plan:**
1. Share the diagnosis sensitively with patient and family; give information and Alzheimer's Society contact.
2. **Start an AChE-I** (e.g., **donepezil 5 → 10 mg**, or a rivastigmine patch) after a pulse/ECG check; monitor GI effects, bradycardia, weight; review response.
3. **Add memantine** when disease becomes moderate–severe.
4. Treat vascular risk factors and comorbid illness; reduce anticholinergic burden.
5. Non-drug: cognitive stimulation or reality orientation; OT assessment and adaptations for apraxia.
6. Safety: falls, cooking, medication supervision, driving, finances.
7. Legal: **lasting power of attorney, advance directive**, capacity assessment.
8. Carer support, respite, day care; community key worker.
9. Monitor and treat BPSD (non-drug first; SSRI for depression; avoid antipsychotics unless severe).
10. Plan future care: home care, then residential care when needed; advance care planning.

**Conclusion.** AD is the commonest dementia. Its pathogenesis centres on amyloid (APP→Aβ) and tau pathology with cholinergic loss, shaped by genetics (APP, PSEN1/2, APOE ε4) and age-related and vascular risk factors. It presents with insidious amnesia, then aphasia, apraxia and agnosia, with BPSD driving burden. Management is:
- holistic and staged;
- **AChE-Is ± memantine** (modest symptomatic benefit);
- anti-amyloid antibodies emerging for early AD;
- non-pharmacological BPSD care;
- risk, legal and carer support.

## In depth

### AD vs normal ageing [1]
- **Ageing:** slowed processing, poorer encoding strategies, **fluid** intelligence decline; crystallised intelligence preserved.
- **AD:** **primary** loss of episodic memory, language and spatial cognition, following pathology from the limbic system to association cortex.
