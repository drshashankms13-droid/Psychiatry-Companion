## Exam answer

**Definition.** Neuroleptic malignant syndrome (NMS) is a **rare but potentially fatal** adverse reaction to antipsychotics and other dopamine antagonists. It is "an acute disorder of **thermoregulation and neuromotor control**" characterised by:
- **muscular rigidity**;
- **hyperthermia**;
- **altered consciousness**;
- **autonomic dysfunction** [1].

The presentation is **heterogeneous**. Many cases are **atypical**, lacking hyperthermia or rigidity, especially with SGAs [1].

### Epidemiology [1]
- Incidence estimates:
  - **0.02–0.03%**;
  - pharmacovigilance data: **0.16%** (1993–2015) and **0.11%** (2004–2017).
- **Mortality about 5.6%**; probably **lower with SGAs than FGAs**.
- **Highest incidence with high-potency FGAs**; lower with SGAs and low-potency FGAs.
- **Almost all antipsychotics** have been implicated, including lurasidone, ziprasidone, iloperidone, aripiprazole, paliperidone (and its palmitate LAI), asenapine and risperidone LAI.
- **No reports as of mid-2024 with pimavanserin, cariprazine, brexpiprazole or lumateperone.**
- **Non-antipsychotic causes:**
  - antidepressants, valproate, phenytoin, lithium;
  - **metoclopramide, prochlorperazine**;
  - SSRI or cholinesterase-inhibitor co-prescription with antipsychotics;
  - SGA + SSRI combinations can produce NMS/serotonin-syndrome overlaps.

### Risk factors [1]
| Drug-related | Patient-related | State-related |
| --- | --- | --- |
| High-potency FGAs; **recent or rapid dose increase**; rapid dose reduction; **abrupt withdrawal of anticholinergics**; **antipsychotic polypharmacy**; higher doses; **depot/LAI**; parenteral use | **Male sex, younger age** (more lethal in older people); organic brain disease; Parkinson's disease (also from **dopaminergic drug withdrawal**); alcoholism; hyperthyroidism; cognitive impairment | **Dehydration, exhaustion, agitation**, catatonia, physical restraint, heat |

### Clinical features (DSM-5-TR / Levenson / International Expert Consensus 2011)
- **Core:**
  - **hyperthermia** (above 38 °C on at least 2 occasions);
  - **"lead-pipe" rigidity**;
  - **altered mental status** (confusion to coma; **mutism, catatonia**);
  - **autonomic instability** (tachycardia, labile or high BP, diaphoresis, sialorrhoea, incontinence) [1].
- **Laboratory:**
  - **CK raised, often above 1,000 IU/L** [1];
  - leukocytosis;
  - altered LFTs;
  - **low serum iron**;
  - metabolic acidosis;
  - myoglobinuria leading to AKI.
- **CK is not diagnostic alone.** Asymptomatic CK rises are common (IM injections, agitation, restraint) [1].
- **Onset** usually within days to 2 weeks of starting or increasing a dose. **Course 7–10 days** (longer with depots).

### Differential diagnosis
| Condition | Distinguishing features |
| --- | --- |
| **Serotonin syndrome** | Serotonergic drug; rapid onset (hours); **clonus, hyperreflexia**, mydriasis, diarrhoea; less rigidity |
| **Malignant catatonia** | Catatonic features before antipsychotics; may be indistinguishable. Both respond to **lorazepam and ECT** |
| **Malignant hyperthermia** | Volatile anaesthetics or succinylcholine; intraoperative |
| **Anticholinergic toxicity** | Dry, flushed skin; mydriasis; urinary retention; no rigidity |
| **Infection** (CNS, sepsis) | Fever, meningism; CSF |
| **Heat stroke** | Dry skin; flaccid |
| **Lethal catatonia / stimulant toxicity / alcohol or benzodiazepine withdrawal** | History |
| **Parkinsonism-hyperpyrexia** syndrome | Dopaminergic drug withdrawal in PD |

### Management [1]
**1. Recognise early and stop the cause:**
- **stop all antipsychotics** and other dopamine antagonists (metoclopramide);
- **failing to stop the antipsychotic predicts mortality** [1];
- review lithium, SSRIs and anticholinergic withdrawal.

**2. In the psychiatric unit (early or mild):**
- monitor temperature, pulse and BP;
- hydrate;
- **benzodiazepines** (IM lorazepam has been used) [1].

**3. Transfer to medical or ICU care (moderate–severe):**
- **IV fluids**: rehydrate, prevent renal failure from myoglobinuria;
- **cooling**: blankets, antipyretics (limited effect because the fever is central);
- correct electrolytes;
- **DVT prophylaxis**;
- airway protection; **ventilation** if needed [1];
- monitor CK, renal function, urine myoglobin, ECG.

**4. Specific drugs** (evidence limited; guidelines vary) [1]:
- **lorazepam** 1–2 mg IV/IM every 4–6 hours (first-line, especially with catatonic features);
- **bromocriptine** 2.5 mg 2–3 times a day, increasing to 15–45 mg/day (dopamine agonist);
- **dantrolene** 1–2.5 mg/kg IV, repeated, up to 10 mg/kg/day (direct muscle relaxant; for severe hyperthermia and rigidity);
- alternatives: amantadine, L-dopa, apomorphine, carbamazepine [1];
- **ECT** is effective, even after pharmacotherapy fails. Consider it for refractory NMS or when underlying catatonia or psychosis needs treatment [1].

**5. Complications to watch:**
- **rhabdomyolysis**, AKI;
- aspiration pneumonia, respiratory failure;
- DIC, PE;
- seizures, arrhythmias.

**6. Restarting antipsychotics (rechallenge carries acceptable risk)** [1]:
- **wait at least 5 days, preferably longer** (at least 2 weeks is common practice), until NMS has **fully resolved**;
- **start very low, increase very slowly**;
- monitor temperature, pulse, BP and CK (CK monitoring is controversial);
- use a **structurally unrelated** drug or one with **low D2 affinity: quetiapine or clozapine**;
- aripiprazole is possible, but it has a long half-life and has been linked to NMS;
- **avoid depots/LAIs and high-potency FGAs**;
- **ensure hydration**; avoid co-prescribing lithium initially;
- inform the patient; document NMS as an **adverse drug reaction**.

**Conclusion.** NMS is a medical emergency diagnosed clinically (fever, rigidity, altered consciousness, autonomic instability, raised CK), with frequent atypical presentations. Treatment is to **stop the antipsychotic**, give supportive ICU care, and use **benzodiazepines, bromocriptine or dantrolene, and ECT** for refractory cases. Rechallenge is usually possible after full recovery, with a low-potency antipsychotic started cautiously.

## In depth

### Background
- First described by **Delay and Deniker (1960)** with haloperidol: "syndrome malin des neuroleptiques".
- **Rhabdomyolysis and renal failure** account for much of the mortality. Mortality fell from about 20–30% in the 1980s with better recognition.
- **NMS is conceptualised as a drug-induced form of malignant catatonia**: they share mechanism and response to lorazepam and ECT [general knowledge].

### Epidemiology (expanded)
- **Recurrence on rechallenge:** about 15–30%; reduced by waiting longer and using low-potency drugs [general knowledge].
- **India:** dehydration in hot climates, IM haloperidol in agitated patients, and catatonia are common risk combinations [Guessing].

### Classification and criteria
**DSM-5-TR NMS criteria** (medication-induced movement disorder):
- dopamine antagonist exposure within 72 hours;
- hyperthermia;
- rigidity;
- **plus** supportive features: mental status change, CK raised at least 4× the upper limit, autonomic instability, tachypnoea, negative work-up.

**International expert consensus (Gurrera 2011) point-weighted criteria:**
- exposure within 72 hours (20 points);
- hyperthermia (18);
- rigidity (17);
- mental status change (13);
- CK raised (10);
- sympathetic lability (10);
- hypermetabolism (5);
- negative work-up (7).

A cut-off of about 74 points gives best agreement [general knowledge].

### Mechanism
- **Central D2 blockade:**
  - in the **hypothalamus** causes hyperthermia;
  - in the **nigrostriatal** pathway causes rigidity, which generates heat;
  - in the **spinal cord and sympathetic** system causes autonomic dysregulation.
- **Sympathoadrenal hyperactivity** hypothesis.
- **Low serum iron** (iron is needed for D2 receptor function) is a marker.
- **Skeletal muscle** effects via calcium (the basis for dantrolene).

### Clinical features
- **Prodrome:** new rigidity, mutism or confusion, then fever. Watch for **unexplained tachycardia or BP lability** in a patient recently started or escalated on antipsychotics.
- **Atypical NMS** with SGAs: less fever or rigidity; diagnosis by exclusion.

### Assessment
- Bloods: **CK (serial)**, U&E, creatinine, LFTs, CBC, serum iron, calcium, phosphate, magnesium, ABG, coagulation.
- **Urine myoglobin.**
- ECG.
- Infection screen (blood cultures, chest X-ray); **LP and CT/MRI** to exclude CNS infection or a lesion.
- Drug levels (lithium).
- Rating: Bush–Francis Catatonia Rating Scale.

### Management details
| Severity (Strawn 2007 staging; general knowledge) | Management |
| --- | --- |
| Mild (rigidity, temperature below 38 °C, HR below 100) | Stop antipsychotic; benzodiazepines; hydration |
| Moderate (rigidity, 38–40 °C, HR 100–120) | Plus lorazepam IV; consider bromocriptine or amantadine |
| Severe (above 40 °C, HR above 120) | ICU; dantrolene + bromocriptine; consider ECT |

### Critical appraisal
- **No RCTs** exist. Drug therapies rest on case series, and dantrolene and bromocriptine **may not reduce mortality** (some analyses suggest worse outcomes) [general knowledge]. Guideline recommendations "vary widely and are based on limited evidence" [1].
- Under-reporting of mild and atypical forms.
- CK elevation alone leads to over-diagnosis, so use clinical criteria [1].

### Indian context
- Practical issues: availability of dantrolene (expensive, limited); bromocriptine is widely available. **ECT** is often the practical rescue in tertiary centres.
- **MHCA 2017:** consent and ECT rules apply. For emergency management of NMS, treatment is medical, not psychiatric emergency treatment under Section 94.

### Recent advances
- **Pharmacogenomics:** DRD2 Taq1A, CYP2D6 variants (inconsistent).
- **Recognition of NMS with newer agents** (lurasidone case reports) [1].
- **Catatonia–NMS spectrum** research.
