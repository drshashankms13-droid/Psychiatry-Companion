## Exam answer

**Definition.** **Intellectual disability (ID)**, called **disorder of intellectual development** in ICD-11 (6A00) and **intellectual developmental disorder** in DSM-5, is characterised by three things [1, 2]:
1. **Significant limitations in intellectual functioning:** reasoning, problem-solving, planning, abstract thinking, judgement, academic learning, learning from experience. Confirmed by clinical assessment **and** standardised intelligence testing, roughly **2 SD below the mean (IQ about 70 or below)**.
2. **Deficits in adaptive functioning** in the **conceptual, social and practical** domains.
3. **Onset during the developmental period.**

In DSM-5 and ICD-11, **severity is defined by adaptive functioning, not IQ alone** [1].

### Goals of assessment
1. Confirm the diagnosis and its **severity**.
2. Find the **cause** (aetiology).
3. Identify **comorbid** physical, sensory and psychiatric conditions, which are often overshadowed.
4. Profile **strengths and needs** for intervention, education and rehabilitation.
5. Determine **disability percentage** for certification and benefits [1, 2].

### Components

**1. History**
- **Presenting concerns:** developmental delay, school failure, behaviour problems.
- **Prenatal:**
  - consanguinity;
  - maternal infections (rubella, CMV, toxoplasmosis);
  - **alcohol (FASD)** and drugs;
  - maternal hypothyroidism, diabetes, PKU;
  - radiation.
- **Perinatal:** prematurity, birth asphyxia, hypoglycaemia, neonatal jaundice.
- **Postnatal:** meningitis or encephalitis, head injury, seizures, lead exposure, malnutrition, severe deprivation [1].
- **Developmental milestones** (motor, language, social) and **regression** (which suggests a neurodegenerative or metabolic disorder, or Rett syndrome).
- **Family history:** ID, genetic syndromes, X-linked inheritance pattern (fragile X).
- **School performance; behaviour; seizures; vision and hearing; sleep; feeding.**

**2. Physical examination**
- Growth parameters (head circumference: microcephaly or macrocephaly).
- **Dysmorphic features**, neurocutaneous markers (ash-leaf spots in tuberous sclerosis; café-au-lait spots).
- Neurological signs; hearing and vision screening; thyroid status; signs of abuse or neglect.

**3. Psychometric assessment of intelligence** [1, 2]
| Tool | Age / notes |
| --- | --- |
| **WPPSI-IV** | 2.5–7 years |
| **WISC-IV/V** (Indian adaptations of WISC-IV) | 6–16 years |
| **Stanford-Binet 5** | 2–85 years |
| **Binet-Kamat Test (BKT)** | Indian; 3 years to adult |
| **MISIC** | Indian adaptation of WISC (older norms) |
| **NIEPID Indian Test of Intelligence** | Indian (2024 gazette lists it) [2] |
| **Leiter-3**, Raven's Progressive Matrices | **Non-verbal**: for language, hearing or motor impairment, ASD [1] |
| **Developmental scales** (under 5): Bayley-III/IV, Developmental Screening Test, Gesell | Global developmental delay |

**4. Adaptive behaviour assessment** [1, 2]
- **Vineland Adaptive Behaviour Scales-3 (VABS-3):** standard internationally.
- **Vineland Social Maturity Scale (VSMS, Indian adaptation):** **mandated for disability % in India (2024)**.
- ABAS-3; AAMR Adaptive Behaviour Scale.
- Domains: conceptual (language, literacy, money, time), social (interpersonal, responsibility, gullibility), practical (self-care, occupational, safety) [1].

**5. Aetiological investigations** (guided by findings)
- **First tier:** **chromosomal microarray** (yield about 15–20%) and **fragile X** (FMR1) testing; karyotype if microarray is unavailable or a balanced rearrangement is suspected.
- **Next:** exome or genome sequencing (yield about 30–40%); targeted gene tests (Rett/MECP2; Angelman/Prader-Willi methylation).
- **Metabolic screen** (TMS, urine organic acids, ammonia, lactate) if regression, consanguinity or episodic illness.
- **Thyroid function**; **MRI brain** (microcephaly, focal signs, seizures); **EEG** (seizures, regression); lead level; audiology; ophthalmology.

**6. Psychiatric and behavioural assessment**
- Psychiatric disorders occur in about **30–40%** (3–4× the general population): ADHD, anxiety, depression, psychosis, ASD, stereotypic movement disorder, challenging behaviour [1].
- **Diagnostic overshadowing:** symptoms are wrongly attributed to ID [1].
- **Adapted tools:** **DC-LD** (UK); PAS-ADD; Aberrant Behaviour Checklist. Behaviour change may signal pain, constipation, dental problems or abuse.
- **Functional behavioural analysis** for challenging behaviour (antecedent–behaviour–consequence).

**7. Family and social assessment**
- Carer burden, parenting, understanding and expectations, resources, schooling, entitlements.

### Severity

| Severity (ICD-11 / DSM-5) | Approximate IQ (reference only) | Adaptive profile |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mild | 50–69 | Academic difficulty; independent self-care; supported employment |
| Moderate | 35–49 | Basic academics; needs support for complex tasks |
| Severe | 20–34 | Limited language; extensive support |
| Profound | <20 | Pervasive support; often sensory or motor comorbidity |

Source: [1].

### Certification in India (RPwD 2024 guidelines) [2]
- **Medical Board:** MS/CMO + paediatrician/physician + psychiatrist (or child psychiatrist) + clinical or rehabilitation psychologist.
- **Disability % from the VSMS score, irrespective of age:**
  - 0–20 profound: **100%**.
  - 21–35 severe: **90%**.
  - 36–54 moderate: **75%**.
  - 55–69 mild: **50%**.
  - 70–84 borderline: **25%**.
- IQ by VSMS, BKT, WISC-IV, MISIC or NIEPID test.
- **Minimum age 1 year.**
- **Under 5:** temporary certificate for **global developmental delay**, valid until age 5.
- **Over 5:** ID certificate, renewed at 10 and 18; **lifelong after 18**.

**Conclusion.** Assessing ID is **multi-dimensional**. It combines history, examination, standardised IQ and adaptive-behaviour testing, aetiological work-up (genetic testing first), and careful screening for physical and psychiatric comorbidity. Diagnosis and severity rest on **adaptive functioning**, not IQ alone. The goal is not a label but a **needs-based plan** for education, therapy, family support, health care and legal entitlements. In India, the 2024 guidelines make VSMS the basis of disability certification.

## In depth

### Background
- **Historical terms:** "idiocy"/"feeble-mindedness" → "mental retardation" (ICD-10, DSM-IV) → "intellectual disability" (US Rosa's Law 2010; DSM-5) → "disorder of intellectual development" (ICD-11) [1].
- **IQ testing:** Binet & Simon (1905). Wechsler scales (1939 onward). Indian adaptations: Binet-Kamat (1930s–60s), MISIC (Malin, 1969).
- **Adaptive behaviour concept:** Doll's Vineland Social Maturity Scale (1935), adapted for India by Malin (VSMS).

### Epidemiology
- **Global prevalence:** about 1–3%. Mild ID accounts for about 85% of cases.
- **India:** NMHS screened for ID; treatment gap 70.5% [3]. Census 2011 recorded about 15 lakh people with "mental retardation".
- **Causes:**
  - Identified in about 50–70% of severe ID; less often in mild ID.
  - **Down syndrome** is the commonest chromosomal cause.
  - **Fragile X** is the commonest inherited cause.
  - **FASD** is the commonest preventable cause in high-income countries.
  - Iodine deficiency and perinatal causes matter more in LMICs [1].

### Classification and criteria
| Feature | DSM-5-TR | ICD-11 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Name | Intellectual developmental disorder | Disorder of intellectual development (6A00) |
| Severity basis | Adaptive functioning in conceptual, social and practical domains | Standardised tests where possible; behavioural indicators otherwise |
| Under 5 | Global developmental delay (GDD) | Provisional diagnosis |
| Borderline intellectual functioning | V-code (not a disorder) | Not a disorder |

### Mechanism in detail: behavioural phenotypes [1]
| Syndrome | Genetics | Behavioural phenotype |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Down syndrome | Trisomy 21 (95%), translocation (4%), mosaic (1%) | Sociable; depression; **early-onset Alzheimer's (after 40)** |
| Fragile X | FMR1 CGG repeat >200 (Xq27.3) | Social anxiety, gaze avoidance, hand flapping, ADHD; macro-orchidism |
| Prader-Willi | Paternal 15q11-13 deletion / maternal UPD | Hyperphagia, skin picking, rigidity, tantrums; psychosis in mUPD |
| Angelman | Maternal 15q11-13 deletion / paternal UPD / UBE3A | Happy demeanour, laughter, seizures, ataxia, absent speech |
| Williams | 7q11.23 deletion | Hypersociable, anxious, visuospatial deficits, hyperacusis |
| Rett | MECP2 (X-linked; girls) | Regression at 6–18 months, hand-wringing, breathing abnormalities |
| Tuberous sclerosis | TSC1/TSC2 | ASD, ADHD, seizures |
| Smith-Magenis | 17p11.2 deletion | Self-injury, inverted melatonin rhythm |
| 22q11.2 deletion | — | Psychosis risk about 25% |
| PKU | PAH, autosomal recessive | Preventable with diet [1] |

### Clinical features in detail
- **Mild ID:** often identified at school; socially adequate. Vulnerable to exploitation, criminal-justice involvement and depression.
- **Severe or profound ID:** medical comorbidity (epilepsy about 30–50%; cerebral palsy; sensory impairments); dysphagia; challenging behaviour.
- **Psychiatric presentation** [1]:
  - Depression shows as withdrawal, irritability, self-injury, sleep and appetite change.
  - Anxiety shows as behavioural disturbance.
  - Psychosis is about 3× commoner; positive symptoms are less systematised.

### Assessment in detail: practical points
- **Choose tests matched to language, culture and sensory or motor abilities.** Indian norms matter: UK norms underestimate the IQ of Indian children.
- **Do not diagnose from a single IQ score.** Consider the standard error (±5) and the adaptive profile.
- **Under 5:** use developmental quotient and GDD; reassess later [2].
- **Hearing and vision** must be tested before attributing delay to ID [2].
- **Ask about regression:** it is a red flag for neurodegenerative or metabolic disease and needs urgent work-up.

### Differential diagnosis
| Condition | Distinguishing points |
| --- | --- |
| Specific learning disability | IQ ≥85; specific academic deficit [2] |
| ASD (without ID) | Social communication deficits with uneven profile |
| Sensory impairment | Audiology and ophthalmology |
| Language disorder | Non-verbal IQ normal |
| Borderline intellectual functioning | IQ 71–84; not a disorder (25% disability band in India [2]) |
| Severe deprivation or neglect | Improvement with enrichment |
| Childhood depression / psychosis | Acquired decline in functioning |
| Neurodegenerative disorder | Progressive regression |

### Management details: linked to assessment
- **Early intervention:** physiotherapy, occupational therapy, speech therapy, special education. District Early Intervention Centres (RBSK).
- **Family:** psychoeducation, parent training, respite, genetic counselling.
- **Health:** epilepsy, thyroid, dental, vision and hearing care; annual health checks.
- **Behaviour:**
  - Positive behaviour support first.
  - Medication only for a specific psychiatric disorder or severe challenging behaviour after non-drug measures: risperidone for aggression; **avoid anticholinergic burden**; low and slow dosing.
  - **STOMP** (UK): stop over-medication.
- **Legal and social:** disability certificate and UDID; National Trust guardianship (local level committee); Niramaya insurance; inclusive education (RTE, RPwD) [2].

### IPS Clinical Practice Guideline (Kishore, Udipi, Seshadri 2019): key points [5]
- **Definition:** significant limitations in **intellectual functioning and adaptive behaviour** during the developmental period. About **2.5%** of the global population has low intellectual functioning commensurate with ID. Indian point prevalence varies widely depending on whether intellectual, adaptive or both deficits are counted.
- **IQ cut-offs:**
  - **DSM-5:** IQ cut-off of 65–75 (70 ± 5 standard error); severity defined by **adaptive functioning** (conceptual, social, practical).
  - **ICD-10:** IQ 70 cut-off; mild 50–69, moderate 35–49, severe 20–34, profound below 20.
- **Adaptive behaviour:** the **Vineland Social Maturity Scale** is "the only standardised measure available in India". If it cannot be used, ask socioculturally relevant questions.
  - **Where IQ and SQ indicate different severity levels, decide in favour of SQ.** SQ reflects real-world functioning.
- **Clinical diagnosis vs disability assessment:** these are different processes. Certification follows the separate RPwD guidelines strictly.
- **Six clinical questions:**
  1. Are there significant limitations in intellectual and adaptive functioning?
  2. Comorbidities?
  3. Treatable aetiological conditions?
  4. Areas for intervention?
  5. Where and how to intervene?
  6. Where does the person stand in the life span?
- **Comorbidity:**
  - **epilepsy in 15–30%**, rising to about **50%** in severe ID;
  - **psychiatric disorders** with a cumulative prevalence of about **40%**;
  - **problem behaviours in 20–80%**.
- **Behavioural phenotypes:** hyperactivity in fragile X; self-hugging and trichotillomania in Smith–Magenis; schizophrenia-like illness in 22q11 deletion.
- **Aetiology:** genetic diagnosis matters for recurrence-risk counselling, prenatal diagnosis and management. **Over 80 potentially treatable causes** exist (for example, inborn errors of metabolism), so early identification is essential.
- **Rating scales for psychopathology:** Reiss Screen, Developmental Behavior Checklist, PIMRA. They are adjuncts to clinical diagnosis.
- **Problem behaviours:**
  - prioritise behaviours that cause **social exclusion or stigma**, or interfere with **learning**;
  - **functional analysis** (antecedents, consequences);
  - three-level plan: restructure the environment; **differential reinforcement** of adaptive behaviour; consequence management.
- **Pharmacotherapy:** psychosocial interventions first if a psychiatric diagnosis cannot be established. **Risperidone** is the most studied for aggression and stereotypies (the CPG refers to Maudsley for dosing).
- **Non-pharmacological, life-span approach:**
  - functional academics, then prevocational and vocational training, then independent living;
  - health and safety skills throughout;
  - aids and appliances for additional disabilities.

### Critical appraisal
- **IQ tests:** cultural bias; outdated Indian norms (MISIC 1969); verbal loading; floor effects in severe ID.
- **Adaptive measures:** depend on informants; the VSMS is old.
- **The 2024 guidelines' reliance on VSMS** for disability percentage simplifies certification but may not capture complex needs.
- **Genetic testing:** costly and poorly available in India. Yield and counselling capacity are limited.
- **Diagnostic overshadowing** leaves psychiatric disorders under-treated, while **antipsychotic over-prescribing** for behaviour is a known problem.

### Indian context
- **RPwD Act 2016:** ID is a specified disability. The 2024 certification rules are set out above [2].
- **National Trust Act 1999:** guardianship, Disha (early intervention), Samarth (respite), Niramaya.
- **NIEPID** (Secunderabad) develops Indian tools, including the Indian Test of Intelligence [2].
- **RBSK:** newborn and child screening; DEICs.
- **Prevention:** iodised salt; maternal care; newborn screening (congenital hypothyroidism; PKU limited); rubella vaccination; avoiding consanguinity-related recessive disorders (counselling).

### Recent advances
- **Established:** ICD-11/DSM-5 adaptive-functioning-based severity; first-tier CMA [1].
- **Emerging:**
  - Exome or genome sequencing as first tier.
  - Precision medicine for syndromic ID (e.g., mTOR inhibitors in TSC; trials in fragile X).
  - Digital adaptive-behaviour assessment.
  - Updated Indian intelligence tests (the NIEPID Indian Test of Intelligence) [2].
