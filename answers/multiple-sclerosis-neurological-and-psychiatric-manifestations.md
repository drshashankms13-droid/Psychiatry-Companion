## Exam answer

### Introduction and aetiology [1]
- **MS:** the commonest demyelinating disease; common in temperate northern regions, rare in the tropics; a major cause of chronic disability.
- **Aetiology:** genes plus environment cause an **immune-mediated inflammatory** CNS disease.
  - **Genetic:** HLA class II (MHC) region.
  - **Environmental:**
    - twin discordance; migration and latitude gradients (risk is fixed in **adolescence**);
    - **low vitamin D / sunlight**;
    - **smoking** (OR 1.36);
    - **EBV** (strongest infective evidence).
  - The hygiene hypothesis is not supported.
- **Pathology:**
  - sharply demarcated **plaques** of demyelination (periventricular, cerebellum, brainstem, cord, also grey matter);
  - BBB breakdown, inflammation (lymphocytes, macrophages), oligodendrocyte loss, gliosis;
  - **early and persistent axonal degeneration**;
  - lesion load greatly exceeds clinical expectation.

### Neurological manifestations [1]
- **Onset:** 20–40 years; females more often.
- **Course:**
  - **relapsing–remitting** at presentation in most → **secondary progressive** in almost all;
  - **primary progressive about 10%** (usual with onset after 50);
  - progressive-relapsing about 5%;
  - benign in about a fifth.
  - **Milestones:** limited walking at a median **8 years**; 100 m with a unilateral aid at 20 years; 10 m at 30 years.
  - **Better prognosis:** sensory or visual onset; younger onset. **Worse:** motor or cerebellar onset, progressive course.
- **Early features:**
  - **retrobulbar (optic) neuritis** (may precede others by years);
  - diplopia or nystagmus;
  - long-tract paraesthesiae or spastic paraparesis;
  - **urinary precipitancy**;
  - ataxia, intention tremor.
- **Signs:**
  - temporal disc pallor, nystagmus, **internuclear ophthalmoplegia** (dissociated conjugate gaze);
  - intention tremor, brisk reflexes, absent abdominals, extensor plantars;
  - impaired vibration and joint position sense.
  - Later: ataxia, dysarthria, visual loss, paraparesis, sensory loss, incontinence.
- **Seizures:** about **2%**.

### Investigations [1]
- **MRI:**
  - **periventricular ovoid T2 lesions of 5 mm or more** (also optic nerve, brainstem, cord);
  - **gadolinium enhancement = active lesions**;
  - **a normal brain MRI all but excludes MS**;
  - FLAIR/DIR for grey-matter lesions;
  - atrophy (grey matter more than white);
  - MRS (low NAA), MTI and DTI abnormal in normal-appearing tissue;
  - MRI can show dissemination in **space and time** (McDonald/Polman criteria).
- **CSF:**
  - mild mononuclear pleocytosis or protein rise in about half;
  - **raised IgG fraction**; **oligoclonal bands**.
- **Evoked potentials:** **delayed visual evoked responses** (subclinical optic lesions); brainstem auditory and somatosensory evoked potentials.

### Psychiatric manifestations [1, 2]
**Overview:**
- Neuropsychiatric symptoms are a **core part of the MS phenotype** [1].
- **Surridge (1969), 108 patients vs muscular dystrophy controls:**
  - psychiatric abnormality **75%**;
  - intellectual deterioration **61%**;
  - mood abnormality 53% (**depression 27%, euphoria 26%**, emotional exaggeration 10%);
  - **personality change 40%** (towards **irritability**);
  - denial of disability 11%; impaired awareness 31%;
  - psychosis rare.
- **Symptoms vary with disease activity:** 39% when stable vs **90% during relapse** (Dalos).
- **Interactional model (Ron & Feinstein):** brain damage creates vulnerability, which enhances the effect of **social stress**.
- **Causes** [2]: psychological impact of diagnosis; poor social support and coping; stress; **iatrogenic** (treatments); inflammation and pathway damage.

**1. Cognitive impairment (40–65%)** [1, 2]:
- **Progressive** forms worse than relapsing–remitting (**72% vs 46%**: Heaton).
- **Often missed:** half of "intact" patients are impaired on testing; verbal skills are preserved.
- **Profile (subcortical):**
  - **slowed information processing** (commonest); attention;
  - **memory and learning** (40–60%; working, semantic, episodic; digit span preserved);
  - abstraction, set-shifting, problem-solving (frontal-like); **verbal fluency**; perseveration.
  - Aphasia is rare.
- **Early:** present after clinically isolated syndromes and optic neuritis with MRI lesions.
- **Correlates:** lesion load, **grey-matter atrophy**.
- **Course:** about a quarter deteriorate; prevalence rises from **26% to 56% over 10 years**.
- **MACFIMS** battery (7 tests).
- **Dementia presentation:** "silent" MS presenting with personality change, apathy and bland indifference has been **mistaken for hysteria or psychiatric illness**; occasionally fulminant (death in weeks).

**2. Depression** [1, 2]:
- **Lifetime up to 50%**; point prevalence 14–31% [2].
- Similar to primary depression but **more irritability** than low self-esteem [1].
- **Mixed aetiology:** psychosocial factors plus lesions (**left medial inferior prefrontal**, dominant anterior temporal atrophy).
- Often with **fatigue and pain** [2].
- **Suicide 2–7.5 times** the general population; suicidality in over a fifth [2].

**3. Euphoria** (formerly considered typical) [1]:
- **cheerful complacency out of keeping with the situation**, without hypomanic overactivity;
- associated with **intellectual deterioration**, greater disability and **denial of disability**;
- may mask underlying misery (8 of 28 admitted feeling depressed).

**4. Pseudobulbar affect / involuntary emotional expression disorder** [1, 2]:
- up to **10%** [1]; about **25%** [2];
- expression without matching feeling, as in pseudobulbar palsy;
- more in advanced disease and with cognitive impairment.

**5. Bipolar disorder:** about twice the general population [1]; lifetime just under 10% [2]. **Steroid-induced** mania or psychosis [2].

**6. Anxiety:** point prevalence up to 50%; GAD, panic, OCD, social anxiety; **uncertain prognosis** is a major driver [2].

**7. Psychosis:**
- **more than twice** the general rate [1]; about **4.3%** [2];
- **expansive delusions**, with paraesthesiae incorporated into delusions;
- clusters around the onset of neurological symptoms; mean 8.5 years after neurological onset in Feinstein's series;
- **periventricular / temporal horn (left)** lesion load;
- **rarely the presenting feature**: missed MS diagnosed as schizophrenia; acute remitting psychosis or depression as the first episode (Matthews) [1].

**8. Conversion symptoms** were historically emphasised (Brain 1930) [1].

**9. Fatigue:** up to **80%** [2].

**10. Stress and relapse:** no clear group evidence that emotional stress triggers onset or relapses, though individual cases are suggestive [1].

### Management [1, 2]
**A. Disease-directed** [1] (2009 content; see the flag):
- relapses: **methylprednisolone** (IV or high-dose oral) speeds recovery;
- DMTs: interferon-β (delays conversion after a first episode), glatiramer, **natalizumab** (PML risk), mitoxantrone, azathioprine, plasma exchange in aggressive disease;
- **vitamin D** may protect against relapse.

**B. Psychiatric** [2]:

| Problem | Treatment |
| --- | --- |
| **Depression** (Table 10.12) | **1:** screen (PHQ-9, HADS, BDI, CES-D); exclude organic and **iatrogenic** causes; check for bipolarity; mild cases: CBT or self-help. **2:** **SSRIs first line** (sertraline ≈ CBT; paroxetine ≈ placebo in one trial; fluoxetine); **SNRI (duloxetine, venlafaxine) if pain**; bupropion, vortioxetine; **start at half dose**; watch for serotonin syndrome with low-dose TCAs used for pain or bladder. **CBT** is the best psychological treatment (combine with drugs if moderate–severe); mindfulness; omega-3 ineffective. **3:** moclobemide; mirtazapine (may worsen fatigue). **4:** **ECT** if suicidal or severe (may exacerbate MS) |
| **Anxiety** | SSRIs, venlafaxine, bupropion; benzodiazepines for **4 weeks or less**; buspirone, β-blockers, pregabalin; CBT |
| **Pseudobulbar affect** | Low-dose amitriptyline or **SSRIs** (fluoxetine, citalopram, sertraline); valproate; **dextromethorphan–quinidine** (FDA-approved) [2]; antidepressants or levodopa [1] |
| **Bipolar / mania** | Low-dose risperidone, olanzapine, ziprasidone; **lithium** limited by diuresis and bladder problems; olanzapine for steroid-induced mania with psychosis |
| **Psychosis** | **Risperidone or clozapine** recommended (low EPS); olanzapine, aripiprazole, quetiapine possible; ECT in refractory cases. **Steroids** if psychosis is a relapse presentation (supervised). Beware **THC**-containing products |
| **Cognitive impairment** | Treat sleep, depression, fatigue; review drugs (**tizanidine, diazepam, gabapentin** worsen cognition); **donepezil** (small positive trials, a larger negative one); memantine weak; **DMTs** more promising; cognitive rehabilitation |
| **Fatigue** | Address depression, sleep and medication; pacing, exercise, **CBT**; **amantadine** (NICE: small benefit; should be offered), **modafinil** (meta-analysis positive); vitamin D |

**C. Rehabilitation:** cognitive impairment strongly influences rehabilitation outcome [1]. Neurology–psychiatry collaboration [2].

### Update from Continuum 2016 [3]
**Diagnosis and course:**
- MS can be **diagnosed at the first attack** if MRI shows **dissemination in space and time**, which a **single scan** can show (chronic plus enhancing lesions).
- **Radiologically isolated syndrome (RIS, 2009):** typical MS-like MRI lesions in a person **without symptoms**, at risk of future events.
  - Subtle **cognitive change** and **depression (especially anxious depression) in about 50%** of an RIS population show that mood disturbance can **precede** clinical MS.
- **Course descriptors:**
  - relapsing vs progressive, each qualified as **active or not** and **progressing or not**, since activity and progression can coexist;
  - a "**topographical model**" unifies the course.
- **Relapse:** new or worsening symptoms lasting **more than 24 hours**, without fever or infection, after **30 days of stability**. **About 40% of relapses leave chronic disability.**
- **Pathology:** demyelination **plus axonal transection**; **cortical (grey matter) lesions**; a degenerative component throughout, more prominent later.

**Disease-modifying therapy (2016):**
- **Aim:** reduce relapses and new MRI lesions to slow disability. Many aim for "**no evidence of disease activity" (NEDA)**: no relapses, no new MRI lesions, no EDSS progression.
- **Treat early:** the **window of opportunity** opens after a clinically isolated syndrome and closes with progression. Treating CIS delays conversion. **Routine MRI** detects subclinical activity.
- **Injectables:** interferon-β (remote follow-up showed lower mortality), glatiramer acetate.
- **Orals:**
  - **fingolimod**: first-dose bradyarrhythmia, macular oedema, LFTs; varicella immunity first;
  - **teriflunomide**: **pregnancy category X**; men need contraception too;
  - **dimethyl fumarate**: flushing, GI upset, lymphopenia.
- **IV (efficacy over safety):**
  - **natalizumab** (PML about **4/1000**, stratified by duration, prior immunosuppression and **JC virus antibodies**);
  - **alemtuzumab** (infusion reactions, **about 30% secondary autoimmunity, mostly thyroid**, possible malignancy);
  - mitoxantrone (cardiotoxicity, leukaemia; rarely used).
- **PML is no longer confined to natalizumab**: stay vigilant across therapies.
- **Aggressive MS:** induction (cyclophosphamide), **rituximab or ocrelizumab** (anti-CD20; ocrelizumab positive in relapsing and **primary progressive** MS), **autologous haematopoietic stem cell transplantation** in expert centres.
- **Poor adherence** is a common cause of breakthrough disease. **Assess compliance at every visit.**

**Relapse treatment:**
- **IV methylprednisolone 1 g/day for 3–5 days** (high-dose oral methylprednisolone is an alternative); ACTH if steroids fail or are not tolerated.
- **Psychiatric relevance:** a case of **steroid-induced psychotic reaction with strong suicidal ideation** after IV methylprednisolone [3, Case 6-1].

**Psychiatric and cognitive symptoms (symptom management article):**
- **Major depressive disorder 36–54%** (vs **16.2%** of controls); **suicide at least twice as common**; **anxiety disorder 35.7%** (vs 28.8%).
- **Causes of depression:**
  - reactive;
  - **structural injury** in specific brain regions;
  - **immune and endocrine change with HPA-axis disruption**;
  - **drug side effects**;
  - comorbid **alcohol** use;
  - **pain** clusters with depression.
  - Depression may present as **hypersomnia** (Case 7-2).
- **Screening** with patient-reported outcome tools: **Beck Depression Inventory**, **two-question screen**, **HADS**, **GAD-7**, and the **Center for Neurologic Study–Lability Scale** for **pseudobulbar affect**.
- **Pseudobulbar affect about 10%** in progressive MS; cognitive dysfunction 60–70%; fatigue 80%.
- **Treatment of depression and anxiety:** drugs, psychotherapy, web-based programmes, exercise, and **ECT, DBS or TMS**. After a failed SSRI, switch class (e.g., an SNRI), and add couples counselling where relevant (Case 7-2).
- **Cognition:**
  - deficits in **40–70%** over time (processing speed, working, verbal and visual memory, executive function); **up to 20% reach mild dementia**; most notable in secondary progressive MS but detectable even in RIS;
  - linked to **cortical plaques and grey matter loss**, not white-matter lesion load; **cognitive reserve** protects;
  - cognitive complaints are **subjective about 50% of the time** (depression, anxiety, poor sleep);
  - screen with **SDMT/PASAT**; full neuropsychology is the gold standard;
  - **treatment:** remove sedating or anticholinergic drugs, treat mood, sleep, pain and fatigue, **cognitive rehabilitation**, exercise or yoga, an enriched lifestyle. Stimulants, AChE-Is and memantine are used, but a **Cochrane review found no convincing evidence** for any drug. **Effective DMT is preventive.**
- **Fatigue:**
  - primary fatigue is **worse with heat** and in the **early afternoon**; constant in 30%;
  - **amantadine 100 mg twice daily**, **modafinil 200–400 mg/day**, armodafinil, stimulants, cooling, exercise, CBT or mindfulness.
- **Pain:**
  - chronic central neuropathic pain in **30–40%**: TCAs, **duloxetine**, AEDs, cannabinoids, TENS, mindfulness;
  - **trigeminal neuralgia**: **carbamazepine** (Level A).
- **Wellness:**
  - exercise; **stop smoking** (smoking drives progression, cognitive impairment and mortality);
  - treat obesity; vitamin D; limit salt;
  - sleep; mental activity (better cognition, less atrophy).

**Differential diagnosis: NMOSD:**
- severe or **bilateral optic neuritis**, **longitudinally extensive myelitis (3 or more segments)**, **area postrema syndrome** (intractable hiccups or vomiting), diencephalic or cerebral syndromes;
- **serum aquaporin-4 IgG** (cell-based assay);
- oligoclonal bands in only about 25% (vs up to 90% in MS);
- treat attacks with IV methylprednisolone and plasma exchange;
- **urgent prevention**: rituximab often preferred, azathioprine (check TPMT), mycophenolate (teratogenic).

**Conclusion.** MS is an immune-mediated demyelinating disease of young adults with a relapsing–remitting then progressive course, optic neuritis, INO, long-tract and cerebellar signs, periventricular MRI lesions, CSF oligoclonal bands and delayed VEPs. Psychiatric morbidity is core:
- **cognitive impairment** (40–65%; slowed processing, memory, executive; may be the sole presentation);
- **depression** (lifetime up to 50%; suicide 2–7.5×);
- **euphoria** (with cognitive decline and denial), **pseudobulbar affect**;
- **anxiety**, **bipolar disorder** (about 2×), and psychosis (about 2×, around 4%; occasionally presenting).

Management combines disease-modifying treatment and steroids for relapses with cautious, half-dose psychotropics (SSRIs, SNRIs for pain; risperidone or clozapine for psychosis; dextromethorphan–quinidine or SSRIs for PBA), CBT, fatigue management (amantadine, modafinil, CBT) and cognitive rehabilitation.
