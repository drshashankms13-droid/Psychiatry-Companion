## Exam answer

**Definition.** Catatonia is a psychomotor syndrome in which reactivity to the environment is markedly reduced or motor activity becomes excessive and peculiar. DSM-5 requires **at least 3 of 12 signs**: stupor, catalepsy, waxy flexibility, mutism, negativism, posturing, mannerism, stereotypy, agitation, grimacing, echolalia and echopraxia [1]. It is a **medical emergency** because untreated stupor rapidly leads to dehydration, venous thromboembolism, aspiration pneumonia and death [2], yet it is one of the most treatable syndromes in psychiatry.

### Classification and frequency

- **DSM-5:** catatonia associated with another mental disorder; catatonic disorder due to another medical condition; unspecified catatonia (useful in the emergency department when the cause is not yet known) [1]. **ICD-11** also lists catatonia as its own grouping, no longer only a subtype of schizophrenia.
- **Subtypes:** **retarded/stuporous**, **excited**, and **malignant** (catatonia with autonomic instability — fever, labile BP, tachycardia, tachypnoea) [2].
- Pooled prevalence about **9%** across psychiatric and medical samples (74 studies, 107,304 people) [2]; more common in **mood disorders** (13–31% of inpatients) than in acute psychosis (7–17%) [1].

### Mechanism

Reduced GABA-A activity and NMDA/glutamate dysfunction in frontal–basal ganglia motor circuits → "motor programs run without frontal direction" [1]. This explains the dramatic response to benzodiazepines (GABA-A agonists) and ECT, and the link with anti-NMDAR encephalitis [1].

### Emergency assessment — four questions

1. **Is it catatonia?** Examine actively: observe → speak → test for catalepsy, waxy flexibility, negativism, gegenhalten, echo-phenomena, grasp reflex. Use the **Bush–Francis Catatonia Rating Scale** (first 14 items screen; 2 or more positive = screen positive).
2. **Is it malignant catatonia or NMS?** Check **temperature, pulse, BP, respiratory rate, sweating, rigidity, consciousness**, and recent antipsychotic exposure [2].
3. **What is the cause?** Psychiatric (bipolar, depression, schizophrenia, postpartum, autism) or medical — encephalitis (including **anti-NMDAR**), non-convulsive status epilepticus, metabolic/endocrine, SLE, CNS infection, **drug withdrawal** (benzodiazepines, clozapine, zolpidem) [2].
4. **What complications are present?** Dehydration, acute kidney injury, rhabdomyolysis, DVT/PE, aspiration, pressure sores, malnutrition.

**Investigations:** CBC, electrolytes, urea/creatinine, glucose, calcium, LFT, **CK**, iron, TSH, urine drug screen, ECG, D-dimer if suspected thrombosis; then as indicated — **EEG** (exclude non-convulsive status), **MRI brain**, **lumbar puncture with anti-NMDAR antibodies**, ANA, HIV, VDRL.

### Management algorithm [2]

Exclude or treat underlying physical illness
→ **Lorazepam challenge:** 2 mg (IV, IM or sublingual); repeat 2 mg after about 3 hours if no effect
→ continue **lorazepam up to 4 mg/day**, then increase to **8–24 mg/day** in divided doses if needed (titrate to response, not sedation)
→ **no response in 1–2 days** (or life-threatening state at any point) → **ECT**, often daily or bilateral at first
→ treat the underlying disorder once catatonia lifts.

**Antipsychotics:** avoid while NMS is possible and in malignant catatonia; high-potency FGAs can **worsen** catatonia and precipitate NMS [1, 2]. If NMS is excluded and catatonia followed **stopping antipsychotics (especially clozapine)**, restart the previous antipsychotic early with a benzodiazepine [2].

### Supportive care (all patients)

- Nil by mouth until swallowing is safe; IV fluids; nasogastric feeding if prolonged.
- **DVT prophylaxis** (low-molecular-weight heparin, stockings), frequent turning, eye and mouth care, bladder monitoring.
- Monitor vital signs **4-hourly** (hourly if malignant); daily CK and renal function.
- Transfer to **medical/ICU care** if autonomic instability, high fever, rhabdomyolysis or inability to maintain intake.

### Response rates

- Benzodiazepines: most patients with mood-disorder or medical catatonia respond rapidly (commonly quoted as 70–80%); response is **lower in schizophrenia (40–50%)** [2].
- ECT: the most successful treatment for catatonia, including malignant catatonia, NMS-overlap, delirious mania, autism and limbic encephalitis [2].

### Key evidence

- **Solmi et al., 2018:** meta-analysis, pooled catatonia prevalence about 9% [2].
- **BAP consensus guideline (Rogers et al., 2023):** benzodiazepines first line; ECT when they fail or when life is at risk [2].
- Lorazepam–diazepam protocol: response in 10 of 12 mood-disorder patients and 18 of 21 with medical or substance-related catatonia [2].

**Conclusion.** In an emergency, recognise catatonia actively, exclude malignant catatonia/NMS and medical causes, start a lorazepam challenge immediately, and move to ECT without delay if benzodiazepines fail in 1–2 days or if life is threatened. Avoid antipsychotics until NMS is excluded, and never let supportive physical care lapse.

## In depth

### Background

- **Karl Kahlbaum (1874)** described catatonia as a distinct disorder with motor, behavioural and psychiatric features and a largely benign course. **Kraepelin** made it a subtype of dementia praecox, a view that lasted through DSM-IV-TR and ICD-10 [1].
- DSM-III-R added a "catatonic features" specifier for mood disorders; DSM-IV added catatonic disorder due to a general medical condition; **DSM-5 (2013)** created a separate catatonia rubric usable across diagnoses [1].
- Until the 1930s, mental hospitals held many stuporous patients fed by stomach tube; the arrival of convulsive therapies and antipsychotics made chronic stupor rare in high-income countries [1].
- Catatonia remains more common in low- and middle-income countries, possibly because of untreated illness and less continuous antipsychotic exposure [1].

### Epidemiology

- Lifetime prevalence in schizophrenia about **8%**; DSM-5 suggests up to **35%** in inpatient settings [1].
- Equal gender distribution in schizophrenia, unlike most severity markers; associated with increased suicide risk [1].
- Catatonia also occurs in children and adolescents, autism spectrum disorder, the peripartum period and PTSD [1, 2].

### Clinical features in detail

| Sign | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Stupor | No psychomotor activity; not actively relating to the environment |
| Catalepsy | Passive induction of a posture held against gravity |
| Waxy flexibility | Slight, even resistance to positioning by the examiner |
| Mutism | No or very little verbal response (exclude aphasia) |
| Negativism | Opposition or no response to instructions or stimuli |
| Posturing | Spontaneous, active maintenance of a posture against gravity |
| Mannerism | Odd caricature of normal actions |
| Stereotypy | Repetitive, abnormally frequent, non-goal-directed movements |
| Agitation | Not influenced by external stimuli |
| Grimacing | — |
| Echolalia / echopraxia | Mimicking another's speech / movements |

Waxy flexibility and catalepsy were once used as synonyms; DSM-5 distinguishes them [1]. Patients often have little recall afterwards, or describe being aware but unable to respond [1]. Severe excitement can cause exhaustion, hyperthermia and injury to self or others [1].

**Examination sequence (Bush–Francis):** observe for 1 minute → engage in conversation → scratch head exaggeratedly (echopraxia) → move arm with variable force (gegenhalten, waxy flexibility) → ask to extend arm, place a finger on the hand (ambitendency) → extend hand without instruction ("don't shake my hand") → check grasp reflex → check for autonomic abnormalities. (Author's summary of the standard instrument.)

### Causes [2]

| Group | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| Psychiatric | Bipolar disorder (commonest), major depression, schizophrenia, postpartum psychosis, PTSD, autism spectrum disorder, conversion disorder |
| Neurological | Non-convulsive status epilepticus, encephalitis (anti-NMDAR, herpes), basal ganglia disorders, subarachnoid haemorrhage, akinetic mutism, dementia |
| Medical | Endocrine/metabolic (thyroid disease, hypercalcaemia, uraemia, Wilson's), SLE, antiphospholipid syndrome, Prader–Willi syndrome, CNS infections, folate deficiency, paraneoplastic disorders [1, 2] |
| Drugs | Withdrawal of benzodiazepines, clozapine, zolpidem; antipsychotic-induced catatonia; some oncology drugs |

### Differential diagnosis

| Condition | Distinguishing feature |
| --- | --- |
| **NMS** | Recent dopamine antagonist; generalised lead-pipe rigidity; marked CK rise; more severe autonomic instability [1, 2] |
| **Malignant catatonia** | Catatonic signs precede fever and autonomic change; may occur without antipsychotics; clinically and biochemically may be indistinguishable from NMS [2] |
| **Serotonin syndrome** | Serotonergic drugs; clonus, hyperreflexia, mydriasis, diarrhoea |
| **Delirium** | Fluctuating attention and consciousness; catatonic signs here do not justify a separate diagnosis [1] |
| **Non-convulsive status** | Subtle twitching, eye deviation; EEG diagnostic |
| **Locked-in syndrome** | Vertical eye movements preserved and used to communicate; brainstem lesion |
| **Akinetic mutism** | Frontal or diencephalic lesion; no negativism or posturing |
| **Acute dystonia / parkinsonism** | Recent antipsychotic; responds to anticholinergic [1] |
| **Stiff-person syndrome, malignant hyperthermia** | Anaesthetic exposure; GAD antibodies |
| **Dissociative stupor** | Eye avoidance, resistance to eye opening; psychological trigger [2] |

### Management details

- **Why lorazepam:** most clinical experience; rapid onset; sublingual tablets are tasteless and useful when the patient will not swallow; IV diazepam or lorazepam can also be used [2].
- **Predicting response:** a positive lorazepam challenge (improvement within minutes to hours) predicts benzodiazepine response; a **zolpidem 10 mg** test dose may also predict response, and frequent zolpidem dosing has been used as treatment [2].
- **Schizophrenia:** benzodiazepine response is lower (40–50%); an RCT of lorazepam up to 6 mg/day showed no benefit for **chronic** catatonic signs; a Cochrane review found only one small trial (17 participants) [2]. Move to ECT sooner.
- **ECT technique:** in malignant catatonia use liberal stimulus dosing to obtain well-generalised seizures; bilateral placement and daily sessions are often used at first. Benzodiazepines raise seizure threshold — reduce dose before sessions or use flumazenil at induction (author's practice note) [2].
- **Antipsychotics after resolution:** if a psychotic disorder is confirmed, start a low-potency SGA cautiously once catatonia resolves (clozapine, olanzapine, aripiprazole and risperidone have been used). The NMS risk is much higher in catatonic than non-catatonic schizophrenia [2].
- **Other drugs (experimental):** amantadine, memantine, carbamazepine, valproate, lithium, fluoxetine, mirtazapine, methylphenidate, zolpidem [2].
- **Neuromodulation:** rTMS and tDCS are promising but lack high-quality RCTs (2024 systematic review) [2].
- **Special groups:** postpartum catatonia — check for postpartum psychosis and Sheehan's syndrome, ECT is highly effective; autism — catatonia often presents as slowing, freezing or loss of skills.

### Evidence table

| Study | Design | Finding |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Solmi et al., 2018 | Meta-analysis, 74 studies, n = 107,304 | Pooled prevalence about 9% [2] |
| Huang et al., 2013 | Observational, n = 12 (mood disorders) | IM lorazepam 2–4 mg: 10 of 12 responded [2] |
| Lin et al. (lorazepam–diazepam protocol, liaison psychiatry) | Observational, n = 21 | 18 of 21 with medical or substance-related catatonia responded [2] |
| Ungvari et al. | Double-blind placebo-controlled crossover RCT, chronic catatonic schizophrenia | Lorazepam up to 6 mg/day had no effect [2] |
| Dutt et al., 2011 (north India) | Descriptive, non-randomised | Poor lorazepam response in catatonic schizophrenia; ECT effective [2] |
| Rogers et al., 2023 (BAP) | Consensus guideline | Benzodiazepines first line, ECT second line or first line if life-threatening [2] |
| 2024 systematic review | Non-invasive brain stimulation | ECT effective; rTMS/tDCS promising [2] |

### Critical appraisal

- **Under-recognition:** catatonia is missed when clinicians do not examine for it; many signs are only elicited by active examination.
- **Weak trial base:** treatment recommendations rest mostly on case series and expert consensus; there are almost no RCTs, partly because withholding effective treatment is unethical.
- **NMS vs malignant catatonia** may be the same process; some authors regard NMS as drug-induced malignant catatonia. The practical rule — stop antipsychotics, give benzodiazepines, use ECT early — is the same for both [2].
- **Antipsychotic controversy:** some advise avoiding antipsychotics altogether; others report success with SGAs, especially in catatonic schizophrenia or after clozapine withdrawal [2].

### Indian context

- Catatonia is common in Indian inpatient and emergency settings, often presenting late with dehydration and medical complications. A descriptive study from north India (Dutt et al., 2011) is among the studies cited by Maudsley [2].
- **ECT under MHCA 2017:** ECT needs **informed consent** (from the patient, or the nominated representative if the patient lacks capacity under supported admission); **unmodified ECT is prohibited** (Section 95); **ECT is not permitted as emergency treatment** under Section 94; ECT in minors needs guardian consent and prior Mental Health Review Board permission (Section 95).
- Families may first consult faith healers for "possession" or a "trance"; psychoeducation about the treatable nature of catatonia helps consent to ECT.
- Lorazepam injection is widely available and inexpensive; ICU access varies, so early transfer planning matters.

### Recent advances

- **BAP consensus guidelines (2023)** — the first comprehensive evidence-based guideline for catatonia [2].
- Recognition of **immune causes** (anti-NMDAR and other autoimmune encephalitides) and of **withdrawal catatonia** after clozapine and benzodiazepines [2].
- Neuroimaging studies implicate orbitofrontal–motor cortex and GABA-A receptor abnormalities (Walther et al., 2019) [2].
- rTMS and tDCS under study as alternatives to ECT [2].
