## Exam answer

**Definition.** A **candidate gene** is a gene chosen for study because its known biological function (for example, dopamine or glutamate signalling) or its location in a linkage region makes it a plausible cause of a disorder. In schizophrenia, the candidate-gene era (1990s–2000s) produced many reported associations — *COMT, NRG1, DISC1, DTNBP1, DAOA, RGS4, BDNF* — but few replicated. Genome-wide association studies (GWAS), copy number variant (CNV) studies and exome sequencing have since replaced hypothesis-driven candidate selection with hypothesis-free, genome-wide searches [1, 2].

### Heritability — the starting point

| Study type | Key finding |
| --- | --- |
| Family studies | Lifetime risk in siblings **about 7–10%**, offspring **about 13–15%**, parents **about 2–6%**; siblings carry an **8–10-fold** increased risk — greater than any environmental factor [1] |
| Both parents affected | Risk in offspring **27%** (39% including related disorders; Danish register, 2010) [1] |
| Twin studies | Concordance **MZ about 47–56%, DZ about 12–16%** [3]; heritability **about 80%** (meta-analyses 81% and 88%) [1] |
| Adoption studies | Children of affected parents adopted away at birth still have **at least 10%** risk — familial transmission is mainly genetic [1] |

MZ concordance well below 100% shows that environment also matters [1].

### Classical candidate genes

| Gene | Location | Function / rationale | Status |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **COMT** (catechol-O-methyltransferase) | 22q11 | Degrades prefrontal dopamine; **Val158Met** polymorphism alters prefrontal efficiency during working memory | Affects cognition and brain activation; association with schizophrenia itself not established [1] |
| **DISC1** (disrupted in schizophrenia 1) | 1q42 | Neurodevelopment, neuronal migration; balanced translocation in a Scottish family with schizophrenia *and* mood disorders | Suggests a mixed psychosis–mood phenotype [3] |
| **NRG1** (neuregulin 1) | 8p | Synaptic plasticity, glutamate/NMDA signalling, myelination | Linked to schizophrenia and to bipolar disorder with psychotic features [3] |
| **DTNBP1** (dysbindin) | 6p | Glutamate release, synaptic function | Also associated with psychotic mania [3] |
| **DAOA (G72/G30)** | 13q | Regulates D-amino acid oxidase → D-serine → NMDA receptor | More strongly linked to mood symptoms than psychosis [3] |
| **RGS4, BDNF** | 1q, 11p | G-protein signalling; neurotrophic support | Inconsistent [3] |
| **GRM3** | 7q | Metabotropic glutamate receptor; inefficient prefrontal activation | Supported by later GWAS [1, 2] |
| **DRD2** | 11q | Target of all current antipsychotics | Confirmed by GWAS [1] |

### Why most candidate-gene findings failed

Small samples → small true effect sizes (odds ratios about 1.1) → many false positives and publication bias → poor replication. GWAS showed that a genome-wide significance threshold of **p < 5 × 10⁻⁸** and **tens of thousands** of cases are needed [1].

### Modern genetic architecture

1. **Common variants (GWAS):** the Psychiatric Genomics Consortium (2014; 36,989 cases, 113,075 controls) found **108 loci**, including **DRD2**, glutamate genes (**GRM3, GRIN2A, SRR, GRIA1**), calcium channels (**CACNA1C, CACNB2, CACNA1I**) and the **MHC** region [1]. Most act by altering gene expression, not protein structure [1]. Common variants explain about **a third** of genetic liability; **polygenic risk scores** summarise them [1].
2. **Rare copy number variants (CNVs):** about 12 CNVs at 11 loci are confirmed, each in **less than 1%** of patients but with large effects — **22q11.2 deletion** (velocardiofacial/DiGeorge syndrome; up to **30%** develop psychosis), 3q29 deletion, 1q21.1, 15q13.3, 16p11.2 duplication and **NRXN1** deletions [1]. They also cause intellectual disability, autism and congenital anomalies.
3. **Rare coding variants (exome sequencing):** an excess of rare disruptive and de novo mutations in synaptic genes [1].

### Convergence on biology

All three approaches point to **synaptic plasticity**: the **NMDA receptor and ARC complexes**, targets of the fragile X protein (**FMRP**), **voltage-gated calcium channels**, and **immune** (MHC, B-lymphocyte) pathways — plus dopamine (DRD2) [1, 3].

### Clinical relevance

- **Pleiotropy:** the same variants raise risk of bipolar disorder, autism and intellectual disability; genes do not respect DSM/ICD categories [1, 3].
- **Genetic counselling:** useful only for carriers of the known CNVs (about **2.5%** of patients); polygenic scores have **no useful clinical predictive value** at present [1].
- **Who to test:** chromosomal microarray in schizophrenia with intellectual disability, dysmorphic features, congenital heart disease, hypocalcaemia or very early onset — mainly to detect 22q11.2 deletion.
- Genes increase **susceptibility**, not inevitability; gene–environment interaction (cannabis, urbanicity, obstetric complications) is being studied in large consortia such as **EU-GEI** [2].

**Conclusion.** Schizophrenia is highly heritable (about 80%) but polygenic. The classical candidate genes (COMT, DISC1, NRG1, dysbindin) were biologically plausible yet largely failed replication, while GWAS, CNV and sequencing studies have identified hundreds of common loci and a handful of rare high-risk CNVs converging on synaptic, glutamatergic, calcium-channel, dopamine and immune pathways. Clinically, genetics is useful for CNV counselling and for pointing to new drug targets, not yet for prediction or diagnosis.

## In depth

### Background

- Kraepelin (1899) and later clinicians noted that dementia praecox ran in families. **Ernst Rüdin (1916)** published the first systematic family study [1].
- **Gottesman and Shields** compiled the classic morbidity-risk figures (1982) and described MZ twins reared apart: 9 of 14 pairs concordant (64%) [1].
- Linkage studies in the 1990s identified many regions (1q32, 10p, 13q32, 18p11.2, 22q11–13, 1q42), several shared with bipolar disorder, but findings were inconsistent [3].
- The candidate-gene era peaked in the 2000s. The turning point was the **PGC 2014 Nature paper** ("108 loci"), which showed that only very large collaborative samples give reliable results [1].

### Epidemiology of familial risk in detail

- Older family studies (Gottesman) gave higher risks than the more rigorous 1989–1995 studies (1.4–6.5% in first-degree relatives vs 0–1.9% in controls). In pooled modern data: parents 2.3% (17 of 725), siblings 7.4% (48 of 645), offspring 15% (37 of 246) [1].
- Parents have lower risk than siblings or offspring probably because illness reduces the chance of having children, and stricter criteria exclude parents with milder spectrum disorders (for example, schizotypal personality) [1].
- **Discordant MZ twins:** offspring of the *unaffected* co-twin had the same risk (17.4%) as offspring of the affected twin (16.8%) in one study — showing that the unaffected twin carries the genetic liability without expressing it [1].
- **Adoption studies:** Heston (1966): 5 of 47 adopted-away children of mothers with schizophrenia developed schizophrenia vs 0 of 50 controls; Kety's Danish studies and Tienari's Finnish study (10.4% vs 2%) confirm genetic transmission; Lichtenstein (Sweden, 2009) found a relative risk of 13.7 for adopted-away children of affected parents [1].
- Heritability of about 80% means that 80% of the **variation in liability in the population** is due to genetic variation — not that 80% of each person's illness is genetic.

### Concepts and methods

| Method | What it detects | Strength | Limitation |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Linkage | Chromosome regions co-segregating with illness in families | Good for single genes of large effect | Weak for complex disorders |
| Candidate-gene association | Variants in pre-selected genes | Cheap, hypothesis-driven | Underpowered; selection bias; poor replication |
| GWAS | Common SNPs across the genome | Hypothesis-free; replicable | Needs tens of thousands; small effects; identifies loci, not causal genes |
| CNV analysis | Deletions/duplications ≥1 kb | Large effects; clinically actionable | Very rare individually |
| Exome/genome sequencing | Rare and de novo coding variants | Finds causal genes | Expensive; needs huge samples |
| Polygenic risk score | Summed effect of many common alleles | Research stratification | Low individual predictive value |

**Twin concordance methods:** pairwise concordance = concordant pairs ÷ all pairs; probandwise concordance counts doubly ascertained pairs twice and is about a quarter higher. Probandwise rates are preferred because they are comparable to population risk [1].

### Mechanism in detail

- **COMT Val158Met:** the Val allele degrades dopamine faster → lower prefrontal dopamine → less efficient prefrontal activation during working memory; Met homozygotes show the reverse. GRM3 risk variants show a similar inefficiency and interact epistatically with COMT — the GRM3 effect is strongest on a COMT Val/Val background [1]. A later meta-analysis did not confirm a COMT effect on activation, highlighting small-sample problems [1].
- **22q11.2 deletion** removes about 2.5 Mb including *COMT* and *PRODH*; carriers have up to 30% risk of psychosis, linking a candidate gene with a high-risk CNV [1].
- **NRXN1** (neurexin 1) is the only schizophrenia CNV involving a single gene; exon-disrupting deletions raise risk. Neurexin is a presynaptic adhesion molecule that organises synapses and transmitter release [1].
- **De novo CNVs** occur in about **5%** of patients (at least twice the control rate); genes within them cluster in the **postsynaptic density**, especially NMDA receptor and ARC complexes involved in learning and memory [1].
- **Exome sequencing** of 2,536 cases and 2,543 controls showed an excess of rare disruptive variants in schizophrenia-associated genes, voltage-gated calcium channels, the ARC complex and FMRP targets [1].
- **Immune pathways:** GWAS associations are enriched in genes expressed in immune tissues (B-lymphocyte lineages), supporting an immune contribution [3]. The strongest GWAS signal lies in the MHC region.

### Schizophrenia-associated CNVs [1]

| CNV | Frequency in cases | Odds ratio (approx.) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 22q11.2 deletion | 0.29% | Very high (no controls carried it) |
| 3q29 deletion | 0.08% | about 58 |
| 16p11.2 duplication | 0.35% | about 12 |
| Williams–Beuren duplication | 0.07% | about 11 |
| NRXN1 exonic deletion | 0.18% | about 9 |
| 1q21.1 deletion | 0.17% | about 8 |
| 15q13.3 deletion | 0.14% | about 8 |
| 15q11.2 deletion | 0.59% | about 2 |

These CNVs are flanked by low-copy repeats, so they arise repeatedly by misalignment during meiosis. Their penetrance for **any** serious neurodevelopmental disorder averages just over **40%**, mostly non-psychotic disorders (developmental delay, autism, congenital malformations) [1].

### Pleiotropy and the psychosis continuum

- Relatives of people with schizophrenia have increased rates of schizoaffective disorder, bipolar disorder, psychotic depression and other psychoses; in a Finnish isolate, 16% of siblings had a lifetime psychotic disorder [3].
- Linkage at 1q42 (DISC1) and 22q11 (COMT) in schizoaffective pedigrees came equally from schizophrenia-predominant and bipolar-predominant families [3].
- Lichtenstein's Swedish family study (2009) showed shared genetic determinants of schizophrenia and bipolar disorder [1].
- These findings support **dimensional** models of psychosis and question whether DSM/ICD categories are the right phenotypes for genetics [1, 3].

### Critical appraisal

- **Candidate-gene studies were mostly false positives:** small samples, flexible analyses and publication bias inflated effects; large GWAS have not supported most classic candidates (COMT, NRG1, DISC1, dysbindin) as common risk variants.
- **"Missing heritability":** common variants explain only part of the twin heritability; rare variants, gene–gene and gene–environment interactions, and overestimated twin heritability may account for the rest.
- **European bias:** almost all discovery samples are of European ancestry; polygenic scores perform worse in other populations, including Indian populations.
- **Ethical risks:** direct-to-consumer polygenic testing could mislead families and increase stigma; genetic results do not determine destiny [1].
- **What genetics has achieved:** it has robustly implicated synaptic, glutamatergic, calcium-channel and immune biology, and has identified CNVs that matter for counselling.

### Indian context

- Indian family and twin studies broadly agree with Western data on familial aggregation; large-scale Indian GWAS samples remain small compared with European consortia, so locus discovery and polygenic scores are less applicable to Indian patients.
- Consanguinity in some communities may increase the contribution of rare recessive variants — an under-researched area.
- Chromosomal microarray is available at tertiary centres, including NIMHANS; families of patients with 22q11.2 deletion need multidisciplinary care (cardiac, endocrine, psychiatric).
- **Genetic counselling** questions are common in India (marriage, childbearing). Clinicians can give empirical risks (siblings about 10%, offspring about 13–15%, both parents affected about 27–40%) and should emphasise that most relatives remain well [1].

### Recent advances

> These developments post-date the uploaded textbook (2017) and come from the author's knowledge; check before marking `reviewed`.

- **C4 (complement component 4) and synaptic pruning (Sekar et al., 2016):** variation in *C4A* expression explains much of the MHC signal and links schizophrenia to excessive complement-mediated synaptic pruning in adolescence.
- **PGC wave 3 (Trubetskoy et al., 2022):** 76,755 cases and 243,649 controls; **287 loci**; associations concentrated in genes expressed in neurons and synapses; fine-mapping prioritised about 120 genes.
- **SCHEMA exome study (Singh et al., 2022):** ultra-rare protein-truncating variants in **10 genes** (including *SETD1A*, *GRIN2A*, *CUL1*, *XPO7*) confer large risk (odds ratios about 3–50).
- **Polygenic risk scores** explain about 7% of variance in liability in European samples; research use only.
- Genetics is being used to prioritise new drug targets (glutamate, calcium-channel and complement pathways).
