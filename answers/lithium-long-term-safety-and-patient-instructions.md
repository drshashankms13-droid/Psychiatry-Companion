## Exam answer

**Definition.** Lithium carbonate is a monovalent cation mood stabiliser with a **narrow therapeutic index**: therapeutic range 0.6–0.8 mmol/L for maintenance (0.8–1.0 for acute mania), toxicity reliably above 1.5 mmol/L. It is excreted almost entirely by the kidney, in competition with sodium. Long-term safety depends on its renal, thyroid, parathyroid, cardiac, neurological and teratogenic effects, and on the patient's understanding of how to take it safely [1].

### Mechanism of adverse effects
- **Kidney:** lithium enters collecting-duct cells via ENaC → inhibits GSK-3 and ADH-stimulated aquaporin-2 → **nephrogenic diabetes insipidus** (polyuria, polydipsia) → chronic tubulointerstitial nephropathy and falling eGFR with long exposure [1].
- **Thyroid:** inhibits iodine uptake and T4/T3 release → hypothyroidism, goitre [1].
- **Parathyroid:** shifts the calcium set-point → hyperparathyroidism, hypercalcaemia [1].
- **Sodium handling:** low sodium or dehydration → proximal tubule reabsorbs more lithium → toxicity [1].

### Long-term adverse effects

| System | Effect | Frequency / notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Renal | NDI (polyuria >3 L/day) | Common (up to 20–40%); usually reversible early [1] |
| | Reduced eGFR / CKD | Risk rises with levels >0.8 mmol/L, duration >15 years, hypertension, toxicity episodes [1] |
| Thyroid | Hypothyroidism | Up to 20% of middle-aged women; easily treated with thyroxine [1] |
| | Goitre; rarely hyperthyroidism | [1] |
| Parathyroid | Hypercalcaemia (hyperparathyroidism) | ~4%; renal stones, osteoporosis, dyspepsia, hypertension [1] |
| Neurological | Fine tremor; cognitive dulling; neurotoxicity even at normal levels; rarely SILENT (persistent cerebellar damage) | [1] |
| Weight / metabolic | Weight gain, oedema | — |
| Skin | Acne, psoriasis worsening | [1] |
| Cardiac | T-wave flattening; sinus node dysfunction; Brugada unmasking (rare) | ECG if cardiac risk [1] |
| GI | Nausea, diarrhoea, metallic taste | Dose-related [1] |
| Haematological | Benign leucocytosis (used to raise neutrophils on clozapine) | [1] |
| Pregnancy | Ebstein's anomaly (absolute risk ~1 in 1,000–2,000); neonatal toxicity, floppy baby, neonatal hypothyroidism | Plan pregnancies [1] |
| Benefit side | ~80% reduction in suicide; possible neuroprotection and lower dementia risk | [1] |

### Toxicity
- **>1.5 mmol/L:** anorexia, nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea, coarse tremor, ataxia, dysarthria, drowsiness, confusion, muscle twitching.
- **>2.0 mmol/L:** disorientation, seizures, coma.
- **>3.0 mmol/L:** death; haemodialysis often needed [1].

### Monitoring

| When | Test |
| --- | --- |
| Before starting | eGFR, U&Es, calcium, TFTs (± thyroid antibodies in women), weight, ECG if cardiac risk, pregnancy test [1] |
| Titration | Level 12 hours post-dose after 7 days and 7 days after each dose change [1] |
| Maintenance | Level, eGFR, U&Es, TFTs, calcium, weight **every 6 months**; every 3 months if elderly, CKD, interacting drugs or first year [1] |

### Instructions to patients (counselling checklist)
1. **Why:** lithium prevents mood episodes and suicide. It works best taken continuously for years. **Do not stop suddenly**: this can trigger mania, even after years of stability [1].
2. **How:** take at the same time daily (usually once at night with prolonged-release tablets). Do not double a missed dose. Stick to the same brand and preparation; liquid strengths differ [1].
3. **Blood tests:** level 12 hours after the last dose (morning, before the dose). Keep all appointments for level, kidney and thyroid tests. Carry a **lithium record book/card** [1].
4. **Fluids and salt:** drink 2–3 litres a day, more in hot weather, exercise, fever. Do not suddenly change salt intake or start a low-salt diet [1].
5. **Situations that raise levels:** vomiting, diarrhoea, fever, heavy sweating, dehydration, fasting. Seek advice and consider withholding a dose. Get a level checked [1].
6. **Interacting drugs:** avoid NSAIDs (ibuprofen, diclofenac). Tell every doctor you take lithium before starting ACE inhibitors, ARBs, diuretics (especially thiazides) or metronidazole. Paracetamol is safe for pain [1].
7. **Warning signs of toxicity:** vomiting, diarrhoea, coarse shaking, unsteadiness, slurred speech, confusion, drowsiness. **Stop lithium and attend hospital urgently** [1].
8. **Side effects to report:** excessive thirst or urination, weight gain, tiredness or feeling cold (thyroid), skin problems [1].
9. **Pregnancy:** use reliable contraception. Plan pregnancy with the psychiatrist; do not stop abruptly if pregnant; consult urgently [1].
10. **Alcohol:** limit; avoid binge drinking (dehydration).
11. **Surgery:** inform the anaesthetist; lithium is usually withheld 24–72 hours before major surgery and restarted when eating and drinking.
12. **Driving and work:** usually fine once stable; caution with tremor or sedation.

### Key evidence
- **Lithium and renal function:** CKD risk is higher with long duration and toxicity episodes. Most patients keep adequate function. The absolute risk of end-stage renal disease is low (~0.5–1%) (Tondo 2017; Kessing 2015).
- **Thyroid:** hypothyroidism 6× more common than in controls (McKnight 2012 meta-analysis).
- **Suicide:** about 80% risk reduction [1].
- **Pregnancy:** first-trimester exposure carries a cardiac malformation RR of about 1.6–2 (Patorno 2017, n=1.3 million).

**Conclusion.** Lithium is safe for most patients when levels are kept in the 0.6–0.8 mmol/L range, organ function is monitored every 6 months, and patients and families are well informed about hydration, interactions and warning signs. Most long-term harms (thyroid, renal) are predictable, detectable and manageable, and they must be weighed against lithium's unique protection against relapse and suicide.

## In depth

### Background
The early lithium toxicity disasters (lithium chloride as a salt substitute in US cardiac patients, 1949, with deaths) delayed acceptance. Schou established level monitoring. Concerns about "lithium nephropathy" (Hestbech 1977) led to the modern monitoring schedules. Recent registry studies show the renal risk is real but smaller than feared, and is concentrated in patients with high levels and toxicity episodes [1].

### Epidemiology of harms
- About 1 in 10 long-term patients have sub-therapeutic levels; monitoring is suboptimal in practice, but automated reminders improve it [1].
- Hypothyroidism up to 20% in middle-aged women; hyperparathyroidism about 4% [1].
- Polyuria in up to 40%; CKD stage 3 in about 20–30% after long-term use (age-confounded); ESRD rare.
- Toxicity admissions: the elderly on ACE inhibitors have a 7× risk [1].

### Classification of lithium toxicity

| Type | Setting | Features |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Acute (overdose, lithium-naïve) | Overdose | GI prominent; CNS later; levels can be very high with fewer symptoms |
| Acute-on-chronic | Overdose in a regular user | Most dangerous |
| Chronic | Dehydration, interacting drugs, renal decline | CNS prominent at modest levels; elderly |

### Mechanism in detail
- **Pharmacokinetics:** no protein binding; volume of distribution ≈ total body water; half-life ~24 hours (longer in the elderly and CKD); renal clearance falls with dehydration and sodium depletion.
- **Narrow therapeutic index:** small changes in sodium or GFR cause large level changes.
- **Interactions:** NSAIDs (reduce renal prostaglandins), ACE inhibitors/ARBs (reduce GFR and aldosterone-mediated sodium retention), thiazides (proximal reabsorption; 25–40% rise), metronidazole, tetracyclines. Lowering levels: theophylline, caffeine, acetazolamide, osmotic diuretics [1].
- **Neurotoxicity:** brain lithium may not match serum levels. Combination with antipsychotics (rare neurotoxic syndrome), ECT and infections raise risk [1].

### Clinical features in detail
- **NDI:** urine osmolality <300 mOsm/kg, high serum sodium in severe cases; amiloride reduces polyuria.
- **Hypothyroidism:** fatigue, weight gain and depression that mimic bipolar depression or lead to rapid cycling. Check TSH whenever mood worsens.
- **Hypercalcaemia:** "stones, bones, groans, moans"; confusion, depression.
- **Cognitive effects:** subjective slowing, memory complaints; usually mild.

### Assessment in detail
- **eGFR trend:** a sustained fall >15 mL/min/year, or eGFR <45, should prompt nephrology review and a risk–benefit discussion (NICE).
- **Urine tests:** 24-hour volume and osmolality if polyuria is present.
- **Calcium/PTH:** yearly, or when symptomatic.
- **ECG:** baseline in those over 40 or with cardiac disease.
- **Drug-level interpretation:** steady state takes 5 days; take the sample 12 hours post-dose; adherence check (an unexpectedly low level suggests non-adherence) [1].

### Differential diagnosis

| Presentation in a lithium patient | Possible causes |
| --- | --- |
| Confusion, ataxia | Lithium toxicity, NMS (with antipsychotics), serotonin syndrome, delirium of other cause, hypercalcaemia |
| Polyuria | NDI, diabetes mellitus (antipsychotics), hypercalcaemia |
| Low mood, lethargy | Hypothyroidism, bipolar depression, hypercalcaemia |
| Tremor | Fine (therapeutic) vs coarse (toxicity); EPS from co-prescribed antipsychotic |

### Management details
- **Toxicity:** stop lithium; IV fluids (normal saline); monitor electrolytes and levels every 4–6 hours; whole-bowel irrigation for sustained-release overdose (activated charcoal is ineffective); **haemodialysis** if level >4.0 mmol/L, or >2.5 with renal impairment or severe neurological features (EXTRIP criteria) [1].
- **NDI:** reduce level; once-daily dosing; **amiloride** 5–10 mg. Avoid thiazides unless under specialist supervision.
- **Hypothyroidism:** levothyroxine; continue lithium [1].
- **Hyperparathyroidism:** endocrinology referral; cinacalcet or parathyroidectomy.
- **CKD:** discuss lithium continuation with the nephrologist; consider the lowest effective level, or switching if eGFR falls progressively.
- **Elderly:** start 200 mg; target 0.4–0.6 mmol/L; avoid NSAIDs.
- **Pregnancy:** consider continuing in high-risk women; detailed fetal echocardiography at 16–20 weeks; monitor levels monthly (then weekly from 36 weeks); reduce or withhold at onset of labour; check the level 12 hours after delivery; neonatal check. Breastfeeding is generally discouraged or closely monitored.
- **Stopping:** taper over 1–3 months [1].

### Critical appraisal
- Renal risk is often over-emphasised. Age, hypertension, diabetes and toxicity episodes confound associations. Nonetheless, levels above 0.8 mmol/L increase renal toxicity [1].
- Monitoring systems, not the drug alone, determine safety. Poor monitoring is the main hazard in resource-poor settings.
- Patient education is effective but rarely done well. NICE recommends written information and a record book [1].
- Over-caution leads to under-use and loss of the anti-suicidal benefit.

### Indian context
- Lithium carbonate 300 mg and 450 mg SR tablets are common in India (unlike UK 400 mg Priadel), so check the strength.
- Hot climate, outdoor labour, gastroenteritis and fasting (religious fasts, Ramadan) increase the risk of toxicity. Specific counselling is needed.
- Over-the-counter NSAIDs (diclofenac) are widely used for pain. Pharmacist and patient education is essential.
- Serum lithium testing is not available in many district hospitals. Plan with the nearest lab or teleconsultation.
- IPS CPG (2017) recommends similar monitoring.

### Recent advances
- **Established:** maintenance targets 0.6–0.8 mmol/L; once-daily prolonged-release dosing reduces polyuria [1].
- **Emerging:** salivary and point-of-care lithium testing; electronic reminder systems [1]; renal biomarkers (urinary NGAL) for early tubular injury.
- **Investigational:** low-dose lithium for neuroprotection; amiloride to prevent NDI.
