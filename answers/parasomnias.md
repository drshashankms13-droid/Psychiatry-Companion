## Exam answer

### Definition and classification [1]
- **Parasomnias** are abnormal behaviours or experiences arising from or during sleep. In the classification Lishman uses, they are one of four categories (dyssomnias, **parasomnias**, medical/psychiatric sleep disorders, proposed disorders).
- **Physiological grouping** (Lishman's framework):
  - **NREM (stage 3–4) arousal disorders:** somnambulism, night terrors (and confusional arousals), a continuum (Kales); the **first third of the night**;
  - **REM parasomnias:** **REM sleep behaviour disorder**, **nightmares**;
  - **sleep–wake transition phenomena:** **sleep paralysis**, **hypnagogic hallucinations**.

### NREM arousal parasomnias [1]

| | **Somnambulism** (sleep-walking) | **Night terrors** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Stage | **Partial arousal from stage 3–4 NREM**; not dream enactment (the popular notion is wrong) | Stage 3–4 NREM |
| Timing | **First third of the night**; genuine episodes usually **within 2 hours of sleep onset** | Within about an hour of sleep onset; rare after midnight |
| Epidemiology | **15% of children** (at least one episode); **2–5% of adults**; males; family history; enuresis | Often with sleep-walking; familial |
| Features | Sitting up, aimless walking; **blank expression**, repetitive purposeless movements, avoids obstacles; monosyllabic replies, suggestible; dressing, opening doors, rarely driving; usually under 10 minutes | **Intense fear, autonomic discharge** (tachycardia, panting), **screams**; a few minutes; occasionally destructive acts or striking others |
| Recall | **Complete amnesia**; on arousal, gradual awakening with **disorientation and "sleep drunkenness"** | **Amnesia**; at most **a single frightening image** (being attacked, choked, crushed) |
| Course | **Children:** benign, delayed maturation; onset before 10 usually outgrown by 15. **Adults:** often **psychopathology** (MMPI), more frequent and intense | Mostly outgrown; adults have more psychological disturbance and daytime anxiety |
| Genetics | **HLA-DQB1\*05 and \*04** (motor-control-in-sleep link, as in narcolepsy and RBD) | Shared |

**Precipitants (increase slow-wave sleep):**
- **sleep deprivation**, shift work, **alcohol**, **stress and anxiety**;
- **drugs at bedtime:** hypnotics, neuroleptics, antidepressants, tranquillisers, stimulants, antihistamines (e.g., thioridazine plus chloral derivative, leading to homicide) [1];
- **zolpidem and other GABA agonists** linked to **somnambulism, automatism, amnesia** and "**sleep driving**" [2].

**Violence and medicolegal issues (Fenwick)** [1]. Features supporting a **genuine sleep automatism**:
- **family and childhood history** of sleep-walking (a **first episode in adulthood is suspicious**);
- onset **within 2 hours** of sleep;
- inappropriate automatic behaviour with confusion; **substantial amnesia**;
- **triggers** (drugs, alcohol, fatigue, stress);
- **no concealment**; summoning help on waking;
- **motiveless, out-of-character** act;
- **penile tumescence** in sexual offences makes sleep automatism highly unlikely.

**R v Burgess (1991):** somnambulism is treated as an **insane automatism** in England and Wales (internal cause, may recur), with disposal now discretionary under the 1991 Act.

### REM parasomnias [1]
- **REM sleep behaviour disorder:** loss of REM atonia leads to **dream enactment**, often violent. Older men; middle or late night; PD, dementia, narcolepsy; **clonazepam** (see the RBD answer).
- **Nightmares:** **REM** dreams; frequent arousals at **90-minute intervals** are "almost always" due to REM nightmares; vivid recall. Contrast with night terrors (NREM, amnesia).
  - Vivid, terrifying dreams occur in about **60% of narcoleptics** (themes of murder or pursuit).
  - Alcohol withdrawal causes **REM rebound**.

### Sleep–wake transition phenomena [1]
- **Sleep paralysis:**
  - brief (seconds to a minute) **flaccid inability to move or speak** on falling asleep (or waking), with intense alarm and sometimes dream imagery of threat;
  - **abolished by touch or the patient's name being called**;
  - in **up to 62% of the normal population**; commoner with sleep disruption (jet lag, hypnotics); frequent isolated cases may be familial;
  - part of the narcolepsy tetrad.
- **Hypnagogic hallucinations:** complex, dream-like, emotionally intense in narcolepsy (vs a word or image in normal people).

### Differential diagnosis [1]

| Feature | NREM arousal disorders | RBD | Nocturnal (frontal) epilepsy |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Sleep stage | 3–4 | REM | **2** |
| Timing | 90 minutes to 2 hours after sleep onset | Later night (more than 60–90 minutes) | **Within 30 minutes** of onset, any time |
| Frequency | Less frequent; little clustering | Repeated | **Clustering**, highly stereotyped |
| Recall | Amnesia | Dream recalled | Variable |
| EEG | No epileptiform activity | REM without atonia | **Epileptiform** |

Other differentials:
- **psychogenic / dissociative** episodes (EEG shows wakefulness);
- **sleep apnoea** (arousals, flailing);
- **periodic limb movements**;
- **panic attacks** from sleep.

### Investigations [1]
- **History from a bed partner**; home **video**.
- **Polysomnography** with EEG, EOG and EMG: stage of origin, REM atonia, epileptiform activity, apnoeas.
- **Sleep-laboratory provocation:** lifting somnambulists to their feet in NREM provoked episodes (Kales).

### Management [1, 2]
1. **Safety first:** **lock doors and windows**, **remove dangerous objects**.
2. **Avoid precipitants:** **sleep deprivation**, **alcohol at bedtime**, stress; review **sedatives and z-drugs**.
3. **Reassurance:** children usually outgrow it, and psychiatric treatment is rarely needed.
4. **Adults:** **full psychiatric evaluation and treatment** (psychopathology common).
5. **Drugs:**
   - **diazepam or flurazepam** (suppress stages 3–4) for persistent sleep-walking (trial) and **night terrors** (better evidence, children and adults);
   - **propranolol** may help night terrors;
   - **RBD: clonazepam** (desipramine, carbidopa, clonidine as alternatives).
6. **Medicolegal assessment** when violence is alleged (Fenwick's criteria).

### Update from Levenson 2019: NREM arousal, nightmare disorder and night-time differentials [3]
**NREM sleep arousal disorder (DSM-5):**
- **commoner in children and adolescents** than adults (unlike RBD);
- the patient acts unusually, walks or eats while not fully alert;
- **PSG is not always needed**: episodes are intermittent and hard to capture in one night;
- **precipitants:** **sleep deprivation**, nocturnal arousals, **shifting bedtimes**, **alcohol**. Sleep deprivation has been used to provoke sleepwalking in research;
- **treatment:**
  - a safe sleeping environment;
  - a **consistent sleep schedule**;
  - fewer nocturnal awakenings;
  - **hypnotics** to prevent arousal, if warranted.

**Drug-induced complex sleep behaviours:**
- **sleepwalking and sleep eating** with z-drugs, **especially zolpidem**;
- more likely at **supratherapeutic doses** or **with alcohol** or other sedatives;
- prevalence unknown.

**REM sleep behaviour disorder:** see the RBD answer (Levenson update).

**Nightmare disorder (DSM-5 parasomnia):**
- repeated, prolonged, **highly distressing dreams**;
- **duration specifiers:** **acute** (under 1 month), **subacute** (1–6 months), **persistent** (over 6 months);
- may come with **sweating, tachycardia and tachypnoea**;
- **contexts:** severe situational stress, **PTSD**, **Parkinson's disease**, **congestive heart failure**, and drugs, notably **β-blockers**;
- **treatment:**
  - **behavioural:** exposure, relaxation, and **imagery rescripting** (the upsetting content is reframed);
  - **drugs:** **tricyclics** (suppress REM); **benzodiazepines** (fewer arousals, so less dream recall);
  - **prazosin** in nightmare disorder **without PTSD**: results unclear.

**Night-time panic and other differentials:**
- panic attacks **only in sleep** are **rare**; day-and-night panic is not uncommon;
- when panic occurs only at night (usually in **NREM**), consider:
  - breathing-related sleep disorder;
  - nightmares;
  - medical triggers (**arrhythmia, angina, reflux**);
- **always consider epilepsy** for unusual night-time behaviour. **Frontal-lobe seizures** can cause stereotyped but bizarre events **in slow-wave sleep**;
- nocturnal panic is treated **mainly with medication**, since behavioural measures are hard to apply during sleep.

**Assessment aids:**
- a **bed-partner interview**; the **Mayo Sleep Questionnaire** for family members;
- **video or audio recordings**;
- a former bed partner's confirmation of the problem **raises the likelihood** of a true disorder.

⚠️ **Discrepancies (not resolved):**
- **Sleep stage of nocturnal seizures:** Lishman [1] places them in **stage 2**, within about 30 minutes of sleep onset. Levenson [3] describes frontal-lobe seizure events **during slow-wave sleep**.
- **Classification:** Lishman's grouping (NREM arousal disorders, REM parasomnias, sleep–wake transition phenomena) predates DSM-5. Levenson uses the DSM-5 labels **NREM sleep arousal disorder** and **nightmare disorder**.

**Conclusion.** Parasomnias are classified by their sleep-stage origin:
- **NREM arousal disorders** (sleep-walking, night terrors): early night, deep sleep, amnesia, common in children and usually outgrown; in adults associated with psychopathology; provoked by sleep deprivation, alcohol, stress and sedatives such as zolpidem;
- **REM parasomnias** (RBD with dream enactment; nightmares);
- **transition phenomena** (sleep paralysis, hypnagogic hallucinations).

Diagnosis relies on the bed-partner history and polysomnography, distinguishing them from nocturnal epilepsy. Management is safety, avoiding precipitants, benzodiazepines (diazepam for NREM disorders, clonazepam for RBD), psychiatric care in adults, and careful medicolegal assessment of violent episodes.
