## Exam answer

### Introduction [1]
- **Epidemiology:**
  - neurosyphilis has declined greatly since penicillin;
  - **resurgence since 1997**, especially in **men who have sex with men** with **HIV coinfection**;
  - partial treatment and HIV have produced **atypical, attenuated presentations**; there has been a shift from parenchymatous (general paresis, tabes) to **meningovascular** disease.
- **Why psychiatrists must know it:** neurosyphilis "can present with virtually any form of psychiatric complaint". **50%** of a recent cohort presented neuropsychiatrically (psychosis, delirium, dementia) (Timmermans & Carr).
- **CSF invasion:** about **25%** of infected people have treponemal CSF invasion; most clear spontaneously.

### Classification and clinical forms [1]

| Form | Timing | Features |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Early asymptomatic** | Early | CSF abnormal (cells, protein, serology) with no signs: **10% of primary, 30% of secondary** syphilis. Benign if treated; may lead to later parenchymatous disease |
| **Acute syphilitic meningitis** | Within 2 years (even with the secondary rash) | Fever, headache, **delirium**, neck stiffness; CSF over 1000 cells; blood serology positive. Good recovery, some residual intellectual impairment |
| **Meningovascular** (10–35%) | **4–10 years** (peak 7) | **Pathology:** basal gummatous leptomeningitis; periarteritis and endarteritis with thrombosis. **Prodrome:** headache, lethargy, slowness, forgetfulness, poor concentration, **irritability, personality change** → dementia with fleeting delusions or episodes of **delirium**. **Basal signs:** cranial nerves (VII, VIII; **deafness in 20%**), pupils, optic neuritis, hydrocephalus, hypothalamic signs. **Vascular:** TIAs, **stroke in young people** (MCA, basilar), pseudobulbar palsy. Can mimic encephalitis or MS. CSF up to 200 cells. **Good prognosis** with treatment |
| **Tabes dorsalis** (with about 20% of general paresis) | 8–12 years | **Lightning pains** (75–90%), paraesthesiae, **sensory ataxia** (positive Romberg), visceral crises, areflexia, **pupils abnormal in 90%**, optic atrophy, Charcot joints. Late paranoid or depressive psychoses |
| **General paresis** | **5–25 years** (mean 10–15) | See below |
| **Juvenile general paresis** | Onset 6–21 years (congenital) | Learning difficulty, fits, simple dementing type |

### General paresis (dementia paralytica, GPI) [1]
**History:**
- Noguchi & Moore (1913) demonstrated *T. pallidum* in the brain: the first proven aetiology of a major mental disorder;
- it is the **only** syphilitic disease with spirochaetes in brain tissue.

**Pathology:**
- thickened meninges, **atrophic brain** (frontal and parietal);
- perivascular lymphocytes and plasma cells; neuronal loss; gliosis ("frosted" ventricular ependyma);
- **rod-shaped microglia with iron pigment** (Prussian blue): pathognomonic.

**Presentation:**
- males, age 30–50;
- **prodrome:** headache, insomnia, lethargy;
- **personality change** (moodiness, apathy, loss of emotional control, **coarsening of behaviour**: frontal);
- **episodic forgetfulness**, then poor concentration, slowing, **dyscalculia**, speech and writing changes, **early loss of insight**;
- **abrupt presentation in about 50%:** a social lapse (law-breaking, violence, indecent exposure), reckless spending, an amnesic episode, a seizure or status, delirium, or a suicide attempt.

**Clinical forms (frequencies changed over time):**

| Form | Features |
| --- | --- |
| **Simple dementing** (now the commonest: 20–60%) | Progressive dementia, shallow affect, mild euphoria or apathy, fleeting persecutory delusions |
| **Depressive** (27%: the commonest in Dewhurst's series) | Classic depression, sometimes **nihilistic or hypochondriacal** delusions; affect shallow and "readily lifted" |
| **Grandiose / expansive** (7–18%; formerly the prototype; persisted longer in **India**) | Bombastic delusions of wealth or power, euphoric condescending mood with shallowness, petulance when challenged; resembles mania |
| **Taboparetic** (about 20%) | Dementia plus tabetic signs; true **Argyll Robertson pupils** and optic atrophy commoner |
| **Others** | Manic; **schizophrenia-like** (paranoid, passivity, hallucinations); **neurasthenic**; acute organic (encephalitis-like); **Korsakoff-like**; epileptic or apoplectic; **Lissauer's** (focal) |

**Examination:**
- **pupils abnormal in about two-thirds** (inequality, irregularity, sluggishness). **Argyll Robertson pupil:** small, irregular, iris atrophy, **reacts to accommodation not light**, poor mydriasis;
- **tremor** (face, lips, hands) in about two-thirds;
- **dysarthria**, dysphasia;
- **brisk reflexes**, extensor plantars, spastic weakness (absent reflexes if taboparetic);
- ataxia; optic atrophy.

**Untreated course:**
- progressive dementia and paralysis;
- seizures in about half; "**congestive attacks**" (transient focal deficits);
- **death within 4–5 years**.

### Neurosyphilis and HIV [1]
- **Shared risk factors;** each potentiates the other's transmission (genital ulcers); syphilis raises HIV viral load.
- **More rapid progression** to neurosyphilis, even with treatment.
- **Serology may be falsely negative.**
- CDC: early symptomatic neurosyphilis in **1.7%** of HIV-positive MSM; 75% presented with **visual disturbance or new headache**; 25% had undiagnosed HIV.
- **Treat any stage as neurosyphilis.**

### Investigations [1]
**Serology:**
- **non-treponemal** (VDRL, RPR): positive in **90% or more** of untreated GPI. They can be negative after antibiotics or with HIV. **False positives:** SLE, leprosy, thyroiditis, haemolytic anaemia, RA, pregnancy, vaccination, viral infections, drug users;
- **treponemal** (TPHA, **FTA-ABS**, EIA): specific; FTA-ABS is almost always positive in blood and CSF in modern forms; positive for life after treatment;
- **EIA** (IgG ± IgM) with confirmatory **TPHA** is now standard.

**CSF (essential whenever neurosyphilis is suspected):**
- raised pressure;
- **lymphocytosis 5–50 cells/mm³**;
- **protein 50–100 mg/dL**;
- raised globulin, oligoclonal bands;
- **CSF VDRL** specific but may be negative; CSF FTA-ABS sensitive but poorly specific;
- **CSF tau** may indicate CNS involvement.

**EEG:** excess theta and slow activity in most GPI patients.

**Diagnostic criteria:**
- **Hooshmand:**
  - positive blood FTA-ABS **plus** suggestive ocular or neurological signs; or
  - blood and CSF FTA-ABS positive **plus** CSF over 5 WBC; or
  - progressive unexplained neurological symptoms plus a penicillin response;
- **CDC 1996 (Box 7.4):** confirmed = reactive serology + **reactive CSF VDRL**; probable = negative CSF VDRL + raised CSF protein or cells + compatible clinical features.

### Differential diagnosis [1]
- **Serology should be considered in all psychiatric admissions.** Roberts & Emsley: the diagnosis had been considered in only 3 of 21 cases; initial labels were schizophrenia, depression, mania, hysteria. About 4% of psychogeriatric inpatients were seropositive.
- **No primary dementia should be diagnosed until syphilis is excluded.** Pupillary signs are rare in vascular dementia.
- Easily missed in **alcohol dependence** (expansiveness, tremor, dysarthria attributed to drink).
- **Frontal tumour**: an anterior basal meningioma can mimic it closely.
- Late-onset epilepsy; unexplained delirium.
- **Meningovascular vs GPI:** meningovascular is earlier and more acute, fluctuates, preserves insight, has more focal signs and a better prognosis.
- **Coincidental** asymptomatic neurosyphilis with an independent psychosis.

### Management [1]
1. **Prevention:** adequate treatment of primary syphilis prevents GPI. **Treat asymptomatic neurosyphilis** as for GPI. **HIV-positive patients:** treat as GPI even in primary or secondary stages.
2. **Antibiotics:**
   - **aqueous benzylpenicillin 18–24 million units IV daily for 10–14 days** (CDC 2006); or daily IM **procaine** benzylpenicillin;
   - **benzathine penicillin weekly is inadequate** (poor CSF levels);
   - **penicillin allergy:** **ceftriaxone 2 g daily for 10–14 days**; or desensitisation; chloramphenicol or doxycycline (limited data);
   - no penicillin resistance has emerged.
3. **Jarisch–Herxheimer reaction** (5–10%): fever, malaise, symptom exacerbation, seizures. Start treatment **in hospital**; **prednisone** the day before and during the first days.
4. **Psychiatric treatment:**
   - antipsychotics (atypicals) for excitement or psychosis; antidepressants for depression; anticonvulsants for seizures;
   - **ECT relatively contraindicated** in active GPI (sudden worsening, focal deficits).
5. **Contact tracing:** test the **partner and children**.
6. **Rehabilitation:** improvement may continue for **up to 2 years**.
7. **Follow-up:**
   - **CSF every 6 months until the cell count normalises**;
   - **retreat** if cells have not fallen by 6 months, the CSF has not normalised by 2 years, cells rise above 5/mm³, or blood titres rise;
   - persistent raised protein alone can be ignored if cells are low;
   - aggressive follow-up if HIV-positive;
   - no clinical improvement with a good CSF response means **re-evaluate the diagnosis**, not routine retreatment.

### Prognosis [1]
- **Hahn et al. (1959), 1086 patients:** **80% of early or mild cases remit** and return to work. Even institutionalised patients have about a 1 in 3 chance of recovering enough for rehabilitation.
- **Poor prognostic signs:** incontinence, inability to dress, neglect of hygiene, **age over 60**.
- **Active CSF** (high cells) responds better but may also progress.
- **At 5 years, resolution in over half** of disorientation, fits, tremor, incontinence, euphoria and depression. Over a quarter recovered memory, judgement, insight, delusions and hallucinations.
- **Mortality:** 31% at 10 years (9% due to GPI); about four times expected.
- New neurological signs occurred in about 30% after a mean of 12 years (Wilner & Brody).

**Conclusion.** Neurosyphilis has returned with HIV and now often presents atypically. Its forms are:
- asymptomatic;
- meningitic;
- **meningovascular** (personality change, delirium, young stroke, cranial nerve palsies);
- **tabes**;
- **general paresis** (insidious frontal personality change and dementia, now commonly simple dementing or depressive; grandiose forms rarer; Argyll Robertson pupils, tremor, dysarthria).

Psychiatrists should keep a low threshold for serology and **CSF examination** in any new psychiatric or cognitive presentation. Treatment is **IV benzylpenicillin 18–24 MU daily for 10–14 days** (with Herxheimer precautions), symptomatic psychotropics (avoid ECT in active disease), contact tracing, and 6-monthly CSF follow-up. Early treatment gives remission in about 80%.
