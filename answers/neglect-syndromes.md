## Exam answer

### Definition and spectrum [1]
- **Unilateral neglect** is a failure to attend to, respond to or use one side of space or of the body that **is not explained by a primary sensory or motor deficit**. A hemianopia "may or may not be present but does not account for the deficit".
- **Spectrum** (Critchley 1953):
  - **Inattention / extinction:** a minor degree, shown only by **double simultaneous stimulation**.
  - **Unawareness:** fails to use the left hand in bimanual tasks or overlooks the left foot with slippers, but uses the limbs normally when attention is drawn to them. The limbs occupy "a lower level in a hierarchy of personal awareness".
  - **Neglect:** half the body ignored in washing and dressing, half the face unshaven, hair uncombed. More likely with confusion or intellectual impairment.
  - **Hemisomatognosia, anosognosia, somatoparaphrenia:** the more florid end (below).

### Types [1]

| Type | Features |
| --- | --- |
| **Sensory extinction / inattention** | With bilateral simultaneous touch, the stimulus contralateral to the lesion is not perceived; **visual inattention** with simultaneous moving objects in both half-fields |
| **Personal (somatic) neglect** | Left limbs ignored in grooming and dressing; left limbs not used |
| **Extrapersonal (visual / spatial) neglect** ("unilateral spatial agnosia") | Left-sided details omitted in drawing and copying; writing **crowded into the right** of the page; food on the left of the plate ignored; **left turnings missed**, so the patient gets lost. An agnosia for space as such, not merely for spatial relations |
| **Peripersonal vs extrapersonal** | Halligan & Marshall: severe neglect for near space (line bisection displaced right) but accurate at 2.5 m (dart throwing) |
| **Representational (imaginal) neglect** | Bisiach & Luzzati: imagining Milan's Piazza del Duomo, patients omit the buildings on their **left from either viewpoint**, though their knowledge of them is preserved |
| **Implicit processing** | Marshall & Halligan: the patient "did not see" flames on the left of a house but **preferred** the house without flames |
| **Hemisomatognosia** | Limbs on one side feel **absent**: episodic (focal seizure aura) or continuous. Insight is preserved when consciousness is clear; with confusion the patient searches for the limbs or accuses others of taking them |
| **Anosognosia** | Unawareness or denial of hemiplegia (below) |

- **Somatic and visual neglect** usually occur together but can dissociate. Somatic neglect without visual neglect is rather rare; the reverse is less rare.
- **Dressing apraxia** often reflects neglect, right–left disorientation and body-image disturbance.

### Anosognosia and related phenomena [1]
- **Anosodiaphoria:** lack of concern, or a flat, facetious reaction when confronted.
- **True anosognosia:** complete unawareness, or **vehement denial**, of the hemiplegia; shallow rationalisations ("I'm tired").
- **Somatoparaphrenia:** limbs attributed to someone else (Lishman's case: the left limbs "belonged to" her daughter Ann).
- **Personification:** limbs treated as objects or pets. **Misoplegia:** hatred of the limb.
- **Course:** commonly **transient** in the early days after acute hemiplegia, receding with clouding; it can persist with delusional elaboration.
- **Generic use:** unawareness of aphasia (Wernicke's), amnesia (Korsakoff), cortical blindness (**Anton's syndrome**), pain (**pain asymbolia**); deficits in dementia; insight in psychosis (Amador & David).
- **Mechanism (unresolved):**
  - **sensory loss** is usual but does not explain it;
  - **cognitive impairment or clouding** is common but not essential;
  - **psychogenic denial** (Weinstein & Kahn: perfectionistic premorbid traits, subtle confusion);
  - Marcel et al. (64 stroke patients): **not unitary**. Unawareness of the *consequences* of paralysis is more common than unawareness of the paralysis itself; some patients are aware but quickly forget.
  - **Against psychogenesis:** strong **left-side predominance** (Starkstein: 38% with left-sided vs 11% with right-sided signs).

### Localisation and frequency [1]
- **Right (non-dominant) parietal**, especially near the **supramarginal and angular gyri**; visual neglect follows **right temporoparietal** lesions (Heilman). Left neglect is far commoner than right.
- **Also:** basal ganglia and thalamus (posterior thalamic infarcts); "subcortical neglect" may act through secondary right-cortical effects (diaschisis). Transient right-sided neglect occurs with left lesions.
- **Anosognosia (Pia et al. review):** right-hemisphere damage is the hallmark, but bilateral damage is common. Frontal, parietal or temporal lesions are equally implicated, and subcortical lesions can cause it. It is **most likely with combined parietal and frontal** lesions.
- **Why left-sided?** Unexplained. Suggestions: left limbs are normally subordinate; aphasia masks right-sided signs; the non-dominant hemisphere is prepotent for the body image.
- **Stroke frequency** (Ringman 2004): neglect in **43% of right- vs 20% of left-hemisphere** strokes at presentation, falling to **17% and 5% at 3 months**.
- **Severity factors:** related to **abruptness** of the lesion, **clarity of consciousness** and the presence of **motor weakness**. Usually transient and variable during examination; occasionally enduring.

### Assessment [1]
1. **Observe** washing, combing, dressing and bimanual activity. Do they use the left hand? Do they correct the deficit when it is pointed out?
2. **Double simultaneous stimulation:** tactile and visual extinction.
3. **Line bisection**; mark the midpoint of a circle.
4. **Drawing and copying:** house, **clock face** (numbers missing on one half), Rey–Osterrieth copy; map of the country; note crowding to one side.
5. **Reading and writing:** words or margins omitted on one side.
6. **Body image:** finger agnosia, autotopagnosia; ask about awareness of weakness (anosognosia, anosodiaphoria); ask whose arm it is (somatoparaphrenia).
7. **Formal test:** **Behavioural Inattention Test** (line bisection, star cancellation).
8. **Exclude** hemianopia as the sole explanation; assess consciousness and cognition.

### Management [1]
1. **Natural history:** most neglect **resolves spontaneously within weeks**; there is **no good evidence that stimulation and exercises** improve on this (Lincoln & Bowen 2006).
2. **Attention-based rehabilitation:** current theories emphasise a failure to direct attention to (or disengage from) one side, leading to theory-driven programmes such as **attentional cueing to the neglected side** (Pierce & Buxbaum 2002).
3. **Practical measures:**
   - early on, or with mental impairment, **approach from the sound side** and arrange belongings and work materials accordingly;
   - correct refractive errors and hearing loss.
4. **Safety:** left-space neglect contributes to **falls and mishaps** and hinders learning a new environment.
5. **Prognosis:**
   - neglect is a "notoriously disabling syndrome";
   - **persistent denial or disowning of hemiplegic limbs carries an especially poor prognosis** for independence, often with prolonged incontinence;
   - neglect is hard to treat when it comes with **lack of insight**.
6. **General:** multidisciplinary stroke rehabilitation (OT, ADL retraining); engage the family; monitor mood.

**Conclusion.** Neglect ranges from extinction to unawareness to frank personal and extrapersonal neglect, and on to anosognosia and somatoparaphrenia. It usually follows **right temporoparietal** lesions, is far commoner on the left, and can involve near, far and even imagined space. It is assessed by observation, double simultaneous stimulation, line bisection, cancellation and drawing. Most cases resolve within weeks. Persistent neglect, especially with denial of hemiplegia, predicts poor functional recovery. Rehabilitation uses attentional cueing and environmental adaptation, but its evidence base is weak.
