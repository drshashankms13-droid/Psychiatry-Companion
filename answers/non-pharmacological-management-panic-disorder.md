## Exam answer

**Definition.** Panic disorder (ICD-11 6B01; DSM-5-TR) is **recurrent unexpected panic attacks**: discrete episodes of intense fear peaking within minutes, with palpitations, sweating, trembling, breathlessness, chest pain, dizziness, derealisation, and fear of dying, losing control or "going crazy". They are followed by **≥1 month of persistent concern** about further attacks or their consequences, and/or maladaptive behaviour change (avoidance, safety behaviours). Agoraphobia is a separate, frequently comorbid diagnosis.

### Mechanism (target of psychological treatment)
- **Clark's cognitive model (1986):** trigger (internal or external) → perceived threat → apprehension → bodily sensations → **catastrophic misinterpretation** ("palpitations = heart attack") → more anxiety → more sensations → panic (vicious circle).
- **Maintaining factors:** safety behaviours (sitting down, carrying water or pills, holding on), avoidance and hypervigilance to body sensations prevent disconfirmation.
- **Behavioural (interoceptive conditioning):** early bodily cues become conditioned stimuli for panic.
- **Biological:** suffocation false-alarm (Klein), CO₂ hypersensitivity, amygdala–brainstem fear network [1].

### Non-pharmacological interventions

| Intervention | Content | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Psychoeducation** | Nature of panic, fight-or-flight, cognitive model, "anxiety is not dangerous" | Basis of all CBT |
| **CBT for panic (first line)** — 7–14 weekly sessions (NICE) | Cognitive restructuring of catastrophic beliefs; **interoceptive exposure** (hyperventilation, spinning, stair-running, breathing through a straw); **in-vivo exposure** to avoided situations; dropping safety behaviours; behavioural experiments; relapse prevention | Response ~50–60%; remission 45–55%; gains maintained; equal to or better than medication long-term [1,2] |
| Panic Control Treatment (Barlow) | Psychoeducation + breathing retraining + cognitive restructuring + interoceptive and in-vivo exposure | Equivalent to imipramine acutely; better maintenance (Barlow 2000) |
| Applied relaxation (Öst) | Progressive muscle relaxation → cue-controlled → rapid relaxation applied in feared situations | Effective, slightly less than CBT |
| Breathing retraining | Slow diaphragmatic breathing for hyperventilation | Modest; risk of becoming a safety behaviour |
| **Self-help / guided self-help / internet CBT** | Books, apps, iCBT (step 2 NICE) | Effective, especially guided [1] |
| Brief/intensive CBT | 4–5-day intensive formats; brief GP-delivered CBT | Comparable outcomes |
| Panic-focused psychodynamic psychotherapy (PFPP; Milrod) | Unconscious meanings of panic, separation and anger conflicts | RCT evidence; comparable to CBT in one trial |
| Mindfulness / ACT | Acceptance of sensations, values-based action | Emerging |
| Exercise | Aerobic exercise 3×/week | Small–moderate benefit; adjunct |
| Lifestyle | Reduce caffeine, alcohol, nicotine; sleep | Nicotine dependence predicts relapse [2] |

### Assessment before treatment
Rule out medical causes (arrhythmia, angina, thyrotoxicosis, hypoglycaemia, phaeochromocytoma, asthma/COPD, vestibular disorders), substances (caffeine, cannabis, stimulants, withdrawal). Do ECG and TSH. Assess agoraphobia, depression, suicidality, alcohol and benzodiazepine use. **Scales:** Panic Disorder Severity Scale (PDSS), Agoraphobic Cognitions Questionnaire, Body Sensations Questionnaire, Mobility Inventory, panic diary.

### Management sequence (NICE stepped care)
Recognition and diagnosis → psychoeducation and self-help → **CBT** (first choice) → SSRI if CBT is unavailable, declined or insufficient → combination for severe or complex cases → specialist referral. Avoid benzodiazepines (they reduce the effect of exposure, cause dependence, and act as safety behaviours) [3].

### Key evidence
- **Barlow 2000 (JAMA, n=312):** CBT and imipramine equal acutely; CBT responders relapsed less after stopping.
- **Clark 1994:** cognitive therapy superior to applied relaxation and imipramine at 15 months.
- **Pompoli 2016 (Cochrane network meta-analysis):** CBT the most effective psychological therapy.
- **Relapse:** up to 30% relapse 1–2 years after CBT; maintenance sessions help [1]. Natural 3-year remission was 75% for panic disorder vs 67% for panic with agoraphobia, but relapse was 12% and 21% [2].

**Conclusion.** CBT with interoceptive and in-vivo exposure, targeting catastrophic misinterpretation and safety behaviours, is the first-line and most durable treatment for panic disorder. It should be offered before or alongside medication, can be delivered in brief, group or digital formats, and should include relapse-prevention planning because the disorder tends to relapse and remit.

## In depth

### Background
- Freud described "anxiety attacks" within anxiety neurosis. Klein (1964) found that imipramine blocked panic but not anticipatory anxiety, which led to the separation of panic disorder in DSM-III (1980).
- Clark (1986) and Beck developed the cognitive model. Barlow developed Panic Control Treatment (1989).
- DSM-5 (2013) made agoraphobia a separate diagnosis and introduced "with panic attacks" as a specifier for any disorder.

### Epidemiology
- Lifetime prevalence of panic disorder ~2–5%; panic attacks ~28% lifetime (NCS-R). Women are affected about twice as often [1].
- Onset late adolescence to mid-30s.
- Predictors of incidence and relapse: previous panic attacks, GAD/MDD, nicotine dependence, female sex, younger age, financial crises [2].
- High utilisation of emergency services and cardiology; non-cardiac chest pain in many.

### Classification and criteria
- **ICD-11 6B01:** recurrent unexpected panic attacks not restricted to particular stimuli, with persistent concern or avoidance for several weeks; not due to another condition.
- **DSM-5-TR:** recurrent unexpected attacks (4 of 13 symptoms) + ≥1 month of concern or behaviour change.
- **Agoraphobia (6B02):** fear of situations where escape is difficult (public transport, open or enclosed spaces, crowds, being outside alone).

### Mechanism in detail
- **Fear circuit:** amygdala, periaqueductal grey, hypothalamus, locus coeruleus; insula interoception; failure of prefrontal inhibition.
- **Respiratory hypotheses:** suffocation false alarm (Klein 1993); hypersensitivity to CO₂ and lactate challenge.
- **Genetics:** heritability ~40%.
- **Anxiety sensitivity** (fear of anxiety sensations) is a cognitive vulnerability and a CBT target.
- **Learning:** interoceptive conditioning, with avoidance as negative reinforcement.
- **How exposure works:** inhibitory learning (Craske). New "safety" memories compete with fear memories; expectancy violation matters more than habituation [1].

### Clinical features in detail
Attacks peak in about 10 minutes. Nocturnal panic occurs in about 40%. Anticipatory anxiety, agoraphobic avoidance, safety behaviours, repeated ER visits and cardiology investigations are common. Cultural variants include *ataque de nervios*, *khyâl cap* (Cambodian), and in India, "dhat" or "gas" attributions and "heart weakness" worries.

### Differential diagnosis

| Condition | Distinguishing feature |
| --- | --- |
| Cardiac arrhythmia, ischaemia | Exertional, ECG changes, older age, risk factors |
| Hyperthyroidism | Persistent symptoms, weight loss, tremor, TSH |
| Phaeochromocytoma | Paroxysmal hypertension, headache; urinary metanephrines |
| Hypoglycaemia | Relation to fasting or insulin |
| Asthma/COPD | Wheeze, spirometry |
| Substance intoxication or withdrawal | Caffeine, stimulants, cannabis, alcohol or benzodiazepine withdrawal |
| Social anxiety, specific phobia | Cued attacks only in feared situations |
| GAD | Persistent worry without discrete attacks |
| PTSD | Trauma cues |
| Illness anxiety | Long-standing disease preoccupation |

### Management details — the CBT protocol (Clark)
1. Formulation of the vicious circle using a recent attack.
2. Psychoeducation; panic diary.
3. **Behavioural experiments:** voluntary hyperventilation (2 minutes) reproduces sensations, which shows they are harmless; focus on the body to induce sensations.
4. **Cognitive restructuring:** evidence for and against; probability estimates; decatastrophising.
5. **Dropping safety behaviours** during exposure (e.g., walking fast when dizzy instead of sitting).
6. **Interoceptive exposure:** spinning in a chair, breathing through a straw, running on the spot, head between the knees.
7. **Graded in-vivo exposure:** fear hierarchy for agoraphobic situations; repeated, prolonged and varied practice (inhibitory learning).
8. Relapse prevention; booster sessions [1].

**Formats:** individual (12–15 sessions), group, brief (4–7 sessions), intensive (1 week), internet-delivered with therapist support, telephone. Combine with an SSRI in severe agoraphobia or comorbid depression. Combination is better acutely, but CBT alone has better maintenance after medication stops (Barlow 2000).

**Special populations:** pregnancy (CBT first-line) [1]; elderly (exclude cardiac causes); adolescents (family-involved CBT).

### Critical appraisal
- CBT trials use waiting-list controls with inflated effects; effects vs psychological placebo are smaller [1].
- Up to 30% relapse after CBT; maintenance CBT helps [1].
- Benzodiazepines may undermine exposure (state-dependent learning), though evidence is mixed. NICE advises against them [3].
- Access: trained CBT therapists are scarce in India. Guided self-help, group CBT and task-shifting to counsellors are pragmatic alternatives [1].

### Indian context
- Panic often presents to cardiologists and emergency departments; patients may be labelled "anxiety" and given alprazolam indefinitely.
- Culturally adapted CBT (somatic idioms of "heart weakness", "nerves", "gas") and family involvement.
- Yoga-based breathing (pranayama) is popular. It can help relaxation, but may become a safety behaviour if used to avoid sensations.
- Tele-MANAS and digital CBT apps (in regional languages) are expanding access.
- IPS CPG (Gautam 2017): CBT and SSRIs first-line [4].

### Recent advances
- **Established:** guided iCBT; brief intensive CBT.
- **Emerging:** VR exposure for agoraphobia; inhibitory-learning-optimised exposure (expectancy violation, variability) [1]; capnometry-guided breathing (Freespira, FDA-cleared).
- **Investigational:** D-cycloserine-augmented exposure (Cochrane: no clear benefit) [1]; rTMS (not effective for panic per the IPS rTMS CPG) [5].
