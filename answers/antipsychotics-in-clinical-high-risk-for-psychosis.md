## Exam answer

**Definition.** The **at-risk mental state (ARMS)**, also called ultra-high risk (UHR) or clinical high risk (CHR), describes help-seeking young people with subthreshold psychotic features and declining function who have a markedly raised short-term risk of developing a psychotic disorder [1]. The **Melbourne UHR criteria** (PACE clinic; Yung 2004) require age 15–25, referral for help, a drop in function (or sustained low function) for ≥1 month in the past year, **and** one of the following [1]:
1. **Attenuated psychotic symptoms (APS):** subthreshold ideas of reference, odd beliefs, perceptual disturbance, paranoid ideation or odd speech, several times a week, for 1 week to 5 years.
2. **Brief limited intermittent psychotic symptoms (BLIPS):** frank psychotic symptoms lasting under 1 week that resolve spontaneously.
3. **Trait vulnerability:** schizotypal personality disorder, or a first-degree relative with psychosis, plus functional decline.

### Mechanism (why antipsychotics were tried)
Rising presynaptic striatal dopamine synthesis capacity during transition (Howes 2011) → aberrant salience → psychosis. The hypothesis: early D2 blockade could prevent sensitisation and transition, and limit the "toxic" effects of untreated psychosis [1].

### The risk to be treated
Transition rates (Fusar-Poli 2012 meta-analysis): **18% at 6 months, 22% at 1 year, 29% at 2 years, 36% at 3 years**. That is roughly 400 times the population risk, but **most do not convert**, and rates have fallen in established clinics [1].

### Pros and cons

| Pros (arguments for) | Cons (arguments against) |
| --- | --- |
| Psychosis is disabling; preventing or delaying even a minority of cases is valuable [1] | **Most (over 60%) are false positives** at 3 years; many would be treated unnecessarily [1] |
| Early trials: McGorry 2002 (risperidone + CBT) cut transition at 6 months; McGlashan 2006 (olanzapine) showed a trend to benefit [1] | Benefit **not sustained** after stopping drug (Phillips 2007); effects may just delay onset [1] |
| May reduce DUP and the severity of first episode | **Adverse effects:** weight gain with olanzapine, EPS with risperidone [1]; metabolic and prolactin effects in adolescents |
| Useful when APS rapidly worsen with risk to self or others (Australian guideline exception) [1] | **Iatrogenic dopamine supersensitivity**, rebound on withdrawal, brain volume changes [1] |
| Low dose may be acceptable to some families | **Stigma and labelling** of young people; self-stigma [1] |
| | Non-specific interventions (supportive therapy, monitoring, CBT) do as well on transition; NNT for any intervention ≈ 9 at 12 months [1] |
| | ARMS youth have high rates of depression, anxiety and substance use, which have their own effective treatments [1] |
| | **NICE (2014):** "Do not offer antipsychotic medication" to people at increased risk [1] |

### Assessment
CAARMS (Comprehensive Assessment of At-Risk Mental States) or SIPS/SOPS; basic symptoms (SPI-A); comorbidity (depression, anxiety, substance use); function (SOFAS/GAF); risk (suicide); family history; cannabis; physical health; organic exclusion where indicated [1].

### Management (recommended approach)
Engage and psychoeducate (non-stigmatising language) → case management, monitoring of mental state and risk → **CBT** (stress–vulnerability model) ± family intervention → treat comorbid depression/anxiety (SSRIs) and substance use → address stressors (education, work, housing) → regular review for transition (CAARMS) → **low-dose atypical antipsychotic only if** psychotic symptoms rapidly worsen with significant functional decline and risk, with close monitoring → if transition occurs, treat as first-episode psychosis (early intervention service) [1].

### Key evidence
- **McGorry 2002 (PACE):** risperidone 1–2 mg + CBT vs needs-based intervention; transition 10% vs 36% at 6 months; difference lost by 12 months in non-adherent patients [1].
- **McGlashan 2006 (PRIME):** olanzapine vs placebo; transition 16% vs 35% (not significant); weight gain about 9 kg [1].
- **McGorry 2013:** CBT + risperidone, CBT + placebo and supportive therapy + placebo all had similar low transition [1].
- **van der Gaag 2013 meta-analysis:** NNT 9 across interventions; CBT best supported [1].
- **Amminger 2010 (omega-3):** promising but not replicated (NEURAPRO) [1].

**Conclusion.** Routine antipsychotic treatment of people at clinical high risk is **not justified**. The predictive value is too low, benefits do not last, harms are real, and safer psychological and case-management approaches are as effective. Antipsychotics should be reserved for rapid worsening with risk, at low dose and with close review. In practice, this means "treat the person and the comorbidity, monitor the psychosis".

## In depth

### Background
- Sullivan (1927) proposed early intervention; Chapman (1966) described early symptoms retrospectively [1].
- Huber's Bonn school described **basic symptoms**: subjective disturbances of thought, perception and drive [1].
- Yung and McGorry set up the PACE clinic in Melbourne (1994) and defined the UHR criteria prospectively, moving away from the retrospective DSM-III prodrome [1].
- In the USA, Miller and McGlashan developed SIPS/SOPS (2003) and the PRIME trial [1].
- DSM-5 (2013) placed **attenuated psychosis syndrome** in Section III (conditions for further study) after major debate. In practice it can be coded as "other specified schizophrenia spectrum disorder" [1].

### Epidemiology
- Transition rates have fallen: Melbourne's early cohorts converted at about 40% in 12 months, later cohorts at about 15% (the "dilution effect" from earlier and wider referral) [1].
- The risk continues long term; in Nelson 2013, about 35% converted by 10 years.
- Among non-transitioned UHR cases, many have persistent or new non-psychotic disorders and poor function (Lin 2015) [1].
- Indian data are limited; studies from NIMHANS and PGIMER suggest similar psychopathology but lower help-seeking for attenuated symptoms.

### Classification and criteria

| Approach | Criteria / tool | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| UHR (Melbourne) | APS, BLIPS, trait + decline; CAARMS | Most used in UK, Australia [1] |
| CHR (North America) | SIPS/SOPS; Criteria of Prodromal Syndromes | Similar groups [1] |
| Basic symptoms | COPER, COGDIS; SPI-A | Earlier stage; sensitivity 98%, specificity 59% (Klosterkötter 2001) [1] |
| Self-disturbance | EASE (Parnas 2005) | Phenomenological; needs training [1] |
| DSM-5 attenuated psychosis syndrome | Section III | Not an official diagnosis |
| ICD-11 | No specific category; schizotypal disorder (6A22) is placed in the psychosis grouping | — |

### Mechanism in detail
- **Dopamine:** progressive rise in striatal dopamine synthesis capacity in those who transition (Howes 2011) [1].
- **Glutamate:** altered thalamic and hippocampal glutamate; lower thalamic glutamate predicts poor functional outcome (Allen 2015) [1].
- **Structure:** parahippocampal grey matter reduction; multi-site imaging classifiers (Koutsouleris 2015) [1].
- **Electrophysiology:** mismatch negativity (MMN), P300 and sensory gating deficits; MMN predicts onset [1].
- **Blood markers:** a 15-analyte assay (inflammation, oxidative stress, hormones) showed predictive potential (Perkins 2015) [1].

### Clinical features in detail — predictors of transition
- **Melbourne (Yung 2004b):** APS plus genetic risk, long symptom duration, poor social function, poor attention; at least one of these gave PPV 80.8% [1].
- **NAPLS (Cannon 2008, n=291):** five predictors (genetic risk with decline, unusual thought content, suspiciousness, poor social function, substance use); combined PPV up to 81% [1].
- **EPOS (Ruhrmann 2010):** six-variable prognostic index (positive symptoms, bizarre thinking, sleep disturbance, schizotypy, GAF, education); PPV 83.3% [1].
- BLIPS converts more often than APS.
- Clinician gestalt ("praecox feeling") had PPV of only 32% (Nelson 2010) [1].

### Assessment in detail

| Domain | Tool / investigation |
| --- | --- |
| Risk status | CAARMS (intensity, frequency, duration of positive symptoms) or SIPS |
| Basic symptoms | SPI-A |
| Function | SOFAS, GAF |
| Comorbidity | Structured interview for depression, anxiety, OCD, substance use |
| Cognition | Brief battery (attention, working memory, verbal fluency) |
| Physical | Weight, BMI, glucose, lipids, prolactin before any antipsychotic |
| Organic screen | Only when indicated: thyroid, drugs, EEG/MRI for atypical features |

### Differential diagnosis

| Condition | Distinguishing feature |
| --- | --- |
| First-episode psychosis | Full-threshold symptoms over 1 week (CAARMS threshold) |
| Schizotypal personality disorder | Long-standing, stable traits without recent decline |
| Depression with psychotic-like experiences | Mood-congruent, accompanies mood episode |
| Substance-induced experiences | Clear temporal link with cannabis or stimulants |
| OCD with poor insight | Intrusive, ego-dystonic, ritual-linked |
| Autism spectrum | Lifelong social difficulty, no decline |
| Normal adolescent unusual experiences | No distress, help-seeking or decline |

### Management details
- **Psychological:** CBT (normalising, stress management, appraisal of unusual experiences); family work; vocational and educational support [1].
- **Omega-3** (1.2 g/day EPA+DHA): safe, but the evidence is weak after NEURAPRO [1].
- **Antidepressants:** naturalistic data (Cornblatt 2007) suggested benefit; treat depression and anxiety on their own merits [1].
- **Antipsychotics if used:** low-dose second-generation drug (e.g., aripiprazole 2–5 mg, risperidone 0.5–2 mg); avoid olanzapine in youth because of weight; set a review date and a stopping plan; obtain informed consent including uncertainty [1].
- **Adolescents:** involve parents; consider the effect on school; metabolic sensitivity.
- **Monitoring:** at least monthly in the first year; urgent pathway if transition occurs.

### Critical appraisal
- **Prediction:** PPV of UHR criteria depends on the base rate. In non-help-seeking or community samples, PPV falls sharply [1]. Prognostic models (EPOS, NAPLS calculators) improve PPV but need external validation.
- **Trial quality:** small samples, falling transition rates mean underpowered trials, and short follow-up. Active "control" arms may themselves be effective [1].
- **Outcome choice:** transition is a dichotomous threshold. Functional outcome and comorbidity may matter more to young people [1].
- **Ethics:**
  - Labelling and stigma.
  - The right not to know the prognosis.
  - Tension between reassurance and monitoring, where monitoring itself may raise anxiety.
  - Legal implications of an "attenuated psychosis" label [1].
- **Counter-argument:** if any intervention can delay or soften psychosis, even for a minority, it is worth offering with care [1]. Antipsychotics are simply not the best first choice.
- **The pharmacological case** may revive with non-D2 agents (muscarinic agonists, glycine-transporter inhibitors), which might avoid D2-related harms; these are still investigational.

### Indian context
- Few dedicated ARMS services exist. Some early intervention programmes, such as NIMHANS early psychosis clinics and SCARF Chennai's first-episode services, see help-seeking youth.
- Pathways to care often go first to faith healers, so attenuated symptoms may be interpreted spiritually and DUP is long. Community awareness and training of general practitioners and school counsellors matter more than specialist ARMS clinics.
- The risk of over-prescribing antipsychotics is real in busy outpatient settings where psychological therapy is scarce. The NICE/Australian caution is directly relevant [1].
- MHCA 2017: informed consent and the rights of minors (guardian involvement) apply; avoid coercive "preventive" treatment.
- Cannabis (bhang, ganja) use in youth is an important modifiable risk.

### Recent advances
- **Established:** NICE recommends CBT ± family intervention for ARMS, not antipsychotics [1].
- **Emerging:** the "clinical staging" model (McGorry), with transdiagnostic youth services (headspace, Orygen) treating early distress regardless of eventual diagnosis; individualised risk calculators (NAPLS-2, PSYCHS).
- **Investigational:** ulotaront and other non-D2 agents in CHR; machine-learning multimodal prediction (PRONIA); sequential multiple assignment trials (STEP, Australia); the AMP SCZ consortium.
