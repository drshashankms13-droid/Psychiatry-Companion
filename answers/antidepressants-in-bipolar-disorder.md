## Exam answer

**Definition of the issue.** Antidepressants are the most commonly prescribed drugs for bipolar depression: 50% of US patients (n=7,760) and 81% of European patients (n=2,231) [1]. Yet their efficacy is doubtful, and they may cause **treatment-emergent affective switch** (TEAS: mania, hypomania or mixed state, usually within 8–12 weeks), **cycle acceleration/rapid cycling**, and chronic irritable dysphoria [1,2]. The statement "antidepressants are contraindicated in bipolar disorder" is **too absolute**. The evidence supports a **limited, conditional role**: adjunctive (never monotherapy), short-term, selected patients, and the right drug [1,3].

### Mechanism
- **Antidepressant efficacy:** monoamine reuptake inhibition → mood improvement, but bipolar depression may be less monoamine-dependent.
- **Switch risk:** increased noradrenergic and dopaminergic tone → triggers mania in vulnerable individuals (bipolar I > II; TCAs and SNRIs > SSRIs > bupropion).
- **Destabilisation:** shortened inter-episode intervals (kindling/cycle acceleration) [1,2].
- **Antimanic co-treatment:** lithium, valproate, carbamazepine or a second-generation antipsychotic reduces the switch [1].

### Evidence — for and against

| Question | Evidence |
| --- | --- |
| **Acute efficacy (adjunctive)** | Mixed. Meta-analyses: response RR 2.3 (4 trials, n=662); RR 1.4, 45% vs 33% (10 trials, n=1,432); small benefit (6 trials, n=1,383). But no benefit in others: RR 1.18 (5 trials, n=906); null (16 trials, n≈2,300). **STEP-BD (Sachs 2007, n=366):** paroxetine/bupropion added to mood stabiliser = placebo (response ~24% vs 27%) [1] |
| **Best-established combination** | **Olanzapine + fluoxetine:** remission 49% vs 33% (olanzapine) vs 25% (placebo); switch ~6% in all arms (Tohen 2003, n=788) [1]; "possibly the strongest evidence for an antidepressant effect in bipolar depression" [3] |
| **Monotherapy** | Paroxetine = placebo (55% vs 53%, n=239) [1]. Mania with monotherapy: HR 2.8 vs no exposure in first 3 months (registry) vs no increase with a mood stabiliser [1]. Maintenance monotherapy: manic recurrence RR 2.4 [1] |
| **Switch, acute (adjunctive)** | 4–8%, same as placebo (RR ≈1.0 across meta-analyses) [1]. Pooled 7,915 patients: 15% vs 14% [1] |
| **Switch by drug** | TCAs RR 2.9 vs others (11% vs 4%); venlafaxine ~15%; bupropion lowest (RR 0.3 vs others) [1]; SSRIs low |
| **Switch by type** | Bipolar I ≈2× bipolar II [1]. In bipolar II, sertraline alone, lithium alone and both had similar hypomania (20%, 19%, 13%; none manic) [1] |
| **Maintenance** | Continuing antidepressant 52 vs 8 weeks: fewer depressive relapses (HR 0.4), no overall benefit; manic HR 2.3 (NS) [1]. Adjunctive maintenance relapse RR 0.70 (7 trials, n=532) [1]; agomelatine/citalopram for 12 months raised mania risk (OR 1.77) [1] |
| **Suicidality** | Unclear; observational data are contradictory [1] |

### Assessment before prescribing
Bipolar I or II; past response to antidepressants; history of TEAS, rapid cycling (≥4 episodes/year) or mixed features; current mixed features; recent mania (within 2–3 months); substance use; severity and suicide risk; current antimanic drug and level (lithium ≥0.6–0.8 mmol/L) [1,2].

### Management (ISBD Task Force 2013-consistent)
1. **First line for bipolar depression:** quetiapine, lurasidone, olanzapine–fluoxetine, lamotrigine, lithium, cariprazine, lumateperone [3].
2. **Consider adjunctive antidepressant** if: there was a previous good response without switch; depression is severe or persistent despite antimanic drugs; or it is bipolar II.
3. **Choose an SSRI or bupropion.** Avoid TCAs and venlafaxine [1,3].
4. **Always with an antimanic drug.** No monotherapy, especially in bipolar I [1,2].
5. **Avoid in:** mixed features, rapid cycling, previous switch or cycle acceleration, recent mania, active substance misuse, frequent or severe manic episodes [1].
6. **Duration:** continue 2–4 months after remission, then taper over 2–4 weeks. Longer only if depressive relapses recur off antidepressant [1].
7. **Monitor:** educate on early signs of hypomania (sleep, energy). If a switch occurs, **stop the antidepressant abruptly** and optimise antimanic treatment [1,4].

### Key evidence
- **STEP-BD (Sachs 2007):** adjunctive antidepressant no better than placebo; no excess switch.
- **Tohen 2003:** olanzapine–fluoxetine > olanzapine > placebo.
- **Altshuler 2017 (bipolar II, n=142):** sertraline monotherapy switch rate similar to lithium.
- **ISBD Task Force (Pacchiarotti 2013):** limited role; avoid monotherapy and in mixed states [4].
- **Viktorin 2014 (Swedish registry):** monotherapy raised mania risk (HR 2.8); not with mood stabiliser cover [1].

**Conclusion.** Antidepressants are **not absolutely contraindicated** but are **relatively contraindicated as monotherapy in bipolar I, and in mixed or rapid-cycling states**. As short-term adjuncts to an antimanic drug in selected patients, especially bipolar II or those with a past good response, they are reasonable and guideline-supported. Even so, better-evidenced first-line options (quetiapine, lurasidone, olanzapine–fluoxetine, lamotrigine) should usually come first.

## In depth

### Background
- Kraepelin's broad manic-depressive insanity (1899–1913) included recurrent depression; Leonhard (1957), Angst and Perris (1966) separated bipolar from unipolar. DSM-III (1980) codified the split [2].
- Tricyclic-induced mania was reported from the 1960s. Wehr and Goodwin (1987) linked antidepressants to rapid cycling.
- Akiskal's "soft bipolar spectrum" (1980s–90s) and Ghaemi's "antidepressant view of the world" critique (2001) argued that many "unipolar" depressions are bipolar spectrum and that antidepressants worsen them [2,5].
- Counter-views: Möller & Grunze (2000), "Have some guidelines gone too far in restricting antidepressants?" [2]. ISBD Task Force consensus (2013) [4].

### Epidemiology
- Patients with bipolar disorder spend about three times as long depressed as manic (Judd 2002–03). Depression drives most morbidity and suicide; about 15% of patients with mood disorders die by suicide [2].
- 9–40% of patients with MDD convert to bipolar disorder over 5–15 years; diagnostic change to bipolar disorder occurs at about 1.25% per year (Angst) [2].
- Bipolar spectrum prevalence is about 6.4% in the community vs 1% for BP-I; up to half of clinic depressions may be spectrum cases [5].
- More than 70% of antidepressants worldwide are prescribed by non-psychiatrists, so unrecognised bipolarity often gets antidepressant monotherapy [5].
- Natural switch rates without antidepressants are substantial (14% in pooled data) [1].

### Classification and criteria
- **DSM-5-TR:** a full manic or hypomanic episode emerging during antidepressant treatment and **persisting beyond the physiological effect** counts towards bipolar I/II. A few symptoms (irritability, agitation) alone do not.
- **ICD-11:** similar; substance-induced mood disorder if symptoms do not persist.
- **TEAS definitions (ISBD):** onset within 8 weeks (12 for some) of starting or increasing the antidepressant; graded as mania, hypomania, mixed, or subsyndromal.
- **Akiskal's "bipolar III":** depression with antidepressant-induced hypomania [5].
- **Ghaemi's bipolar spectrum disorder criteria:**
  - **A:** at least one major depressive episode.
  - **B:** no spontaneous hypomania or mania.
  - **C:** family history of bipolar disorder in a first-degree relative, or antidepressant-induced mania/hypomania.
  - **D (soft signs):** hyperthymic personality; more than 3 MDEs; brief MDEs (under 3 months); atypical features; psychotic MDE; onset before 25; postpartum onset; antidepressant "wear-off"; failure of 3 or more antidepressants.
  - The diagnosis requires both C criteria plus 1 D, or one C plus 2 D, or 6 of 9 D [2,5].

### Mechanism in detail
- **Catecholamine hypothesis:** TCAs and SNRIs increase noradrenaline → greater switch risk than SSRIs. Bupropion is dopaminergic/noradrenergic but empirically has low switch risk [1].
- **Circadian disruption:** antidepressants and sleep deprivation advance or shift rhythms → mania.
- **Kindling/sensitisation (Post):** repeated episodes shorten cycle length; antidepressants may accelerate this.
- **Protective effect of antimanic drugs:** lithium (GSK-3, circadian stabilisation) at levels ≥0.6 mmol/L — patients who switched had mean lithium levels of 0.4 [1].
- **Genetics:** 5-HTTLPR short allele and other variants are associated with switch (inconsistent) [1].

### Clinical features in detail
- **Features of bipolar depression vs unipolar:** earlier onset; abrupt onset; more frequent, shorter episodes; psychotic features; atypical features (hypersomnia, hyperphagia, leaden paralysis); psychomotor retardation; postpartum onset; family history of bipolar disorder [2,3,5].
- **TEAS presentation:** decreased need for sleep, talkativeness, irritability, risk-taking; may be mixed (dysphoric activation).
- **Antidepressant-associated chronic irritable dysphoria (ACID):** STEP-BD described it (El-Mallakh 2008) [2].
- **Rapid cycling:** ≥4 episodes per year; antidepressants may maintain it.

### Assessment in detail

| Tool | Use |
| --- | --- |
| Mood Disorder Questionnaire (MDQ) | Screen for past hypomania; also used in monitoring [1,5] |
| Hypomania Checklist (HCL-32) | Detects hypomania in depressed patients |
| Bipolarity Index | Score ≥50 is suggestive [5] |
| Two-question mood lability screen | "Frequent ups and downs?" "Without cause?" [5] |
| Life chart (NIMH-LCM) | Course, cycling, drug effects |
| YMRS, MADRS/HAM-D, PHQ-9 | Monitoring [1] |
| Collateral history | Patients under-report hypomania [5] |

### Differential — situations that look like "antidepressant switch"

| Situation | Clue |
| --- | --- |
| True TEAS | Full syndrome persisting after stopping the antidepressant |
| Akathisia / activation syndrome | Restlessness without euphoria or grandiosity; early in SSRI treatment |
| Natural course of bipolar disorder | Switch independent of drug timing |
| Serotonin syndrome | Autonomic instability, clonus, hyperthermia |
| Substance-induced | Stimulants, steroids |
| Mixed depression | Agitation and racing thoughts present before the antidepressant |

### Management details
- **Bipolar I depression:** antidepressants as **third-line adjuncts** in CANMAT/ISBD 2018 (SSRI/bupropion added to lithium/valproate/SGA). First line: quetiapine, lurasidone, lithium, lamotrigine (CANMAT 2018 also lists lurasidone + lithium/valproate adjunct) [3].
- **Bipolar II depression:** quetiapine first line; sertraline, venlafaxine and fluoxetine monotherapy are second-line options in CANMAT 2018 [3].
- **Maintenance:** avoid routine long-term antidepressants. Consider them only if depressive relapses recur repeatedly without them and there is no history of switch [1].
- **MAOIs:** tranylcypromine had a large effect size without switch in a meta-analysis, but the diet and drug interactions restrict its use [3].
- **Special groups:**
  - Pregnancy: SSRIs (sertraline) with a mood stabiliser; avoid valproate.
  - Adolescents: higher switch risk; suicidality warnings.
  - Elderly: hyponatraemia, falls.

### Critical appraisal
- **Trial populations exclude** rapid cyclers, substance users and suicidal patients, and are closely monitored, so real-world switch rates may be higher [1].
- **Illusion of efficacy:** spontaneous cycling out of depression may be attributed to the antidepressant [1].
- **Most data are bipolar I** (1,842 vs 227 bipolar II in 11 trials) [1].
- **Spectrum over-diagnosis risk:** broadening bipolarity may dilute the concept (Baldessarini) and over-expose people to mood stabilisers and antipsychotics [2,5].
- **Under-diagnosis risk:** missing soft bipolarity leads to antidepressant monotherapy, switch and suicidality [5].
- **Guideline divergence:** NICE favours olanzapine–fluoxetine first; BAP and CANMAT emphasise quetiapine, lurasidone and lamotrigine, and place antidepressants lower [3].

### Indian context
- Antidepressants are widely prescribed by general physicians in India without psychiatric assessment; screening for bipolarity (MDQ, two-question screen) in primary care is recommended [5].
- **IPS clinical practice guidelines for bipolar disorder (2017; Shah et al.)** follow a similar line: avoid monotherapy, use SSRIs or bupropion with a mood stabiliser, short duration.
- Cost: quetiapine, olanzapine and fluoxetine are cheap generics; lurasidone is moderately priced; lumateperone is not available.
- Psychoeducation of families about early hypomania signs is feasible and important [5].

### Recent advances
- **Established:** lumateperone (2021) and cariprazine (2019) approved for bipolar depression [3].
- **Emerging:** ketamine/esketamine for refractory bipolar depression (low switch risk, low certainty) [3]; pramipexole adjunct [3].
- **Investigational:** pharmacogenomic and clinical risk models for switch; digital mood monitoring to detect early switch.
