## Exam answer

**Definition.** Tardive dyskinesia (TD) is a **delayed-onset, often persistent hyperkinetic movement disorder** after at least **3 months** of exposure to dopamine receptor-blocking agents (1 month in those aged ≥60; DSM-5-TR). It may also emerge within 4 weeks of withdrawing oral drugs (8 weeks for depots). It consists of involuntary choreiform, athetoid or rhythmic movements, most often **orofacial-lingual-masticatory** (lip smacking, chewing, tongue protrusion — "fly-catcher", "bon-bon sign"), plus limb choreoathetosis ("piano-playing" fingers), trunk rocking and respiratory dyskinesia. The broader **tardive syndromes** include tardive dystonia, akathisia, tics, myoclonus and tremor.

### Pathophysiology
1. **Dopamine receptor supersensitivity:** chronic D2 blockade in the nigrostriatal pathway → up-regulation and supersensitivity of postsynaptic D2 receptors → hyperkinesia. This explains worsening on dose reduction ("withdrawal-emergent") and temporary masking by increasing the dose.
2. **Indirect-pathway imbalance:** blockade of D2 on indirect-pathway striatal neurons → maladaptive plasticity → reduced GABAergic output from the striatum to GPi/SNr → thalamocortical disinhibition.
3. **GABA hypofunction:** loss or dysfunction of striatal GABAergic interneurons (supported by GABA agonist benefit).
4. **Oxidative stress / neurotoxicity:** increased dopamine turnover → free radicals → damage to striatal neurons (rationale for vitamin E, B6, ginkgo).
5. **Synaptic plasticity / maladaptive learning** in the cortico-striatal circuits.
6. **Genetic vulnerability:** DRD2/DRD3 (Ser9Gly), CYP2D6 poor metabolisers, HTR2C, SOD2, VMAT2 polymorphisms.
7. **Illness-intrinsic:** spontaneous dyskinesias occur in 9% of never-medicated patients with established psychosis, so antipsychotics add to an inherent risk [1].

### Risk factors

| Patient | Drug |
| --- | --- |
| Older age (strongest), female (post-menopausal) | FGA vs SGA (annual incidence 6.5% vs 2.6%) [1] |
| Affective illness (mood disorders) | High D2 occupancy, high dose, long duration |
| Early acute EPS, parkinsonism | Intermittent treatment, frequent interruptions |
| Diabetes, substance and alcohol misuse | Anticholinergic co-prescription (may unmask or worsen) |
| Brain damage, intellectual disability | Metoclopramide, other D2 blockers [1] |
| Non-white ethnicity (African) | Aripiprazole and clozapine lowest liability [1] |

### Assessment
- **AIMS** (Abnormal Involuntary Movement Scale) at baseline, then every 6–12 months (every 3–6 months if high risk). Glasgow Antipsychotic Side-effect Scale.
- Distinguish from spontaneous dyskinesia, Huntington's disease, Wilson's disease, edentulous dyskinesia, stereotypies, tics, and drug-induced chorea (levodopa, stimulants, phenytoin).
- Assess functional and psychological impact (stigma, eating, speech, breathing).

### Management
1. **Prevention:**
   - Use the minimum effective dose; prefer SGAs with low TD liability (aripiprazole, quetiapine, clozapine).
   - Avoid unnecessary antipsychotics (e.g., for anxiety, insomnia) and long-term anticholinergics; avoid metoclopramide.
   - Monitor regularly with AIMS; inform the patient [1].
2. **First steps:**
   - Stop anticholinergic drugs.
   - Review the indication and dose of the antipsychotic. A dose reduction may at first worsen TD; the evidence for benefit is weak.
   - **Switch** to **clozapine** (best supported) or quetiapine; olanzapine and aripiprazole are possible options [1].
3. **Add-on treatment (first choice): VMAT2 inhibitors** [1]:
   - **Valbenazine** 40 → 80 mg once daily.
   - **Deutetrabenazine** 12–48 mg/day in divided doses.
   - **Tetrabenazine** 25–200 mg/day (the only licensed UK treatment; depression, sedation, parkinsonism, akathisia).
4. **Other options with some evidence** [1]:
   - **Vitamin B6** (pyridoxine) up to 400 mg/day.
   - **Vitamin E** 400–1,600 IU (slows deterioration).
   - **Amantadine** 100–300 mg.
   - **Clonazepam** 1–4 mg (tolerance; intermittent use).
   - **Ginkgo biloba** 240 mg.
   - Melatonin 10 mg; propranolol; levetiracetam.
5. **Focal or severe:** botulinum toxin for focal dystonia or dyskinesia; **DBS (GPi)** for severe refractory cases; ECT; HF bilateral rTMS (RCT data) [1].

### Key evidence
- **Carbon 2017/2018 meta-analysis:** annual TD incidence 6.5% with FGAs vs 2.6% with SGAs [1].
- **KINECT 3 (Hauser 2017):** valbenazine 80 mg reduced AIMS by −3.2 vs −0.1 with placebo.
- **ARM-TD and AIM-TD (Fernandez 2017; Anderson 2017):** deutetrabenazine effective.
- **2020 network meta-analysis:** VMAT2 inhibitors, vitamin E, amantadine and B6 probably effective [1].

**Conclusion.** TD results from long-term dopamine blockade producing receptor supersensitivity and striatal circuit dysfunction, on top of an illness-related vulnerability. It is often irreversible, so **prevention and early detection** are paramount. Established TD is managed by removing anticholinergics, switching to clozapine or quetiapine, and adding a VMAT2 inhibitor, with B6, vitamin E or amantadine as alternatives where VMAT2 inhibitors are unavailable.

## In depth

### Background
- First described by Schonecker (1957) and Sigwald (1959) after chlorpromazine. Faurbye named it "tardive dyskinesia" in 1964.
- The APA Task Force (1980, 1992) recognised TD as a major public health problem and a medicolegal issue.
- The SGA era reduced but did not eliminate TD [1].
- Valbenazine and deutetrabenazine were approved by the FDA in 2017, the first approved treatments for TD.

### Epidemiology
- Prevalence about 20–30% in long-term FGA-treated patients; about 7% (SGA only) to 30% (FGA) across studies (Carbon 2017, J Clin Psychiatry).
- Annual incidence: FGA 6.5%, SGA 2.6% [1]. Overall about 5% per year of exposure [1]. Elderly: 25–30% after 1 year of FGA.
- Spontaneous dyskinesias: about 4–9% of never-medicated patients with schizophrenia [1].
- Associated with greater cognitive impairment, more severe psychopathology and higher mortality [1].

### Classification and criteria
- **DSM-5-TR (medication-induced movement disorders):** tardive dyskinesia; tardive dystonia; tardive akathisia; neuroleptic-induced parkinsonism; acute dystonia; acute akathisia; NMS; medication-induced postural tremor; antidepressant discontinuation syndrome.
- **ICD-11:** 8A07.10 drug-induced dyskinesia (secondary hyperkinetic movement disorder).
- **Schooler–Kane research criteria (1982):** at least moderate abnormal movements in one area or mild in two (AIMS), after ≥3 months of exposure.
- **Subtypes:** classic orobuccolingual; limb-truncal; respiratory; withdrawal-emergent dyskinesia (transient, resolves within 6–12 weeks); covert dyskinesia (masked by the drug).

### Mechanism in detail

| Hypothesis | Evidence | Therapeutic implication |
| --- | --- | --- |
| D2 supersensitivity | Animal models; worsening on withdrawal; masking with dose increase | VMAT2 inhibitors deplete presynaptic dopamine |
| Indirect-pathway / GABA deficiency | Reduced GAD in primate models | GABA agonists (clonazepam, levetiracetam) |
| Oxidative stress | Lipid peroxidation markers; MnSOD polymorphism | Vitamin E, B6, ginkgo, melatonin |
| Maladaptive plasticity | Altered cortical excitability | rTMS; DBS |
| Serotonergic | 5-HT2A antagonism protective | Atypicals |
| Genetic | DRD3 Ser9Gly, HSPG2, CYP2D6 | Risk prediction (research) |

### Clinical features in detail
- **Orofacial:** puckering, smacking, chewing, tongue darting, blinking, grimacing.
- **Limb:** choreoathetoid finger movements, foot tapping, ankle movements.
- **Trunk:** rocking, pelvic thrusting.
- **Respiratory:** irregular breathing, grunting.
- Movements are worse with stress and distraction (activation manoeuvres), reduced by voluntary action, and absent in sleep.
- Many patients are unaware of them; families notice. TD can affect eating, speech and dentures, and is stigmatising [1].
- **Tardive dystonia:** retrocollis, opisthotonos; younger males; more disabling.

### Assessment in detail

| Tool | Detail |
| --- | --- |
| AIMS | 12 items; 7 body areas rated 0–4; examination procedure with activation (e.g., finger tapping, mouth open) |
| ESRS (Extrapyramidal Symptom Rating Scale) | Comprehensive EPS |
| DISCUS | Dyskinesia scale for intellectual disability |
| Video recording | Track change |
| Investigations when atypical | Copper and caeruloplasmin (Wilson's), HD genetic testing, TSH, glucose, MRI |

### Differential diagnosis

| Condition | Distinguishing feature |
| --- | --- |
| Spontaneous dyskinesia (schizophrenia, elderly) | Present before drugs [1] |
| Huntington's disease | Family history, cognitive and psychiatric decline, generalised chorea; CAG repeats |
| Wilson's disease | Young, liver disease, Kayser–Fleischer rings |
| Sydenham's chorea, SLE, hyperthyroidism, pregnancy chorea | Context |
| Edentulous oral dyskinesia | Ill-fitting dentures |
| Stereotypies, mannerisms | Psychotic or catatonic behaviour; purposeful-looking |
| Tics / Tourette's | Premonitory urge, suppressible |
| Withdrawal-emergent dyskinesia | After abrupt stop; resolves in weeks |
| Other drug-induced: levodopa, stimulants, phenytoin, lithium (tremor) | Drug history |

### Management details
- **VMAT2 inhibitors:**
  - **Valbenazine:** 80 mg once daily; adverse effects include somnolence, QT prolongation at high exposure, parkinsonism; avoid with strong CYP3A4 inducers; reduce dose with strong CYP2D6 or 3A4 inhibitors.
  - **Deutetrabenazine:** twice daily with food; dose maximum 36 mg with strong CYP2D6 inhibitors.
  - **Tetrabenazine:** CYP2D6 genotyping for doses >50 mg (US); risk of depression and suicidality.
  - None of the three is approved or widely available in India (tetrabenazine is available).
- **Clozapine switch:** consider even in non-treatment-resistant illness with severe TD [1].
- **Do not** escalate the antipsychotic dose to suppress TD (it masks but worsens long-term).
- **Elderly:** lowest doses; avoid metoclopramide.
- **Pregnancy:** vitamin B6 and E are safe; avoid VMAT2 inhibitors.
- **Psychosocial:** counselling, dental care, speech therapy.

### Critical appraisal
- Most older treatments have weak, small-sample evidence [1].
- VMAT2 inhibitor trials are short (6–12 weeks) and industry-funded, with modest absolute AIMS changes; long-term open-label data are supportive.
- **Dose reduction** dilemma: it may worsen TD in the short term and risks relapse [1].
- **Reversibility:** a minority remit after stopping the drug; this is age-dependent [1].
- The SGA advantage may be partly confounded by high-dose FGA comparators, although the meta-analysis did not support this [1].

### Indian context
- High historical FGA use (haloperidol, trifluoperazine, fluphenazine depot) and long-term trihexyphenidyl co-prescription in India mean a large prevalent TD population.
- AIMS monitoring is rarely systematic; it should be part of OPD protocols.
- **Cost:** valbenazine and deutetrabenazine are not readily available or affordable. **Tetrabenazine, clonazepam, vitamin E, B6, amantadine and ginkgo** are accessible.
- Clozapine is cheap; ANC monitoring is the barrier.
- Medicolegal: informed consent about TD risk is good practice.

### Recent advances
- **Established:** VMAT2 inhibitors as first-choice add-ons [1].
- **Emerging:** once-daily extended-release deutetrabenazine (2023); valbenazine sprinkle formulation; long-term safety data.
- **Investigational:** HF bilateral rTMS (RCT) [1]; GPi-DBS [1]; genetic risk prediction; ondansetron; levetiracetam [1].
