## Exam answer

### Definition and mechanism [1]
- **RBD** (Schenck 1986) is an acute behavioural disturbance **arising in REM sleep**: the **enactment of altered dreams**, confirmed by polysomnography.
- **Mechanism:** the **normal pontine inhibitory outflow to spinal motor neurones during REM sleep is diminished** ("**REM sleep without atonia**", Fig. 13.4), so dream-related movements emerge.
- **Normal REM:** "commanded but inhibited" movement. Dreams involve imagined movement, but EMG is lowest in REM.

### Epidemiology and associations [1]
- **Schenck & Mahowald, 70 cases:**
  - **older men** predominate; mean onset **53 years** (range 9–73);
  - most otherwise healthy, but **one-third have a CNS disorder**: **dementia, Parkinson's disease, narcolepsy**, occasionally vascular or other **brainstem** lesions;
  - some linked to **drug or alcohol withdrawal** or major stress;
  - **progressive** until treated;
  - **three-quarters had repeated injuries** (bruises, lacerations, occasionally fractures or dislocations).
- **Narcolepsy:** RBD in about **12%** of narcoleptics studied by PSG; **stimulants and tricyclics** may induce or worsen it. RBD is associated with **HLA-DQw1** (a supratype of DQB1\*0602, the narcolepsy allele).
- **Multiple system atrophy:** RBD, daytime sleepiness and insomnia are very common [1, Ch. 12].
- **Alcohol:** chronic alcoholics lose normal REM motor inhibition (increased REM EMG) [1].
- Initially often misdiagnosed as **nocturnal epilepsy, obstructive sleep apnoea or psychiatric disorder**.

### Clinical features [1]
- Behaviour in the **middle or last third of the night**, almost always **more than 60–90 minutes after sleep onset** (when REM predominates).
- **Dream change:** dreams become more **vivid, action-filled and violent**; being **chased or attacked**; protecting family.
- **Behaviour:** talking, **shouting**, punching, kicking, **jumping out of bed**, grabbing at others; **injury to self and bed partner**.
- **Recall:** patients usually (not always) **remember the dream** that drove the action but **not their behaviour**.
- **Rousability:** they can be **roused during the episode**.
- **Illustrative cases:** a placid 52-year-old salesman punching his wife and through a wall while "protecting his family"; a 67-year-old with dementia "fighting squirrels in the attic".

### Polysomnography [1]
- **Usual sleep-stage distribution and cycling preserved**; sometimes **reduced REM latency, increased REM density**, more stage 3–4.
- **Periodic and aperiodic limb twitching in NREM.**
- **Defining feature: intermittent loss of the normal EMG atonia during REM** (REM sleep without atonia), with muscle twitching and body movements (Fig. 13.4).
- **No seizure activity.**

### Differential diagnosis [1]

| Condition | Distinguishing features |
| --- | --- |
| **NREM parasomnias** (sleep-walking, night terrors, confusional arousals) | Arise from **stage 3–4 NREM**, **first third of the night** (within 1–2 hours of sleep onset); younger; **amnesia**, no dream recall (night terrors: a single frightening image); confused and difficult to rouse |
| **Nocturnal (frontal) epilepsy** | Stereotyped; **clustering**; **stage 2 sleep**, often **within 30 minutes of sleep onset**; epileptiform EEG |
| **Nightmares** | REM; awaken with vivid recall but **no motor enactment** |
| **Obstructive sleep apnoea** | Snoring, apnoeas, flailing arousals; PSG shows apnoeas and desaturation |
| **Periodic limb movements** | Brief stereotyped leg jerks every 20–40 seconds in NREM |
| **Psychiatric / dissociative** | PSG shows wakefulness during events |

RBD pointers: **older age at onset**, associated **degenerative neurological disease**, a **history of vivid dreams**, and **rousability** [1, Ch. 6].

### Management [1]
1. **Safety:** protect the patient and bed partner from injury. *(Specific bedroom-safety measures are not detailed in the source; Lishman stresses protection in parasomnias generally.)*
2. **Clonazepam:** "rapidly effective in controlling both the disturbing dreams and the problematic sleep behaviours", with only minor lapses. Other sedative-hypnotics were unhelpful.
3. **Alternatives** (e.g., when **sleep apnoea** contraindicates clonazepam): **desipramine, carbidopa, clonidine**.
4. **Review drugs:** stimulants and tricyclics (in narcolepsy), and alcohol or drug withdrawal.
5. **Investigate for associated neurological disease** (parkinsonism, dementia, narcolepsy, brainstem lesion).

### Update from Continuum 2022: RBD as a prodromal synucleinopathy [2]
- **Isolated (idiopathic) RBD** is **highly specific** for future **PD, DLB or MSA**: **at least half, and perhaps up to 80%**, are later diagnosed with an α-synucleinopathy.
  - PD diagnosis occurs at **about 6% per year**.
  - Of prodromal people who convert, about **55% develop PD, 45% DLB**, rarely MSA.
  - **More severe cognitive dysfunction predicts dementia.**
- **Prevalence:** **at least 2.2%** of older adults, similar to the prevalence of PD. Prodromal PD is **commoner in men**.
- **Only call RBD "isolated" after evaluation:** exclude **narcolepsy, structural brainstem lesions and immune-mediated disorders** (history, examination, imaging as appropriate).
- **Antidepressants** can cause RBD or lower its threshold:
  - **SSRIs, SNRIs, TCAs** aggravate it, so review them;
  - in some patients the **depression itself may be a prodromal feature** of synucleinopathy (psychiatric relevance).
- **PSG:** REM sleep without atonia shows as **increased chin or limb EMG**. In MSA, **sleep-disordered breathing or stridor** can be mistaken for dream enactment, so **PSG is needed when in doubt**.
- **Treatment:**
  - **safe sleep environment** (remove sharp objects; bedrails);
  - **melatonin and/or clonazepam**, though "no firm evidence" supports either.
- **Counselling and follow-up:**
  - discuss the **risk of a neurodegenerative disorder**;
  - refer for anxiety or depression;
  - **regular exercise**, vascular risk-factor control, **avoid head trauma**;
  - offer **research participation**;
  - **no treatment yet prevents conversion**; prodromal criteria and biomarkers are **research tools, not yet clinical**.

**Conclusion.** RBD is a REM parasomnia in which loss of REM atonia lets patients act out vivid, often violent dreams. It typically affects older men in the later part of the night, causes frequent injury, and is associated with Parkinson's disease, dementia, MSA and narcolepsy. **Polysomnography** confirms **REM sleep without atonia** and excludes epilepsy and OSA. **Clonazepam** or **melatonin** (Continuum 2022; Lishman also lists desipramine, carbidopa and clonidine) plus safety measures. Isolated RBD is a strong **prodrome of PD, DLB or MSA** (50–80% convert, about 6% a year to PD), so counselling, review of serotonergic antidepressants and neurological follow-up are essential.
