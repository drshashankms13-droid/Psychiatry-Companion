## Exam answer

**Context [1, 2].**
- **Kidney transplantation is the treatment of choice** for many ESRD patients. It improves **survival and quality of life** compared with dialysis and **saves costs long-term**, so payers favour early, **pre-emptive** transplantation [1, 2].
- **Kidney is the commonest transplant:** about **80%** of the US waiting list; 19,060 transplants in 2016.
- **Kidney candidates wait longest:** median **4.2 years**. While waiting, 4–10% become unsuitable and 10–15% die [1].
- **10-year survival:** **78%** with a living-donor kidney, 63% with a deceased-donor kidney. **Graft survival** at 10 years is **49%**, so many face a **second transplant** [1].
- **Living donors** provide more than one-third of US kidneys (about 5,600 a year) [1].

### Phases and their psychiatric issues (transplant timeline) [1]
| Phase | Issues |
| --- | --- |
| **ESRD and dialysis (pre-transplant)** [2] | About **70%** of urban haemodialysis patients have a DSM-IV Axis I disorder. **MDD 20–27%**. Depression predicts death or hospitalisation (HR **2.07**). Anxiety disorder in about 45% (phobias, **panic**). **Needle phobia**. Alcohol problems in 28% of one sample. **Non-adherence** (missed or shortened sessions, fluid and diet; median **19 pills a day**). **Cognitive impairment**. **Withdrawal from dialysis** decisions. |
| **Evaluation** | **Pre-transplant psychosocial assessment**, which determines whether psychiatric issues are **conditions for listing**. |
| **Waiting** | Loss of work, driving, roles (breadwinner, parent) and ADLs, bringing anxiety, depression, anger, denial and grief. **Ambivalence** shows as missed appointments. Eligibility may change; counsel the patient for **either transplant or death**. **Anticipatory anxiety** about "the call". |
| **The call and surgery** | Elation mixed with **panic**. Some **decline the organ**. Postoperative **delirium** and sedation. |
| **Early post-transplant** | Within year 1, **20% of kidney recipients** (30% liver, up to 60% heart) develop mood or anxiety disorders. PTSD in **3–15%**. **Steroid** and **calcineurin-inhibitor** neuropsychiatric effects. **Delayed graft function**. |
| **Long-term** | **Non-adherence** (highest in kidney recipients). Depression with **graft loss** or declining function after more than 3 years. Return to tobacco. **CNI nephrotoxicity**. |

**Three psychological themes in kidney recipients** (Baines 2002) [1]:
1. **Fear of rejection.**
2. **Paradoxical loss** after a successful transplant.
3. **Psychological adaptation to the new kidney.**

Individual psychotherapy resolved these themes and reduced depression. Perceived **loss of control** and attributing outcomes to chance are linked to depressed mood.

**Adaptive styles** [1]:
- **Long-term chronically ill patients** may cling to the **sick role**. They find a "state of health" hard, complain of being rushed, and develop unexplained pain or non-adherence.
- **Acutely ill, previously well patients** may **deny** their illness, push too fast and resent the restrictions, which also leads to non-adherence.

### Pre-transplant psychosocial evaluation [1]
**Ten goals (Table 29–1; Levenson and Olbrisch):**
1. Assess **coping** and intervene.
2. Diagnose **psychiatric disorders** and plan monitoring.
3. Assess capacity to understand and give **informed consent**.
4. Assess ability to **collaborate and adhere**.
5. Assess **substance use** and the ability to stay abstinent.
6. Identify **health behaviours** (tobacco, diet, exercise).
7. Help the team understand the **patient as a person**.
8. Evaluate **social support**, finances and logistics.
9. Plan **services for patient and family** across the phases.
10. Record a **baseline of mental functioning**.

**Process:**
- Social-work screening, then psychiatric evaluation if needed.
- Collateral information; a **multidisciplinary** team.
- **Written contracts** (especially for substance use).
- **Re-evaluation** after the patient has worked on deficits.

**Instruments:**
- **SIRT** (Structured Interview for Renal Transplantation): unscored, a guide to the interview.
- **PACT, TERS, SIPAT:** heuristic rating tools.

**The consultant's role:**
- Be candid that recommendations go to the team.
- Act as an **optimiser and advocate, not a gatekeeper**: most programmes don't reject outright, but ask the patient to address problems and be re-evaluated.
- Use team discussion and the ethics committee in hard cases, and **document thoroughly**.

**Selection stringency:** heart programmes are the strictest, then liver, then **kidney (the least strict)**. This reflects organ availability, more than 30 years of experience, and the clear cost advantage over dialysis. Even so, kidney candidates with **mental disorder or non-adherence are less likely to complete work-up** (Mucsi 2017).

### Complex candidates [1]
- **Psychosis:**
  - case series show **carefully selected patients do well**;
  - prerequisites: **demonstrated adherence**, **in-residence support**, and a working relationship with the team;
  - **(kidney)** Irish registry: fewer than 1% of recipients had bipolar disorder or schizophrenia, and outcomes did **not differ**;
  - **(kidney)** USRDS (n = 39,628): psychosis needing hospitalisation (**1.5%**) was linked to **more death, graft loss and non-adherence-related graft loss**, possibly through undertreatment;
  - **restart antipsychotics early** after surgery (often overlooked).
- **Personality disorders:**
  - **10–26%** of candidates;
  - **borderline** carries the **highest non-adherence risk**; obsessional traits fit transplant demands better;
  - manage with **limited, consistent staff**, clear rules, a written contract and CBT to reduce splitting.
- **Substance use:**
  - **tobacco (kidney):** progression of kidney disease, **graft rejection and failure**, cardiovascular events, malignancy, and a **4-fold** rise in arteriopathy. Past smoking predicts graft failure. Return to smoking is **10–40%**. Check **urinary cotinine**;
  - **illicit drugs:** relapse **3.7% a year** across organs (6.1% in non-liver recipients);
  - **methadone maintenance:** forced tapering before transplant "could be considered **unethical**", because relapse rises to up to 82%.

### Post-transplant adherence: the central problem [1, 2]
- **Lifelong immunosuppression** is essential. Missing even occasional doses (**adherence below 95%**) worsens outcomes. Non-adherence causes **up to 36% of graft losses** after the early period.
- **Immunosuppressant non-adherence is highest in kidney recipients: 36 per 100 patients a year** (vs 7–15 for other organs). Across organs it is **23 per 100**.
- **Risk factors:**
  - **adolescence or young adulthood**;
  - **pre-transplant non-adherence**;
  - **depression and anxiety**; substance misuse;
  - poor coping and self-efficacy;
  - poor support and health literacy;
  - low socioeconomic status; complex regimens.
- **Rejection is often silent**, so patients stop drugs because they feel well.
- **Assessment (Table 29–2):**
  - ask routinely: "How often did you miss a dose in the past 4 weeks? More than one consecutive dose?";
  - adherence covers medication, clinic visits, self-monitoring, tests, lifestyle and avoiding substances;
  - use **multiple sources**: patient, family, coordinator, drug levels, pharmacy.
- **Interventions:**
  - **multimodal** (cognitive, educational, counselling and psychological, at patient, provider and system levels) works best;
  - **motivational interviewing** and problem-solving; apps, electronic pill monitoring, texts;
  - **booster sessions**, because adherence decays;
  - **(kidney)** a brief MI/CBT pilot improved immunosuppressant adherence (Cukor 2017) [2];
  - **(kidney)** Chang 2004: self-care education, vocational counselling and peer mentoring improved QoL and was cost-effective;
  - **adolescent kidney recipients:** medication knowledge, **pillboxes**, parental involvement;
  - **variability (SD over 2.5) in tacrolimus levels** predicts rejection.

### Psychopharmacology [1, 2]
- **After a working graft**, drug clearance returns to near normal within about a month. Doses lowered before transplant **may need raising**.
- **Delayed graft function:** dialysis needed in the **first week**; occurs in **10–25%** (up to 50% with marginal organs). Renally cleared drugs and metabolites rise, and immunosuppressant levels can be **3–6 times** higher.
- **Acute rejection:** **25–60%** of kidney recipients, mostly in the first 6 months. No psychotropic dose change is needed if it resolves quickly.
- **Chronic CNI nephrotoxicity:** renal failure in **7–21% by 5 years** (non-renal organs). Always consider renal function.

**Immunosuppressant neuropsychiatric effects:**

| Agent | Effects |
| --- | --- |
| **Cyclosporine** | Tremor, restlessness, headache. **About 12%** have serious toxicity: confusion, psychosis, seizures, speech apraxia, **cortical blindness**, coma (worse with IV and higher doses; **trough levels correlate poorly**) |
| **Tacrolimus** | Tremor, headache, insomnia, **vivid dreams**, anxiety, agitation, **akathisia**. Delirium, seizures or dysarthria in 8.4% (linked to higher levels). Higher risk with existing CNS damage |
| **Cyclosporine and tacrolimus** | **PRES:** parieto-occipital white-matter change (**MRI better than CT**), usually early; **resolves on stopping**; not level-dependent |
| **Corticosteroids** | High dose early and as "pulses" for rejection, causing mood and psychotic effects |
| Sirolimus, everolimus | No reported neurotoxicity |
| Mycophenolate | Few reports; confounded |
| **OKT3** | Severe headache, tremor, agitation, depression; cerebral oedema and encephalopathy |
| Rituximab | PML |

**Interactions:**
- Most immunosuppressants are **CYP3A4** substrates with narrow therapeutic indices.
- **Inhibitors** (strongest first): **fluvoxamine, nefazodone > fluoxetine > sertraline, TCAs, paroxetine > venlafaxine**. Nefazodone has caused **toxic CNI levels with renal failure and delirium**.
- **Inducers** (**carbamazepine, modafinil, St John's wort**) lower levels and raise the **risk of rejection**.
- **Shared side effects to avoid stacking:**
  - GI upset (over 60% on immunosuppressants), so avoid adding SSRIs or venlafaxine;
  - metabolic syndrome;
  - **QT prolongation** with tacrolimus.
- **Smoking cessation:**
  - **bupropion** with caution (seizure risk from CNIs);
  - **reduce varenicline** in renal impairment;
  - nicotine clearance changes in severe renal disease.

**Renal-impairment prescribing** (relevant before transplant, with DGF, or with graft failure) [2]:
- **SSRIs:** citalopram, escitalopram and sertraline have the fewest interactions; paroxetine clearance is reduced.
- **TCAs:** **nortriptyline or desipramine** preferred.
- **Venlafaxine and desvenlafaxine:** reduce the dose.
- **Antipsychotics:** **paliperidone** needs dose reduction.
- **Benzodiazepines:** **lorazepam or oxazepam**, at reduced dose.
- **Lithium:** contraindicated in acute renal failure. On dialysis, give **300–600 mg after each session**; check the level **2–3 hours or more after dialysis**.
- **Others:** adjust gabapentin, pregabalin and topiramate by creatinine clearance. Use **galantamine** cautiously or avoid it. Reduce **memantine** in severe impairment.

### Living kidney donors [1]
**Evaluation** (mandatory in the US; Table 29–3):
- **knowledge** of the risks;
- **motivation and ambivalence**; **coercion or inducement**;
- attitudes of family; support and **finances**;
- psychiatric, personality, pain and trauma history; substance use;
- **capacity to consent**;
- an **independent living-donor advocate**.

**Outcomes:**
- Mortality is below 1%.
- Kidney donors miss **4–6 weeks of work**; laparoscopic nephrectomy speeds recovery.
- 30% of kidney donors lose more than **$2,500**.
- Most donors **do not regret** donating, and QoL equals or exceeds the general population.
- **Kidney donor depression** matches general-population rates. Predictors: non-white race, younger age, slow recovery, **financial burden**, and **feeling morally obligated**.
- **Non-directed (altruistic) donors** (1–2%) are evaluated with extra caution. In one study 16% of those fully evaluated were declined for psychosocial reasons, yet their outcomes equal those of directed donors.

**Conclusion.** [Certain, per the source] The psychiatrist's job in renal transplantation is to:
- **optimise rather than gatekeep**, through structured pre-transplant evaluation;
- treat the **high pre-existing burden** of depression, anxiety, cognitive impairment and non-adherence carried over from dialysis;
- **prevent immunosuppressant non-adherence**, which is commonest in kidney recipients and a leading cause of late graft loss;
- recognise **CNI and steroid neurotoxicity, including PRES**;
- prescribe around **CYP3A4 interactions and changing renal function**;
- protect **living donors** from coercion.

Carefully selected patients with severe mental illness can be transplanted successfully.

## In depth

### Tools named in the source [1]
- **Transplant-specific:**
  - **PACT** (Psychosocial Assessment of Candidates for Transplantation);
  - **TERS** (Transplant Evaluation Rating Scale);
  - **SIPAT** (Stanford Integrated Psychosocial Assessment for Transplant);
  - **SIRT** (Structured Interview for Renal Transplantation).
- **For living donors:** Live Donor Assessment Tool; Donor Cognition Instrument.

### Psychotherapeutic formats [1]
- Individual therapy for transplant themes.
- **Telephone therapy** for wait-listed patients.
- **Peer mentoring:** introduce the mentor early and match by demographics.
- **Group therapy** (Abbey and Farrow): pre- vs post-transplant; organ-specific or mixed; patients and caregivers. It improved affect and QoL and reduced calls to coordinators.
- Internet interventions (heart) reduced distress and improved adherence.

### Depression and outcomes [1]
- Across organs, **depression before or after transplant predicts poorer survival** (meta-analysis, Dew 2015) and death-censored graft loss.
- Anxiety was not significantly linked.
- Whether treating depression improves survival is unclear; one study suggested benefit.

### Dialysis-phase interventions for adherence and depression [2]
- **CBT** improved fluid adherence (interdialytic weight gain) and mood.
- Educational interventions alone are questionable: in one study, better phosphate knowledge went with worse adherence.
- **Antidepressant evidence in ESRD is weak:** Cochrane 2016 found 4 small trials; a larger sertraline trial was negative. In CKD without ESRD, sertraline equalled placebo (Hedayati 2017).
