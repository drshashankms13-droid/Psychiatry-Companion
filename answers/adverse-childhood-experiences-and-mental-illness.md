## Exam answer

**Definition.** **Adverse childhood experiences (ACEs)** are potentially traumatic events and chronic stressors occurring before age 18. The original ACE study grouped them into three domains:
- **Abuse:** emotional, physical, sexual.
- **Neglect:** emotional, physical.
- **Household dysfunction:** a household member with substance misuse or mental illness; domestic violence against the mother; parental separation or divorce; an incarcerated household member.

The broader term **childhood adversity** also includes poverty, bullying, community violence, war, displacement and institutional care [1, 2].

**Trauma**, as defined by SAMHSA, is an event or set of circumstances experienced as physically or emotionally harmful or life-threatening, with lasting effects on functioning and well-being [3].

### Mechanism
ACEs → chronic or toxic stress during **sensitive periods** of brain development → several pathways:
1. **HPA axis dysregulation:** sustained cortisol and CRH; glucocorticoid receptor (NR3C1) and **FKBP5 methylation** → altered stress reactivity [1, 3].
2. **Brain changes:** smaller hippocampus, amygdala hyper-reactivity, weaker prefrontal–limbic connectivity, delayed myelination [1].
3. **Inflammation:** raised CRP, IL-6, TNF-α; telomere shortening and "weathering" [1, 3].
4. **Attachment and psychological pathways:** insecure or disorganised attachment (Bowlby); poor mentalisation (Fonagy); negative self-schemas; emotion dysregulation [1].
5. **Behavioural pathways:** "maladaptive coping" (smoking, alcohol, drugs, risky sex, disordered eating) → physical and mental illness and early death [3].
6. **Social pathways:** school failure, poverty, revictimisation; intergenerational transmission (including epigenetic) [1, 3].

### Associated adult outcomes

| Outcome | Strength of association |
| --- | --- |
| Depression (earlier onset, more recurrent, poorer treatment response) | About 4-fold with ≥4 ACEs [1, 2] |
| Suicide attempts | About **12-fold** with ≥4 ACEs [1, 2] |
| PTSD and **complex PTSD**; dissociative disorders | Strong [1] |
| Substance use disorders | Up to 7–10-fold for ≥4 ACEs [2] |
| Borderline and antisocial personality disorder | Strong [1] |
| Psychosis | Childhood adversity OR about 2.8 [4] |
| Anxiety, eating disorders, somatic symptom disorders | Moderate |
| Physical illness: ischaemic heart disease, COPD, cancer, autoimmune disease, shortened lifespan | Graded [2, 3] |

**Key features of the association:**
- **Dose–response:** the more ACEs, the higher the risk.
- **Non-specific (multifinality):** the same adversity can lead to many different disorders.
- Risk is moderated by genes (e.g., FKBP5 × abuse), timing and protective factors [1, 2].

### Assessment
- Ask about adversity routinely in the developmental history, sensitively and in private, normalising the question [3].
- **Tools:** ACE questionnaire (10 items); WHO ACE-IQ; Childhood Trauma Questionnaire (CTQ, 28 items, 5 subscales); CECA interview.
- Screen for PTSD or complex PTSD (PCL-5, ITQ), dissociation, suicidality and substance use. Check current safety, including intimate partner violence [3].

### Management
- **Prevention (public health):**
  - Home visiting and parenting programmes.
  - Maternal mental health care.
  - Poverty reduction.
  - School-based programmes.
  - Child protection: POCSO, Child Welfare Committees, Childline 1098 [1].
- **Early intervention in children:** trauma-focused CBT (TF-CBT), parent–child interaction therapy, a safe and stable caregiver.
- **Adults:**
  - **Trauma-informed care** as a universal precaution, using SAMHSA's 4 Rs and 6 principles [3].
  - Trauma-focused therapies (TF-CBT, prolonged exposure, CPT, EMDR) for PTSD.
  - Phase-based treatment (stabilisation, e.g., STAIR, before processing) for complex PTSD.
  - DBT or mentalisation-based treatment for BPD.
  - Treat co-morbid depression and substance use.
- **Build resilience:** one consistent caring adult, positive childhood experiences, education and social support [1].

### Key evidence
- **Felitti et al. 1998 ACE study** (Kaiser Permanente, about 9,500 adults): ACEs were common, and adult outcomes showed a strong **graded, dose–response relationship** with them [1, 3].
- **Hughes et al. 2017** (Lancet Public Health, 37 studies): ≥4 ACEs strongly predicted problematic drug use and interpersonal or self-directed violence [2].
- **Varese et al. 2012:** childhood adversity raised psychosis risk (OR 2.8; population attributable fraction about 33%) [4].
- **Green et al. 2010** (US National Comorbidity Survey Replication): childhood adversities were associated with about 45% of childhood-onset and about 26–32% of later-onset disorders. The WHO World Mental Health surveys (Kessler 2010, 21 countries) attributed about 30% of all disorders to childhood adversities [2].

**Conclusion.** Childhood adversity is one of the largest **modifiable** risk factors for adult mental and physical illness. It acts through biological embedding of stress, attachment and behavioural pathways, with a dose–response effect. Its effects are probabilistic, not deterministic: resilience is common. Psychiatrists should take a developmental trauma history, practise trauma-informed care and advocate for child protection and early-years investment.

## In depth

### Background
- **Freud** (1896 seduction theory) first linked childhood sexual trauma to adult neurosis, then retreated from it.
- **Bowlby** (1944, *Forty-four juvenile thieves*; later attachment theory) showed that early separation and maternal deprivation have lasting effects. **Spitz** described hospitalism and anaclitic depression.
- **Harlow's** monkeys and **Meaney's** rat studies of licking and grooming (Weaver et al. 2004) showed that maternal care epigenetically programmes glucocorticoid receptor expression.
- Recognition of **PTSD** in Vietnam veterans (DSM-III, 1980) and of intimate partner violence broadened awareness of the health effects of trauma [3].
- **1998:** Felitti and Anda's ACE study made childhood adversity a public-health concern. It was later replicated across all US states (BRFSS) [3].
- The **"weathering" hypothesis** (Geronimus) and Holocaust or 9/11 offspring studies suggested that stress can also be transmitted across generations [3].

### Epidemiology
- In the ACE study, about two-thirds of mostly middle-class insured adults reported at least one ACE, and about 1 in 8 reported ≥4 [1, 3].
- **NCS-R and WHO World Mental Health surveys (Green et al. 2010; Kessler et al. 2010):** childhood adversities are highly inter-correlated. Maladaptive family functioning (abuse, neglect, parental mental illness, substance use, violence) accounts for most of the effect [2].
- **India:** the Ministry of Women and Child Development *Study on Child Abuse* (2007) reported that about two-thirds of children experienced physical abuse and about half reported some form of sexual abuse. Studies using ACE-IQ in Indian adolescents and young adults report high rates of exposure, especially to household dysfunction and community violence. Figures vary widely by sample, so check the original sources before quoting numbers.
- **Risk factors for ACEs:** poverty, parental mental illness or substance use, domestic violence, single or very young parenthood, disability in the child, conflict and displacement [1, 2].

### Classification and criteria
- **ICD-11:**
  - **PTSD (6B40)** and **complex PTSD (6B41)**. Complex PTSD adds disturbances in self-organisation (affect dysregulation, negative self-concept, relational difficulties) and is typically linked to prolonged or repeated trauma such as childhood abuse.
  - Prolonged grief disorder.
  - The "maltreatment" Z/Q codes (QE82 personal history of maltreatment).
- **DSM-5-TR:** trauma- and stressor-related disorders, including reactive attachment disorder and disinhibited social engagement disorder in children; and "Other conditions that may be a focus of clinical attention" (child abuse and neglect codes).

### Mechanism in detail

| System | Finding | Example evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| HPA axis | Early hyper-reactivity, then often blunted cortisol; altered GR sensitivity | NR3C1 promoter methylation in suicide victims abused in childhood (McGowan 2009) [1] |
| Gene × environment | FKBP5 risk alleles + abuse → PTSD; allele-specific demethylation (Klengel 2013) | Supports epigenetic embedding [1] |
| Inflammation | Raised CRP in adults maltreated as children (Dunedin; Danese 2007) | Links ACEs to depression and cardiometabolic disease [1, 3] |
| Brain structure | ↓ hippocampal volume, ↓ PFC grey matter, ↑ amygdala reactivity; Teicher's "ecophenotype" of maltreated depression | Maltreated patients with depression differ biologically and respond less to treatment |
| Development | Institutional deprivation → structural brain changes and later disorder (Bucharest Early Intervention Project) [1] | Foster care before about 2 years improved outcomes |
| Psychological | Insecure or disorganised attachment; mentalisation deficits; negative schemas; hypervigilant threat processing ("latent vulnerability") | BPD, PTSD, chronic anxiety [1] |
| Autonomic | Sympathetic hyperarousal and narrowed "window of tolerance"; dorsal vagal "freeze" and dissociation [3] | Basis of grounding and co-regulation techniques |

### Clinical features in detail
The **maltreated ecophenotype** shows:
- earlier onset, more chronic and recurrent course;
- more co-morbidity (substance use, personality, PTSD);
- more suicidality;
- poorer response to antidepressants and psychotherapy (Nanni 2012: maltreatment predicts recurrent or persistent depression and non-response).

Adults may present not with trauma but with somatic complaints, repeated self-harm, relationship instability, dissociation, chronic pain or difficulty with medical examinations [3].

### Assessment in detail

| Tool | Format | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ACE questionnaire | 10 yes/no items, self-report | Quick, sums exposure; ignores severity, timing and protective factors |
| WHO ACE-IQ | 29 items; adds bullying, community and collective violence | Designed for global and LMIC use |
| CTQ-SF | 28 items, 5 subscales (EA, PA, SA, EN, PN) + minimisation scale | Widely used in research |
| CECA | Semi-structured interview | Gold standard for detail; time-consuming |
| ITQ | 12 items | Diagnoses ICD-11 PTSD and complex PTSD |

**How to ask:**
- Ask as part of the social history, in private.
- Listen generously without pressing for details.
- Thank the person and acknowledge their strength.
- Agree with them what to document [3].

### Differential: how to interpret ACE-related presentations

| Presentation | Consider |
| --- | --- |
| Chronic emotional dysregulation, self-harm | Complex PTSD vs BPD (often co-occur) |
| Hallucinations in trauma survivors | PTSD with dissociative or psychotic features vs primary psychosis |
| Memory gaps, depersonalisation | Dissociative disorder |
| Medically unexplained symptoms | Bodily distress disorder; trauma-related somatisation |
| Attention problems in maltreated child | Trauma-related hyperarousal vs ADHD |

### Management details
- **Trauma-informed care (organisational level)** [3]:
  - **SAMHSA 4 Rs:** **Realise** the impact of trauma; **Recognise** signs in patients and staff; **Respond** by building trauma knowledge into policy; **Resist re-traumatisation**.
  - **6 principles:** safety; trustworthiness and transparency; peer support; collaboration and mutuality; empowerment, voice and choice; attention to cultural, historical and gender issues.
- **In the encounter** [3]:
  - Introduce yourself; keep eye level; no barriers; clear exits.
  - Explain and ask permission before each step of an examination; drape properly; use clinical, non-personalising language.
  - Offer a chaperone.
  - If the patient dissociates, stop the examination, re-orient and reschedule.
  - Offer **square breathing** (4-4-4-4) for co-regulation.
  - For telemedicine, confirm privacy and agree a safe word.
- **Pharmacotherapy:** SSRIs or venlafaxine for PTSD and depression. Prazosin for nightmares has mixed evidence. Avoid routine benzodiazepines.
- **Children:**
  - TF-CBT for 3–18-year-olds.
  - Child–parent psychotherapy for under-5s.
  - Mandatory POCSO reporting.
  - Coordination with the Child Welfare Committee.
- **Clinician well-being:** vicarious trauma and burnout are real. Supervision and organisational support are needed [3].

### Critical appraisal
- **Retrospective recall bias:** current depression colours memories. Prospective and retrospective reports identify different people. **Subjective experience** of adversity predicts psychopathology better than objective records (Danese & Widom 2020).
- **Confounding:** shared genes (passive gene–environment correlation), poverty, and parental psychopathology can explain part of the association. Twin and sibling designs reduce, but do not eliminate, effects.
- **ACE score limitations:** it is a crude count. It gives equal weight to divorce and sexual abuse, ignores severity, chronicity, timing and protective factors, and omits poverty, discrimination and bullying. It is **not valid as an individual screening or prediction tool**.
- **Determinism risk:** most people with ACEs do not develop disorders. Labelling can stigmatise or fatalise; a resilience framework is needed [1].
- **The linear model is too simple.** A web-like model with intersecting experiences and exit points is more accurate [3].

### Indian context
- **POCSO Act, 2012:** mandatory reporting (Section 19); child-friendly procedures; Special Courts. Psychiatrists assess and support child victims.
- **Juvenile Justice (Care and Protection of Children) Act, 2015:** Child Welfare Committees; alternatives to institutional care (foster care, adoption), which is relevant given the BEIP evidence.
- **Other provisions:** Childline 1098 (now integrated with ERSS-112); RBSK screening; RKSK adolescent health; Mission Vatsalya (child protection services).
- **Common Indian adversities:**
  - Corporal punishment at home and school.
  - Child marriage.
  - Child labour.
  - Parental alcohol misuse with domestic violence.
  - Migration and separation.
  - Exposure to communal or regional conflict (e.g., Kashmir, Manipur).
  - Academic pressure.
- **Services:** NIMHANS child and adolescent psychiatry; SAMVAD (NIMHANS) for children in difficult circumstances and child protection; the Tele-MANAS helpline.

### Recent advances
- **Established:** trauma-informed care as a "universal precaution" in health systems [3]; ICD-11 complex PTSD.
- **Emerging:**
  - **Positive childhood experiences (PCEs)** as protective factors that offset ACE risk.
  - Polygenic × adversity models.
  - Epigenetic clocks (accelerated ageing after adversity).
  - Inflammation-targeted treatment for maltreated depression (investigational).
  - Interventions aimed at intergenerational transmission (parental mental health in pregnancy).
- **Debate:** whether ACE screening in primary care improves outcomes. Evidence is currently insufficient, so the focus should be on response and support, not screening alone.
