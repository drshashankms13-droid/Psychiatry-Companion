## Exam answer

**Definition.** Cognitive impairment is a **core, trait-like feature** of schizophrenia, separate from positive and negative symptoms. It is a deficit in neurocognition (attention, memory, processing speed, executive function) and social cognition. On average, patients perform about **1 SD below** controls on individual domains and **1.5–2 SD below** on composite scores. It is present before psychosis begins, largely stable afterwards, and is the strongest predictor of functional outcome. Antipsychotics barely improve it [1,2].

### Mechanism
Genetic liability (strong negative genetic correlation between schizophrenia risk and cognitive ability) + environment (obstetric complications, childhood trauma, deprivation, cannabis) → neurodevelopmental disruption → **cortical excitation/inhibition (E/I) imbalance**: parvalbumin GABA interneuron deficit (low GAD67), NMDA hypofunction, **cortical hypodopaminergia** (D1), and reduced muscarinic M1/M4 signalling → disrupted gamma oscillations and network function → impaired working memory, learning and executive function [1].

### Profile of deficits (MATRICS domains)

| Domain | Typical deficit | Example test |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Processing speed | Large (partly drug-related) | BACS symbol coding, Trail Making A |
| Attention/vigilance | Moderate–large | Continuous Performance Test |
| Working memory | Large | Letter–number span |
| Verbal learning and memory | **Largest; best distinguishes patients** (Bilder 2000) | Hopkins Verbal Learning Test |
| Visual learning and memory | Moderate | Brief Visuospatial Memory Test |
| Reasoning/problem-solving (executive) | Moderate–large | NAB Mazes, WCST |
| Social cognition | Moderate–large (theory of mind, emotion recognition, attribution bias) | MSCEIT |

The first-episode study by **Bilder 2000** (n=94) found a generalised deficit of **1.53 SD**, with additional relative deficits in memory and executive function. Executive and attention deficits were most linked to poor functional outcome [3].

### Course
Present in childhood (global deficits, delayed milestones) → widening in adolescence (slower development) → present in the clinical high-risk state (worst in those who convert) → at first episode, ~1.5 SD → **stable** thereafter (Heaton 2001, n=142, mean follow-up 3 years and up to 10) → earlier age-related decline and higher dementia risk after 65, linked to smoking, obesity, diabetes and inactivity [1,3].

### Assessment
- **MATRICS Consensus Cognitive Battery (MCCB)** (7 domains); BACS (brief, ~35 minutes); MoCA as a screen.
- Social cognition battery; functional capacity (UCSD Performance-based Skills Assessment, UPSA).
- Exclude confounders: anticholinergics, benzodiazepines, sedation, depression, substance use, metabolic disease.

### Management
1. Remove harmful contributors: reduce anticholinergic drugs (improves cognition) and benzodiazepines; use the lowest effective antipsychotic dose; avoid polypharmacy [1].
2. Treat depression, sleep problems and physical illness; stop smoking; exercise (aerobic exercise improves cognition).
3. **Cognitive remediation therapy (CRT)**, combined with vocational rehabilitation: moderate effect on cognition and small–moderate effect on function [1].
4. Social cognition training (SCIT); IPS supported employment with cognitive adaptations.
5. No licensed drug. Investigational options: xanomeline–trospium (M1/M4), TAAR1 agonists, cannabidiol, memantine. Cholinesterase inhibitors and glutamate agents have failed [1].

### Key evidence
- **Heinrichs & Zakzanis 1998:** meta-analysis; large effect sizes across domains.
- **Bilder 2000:** first-episode generalised deficit of 1.5 SD; memory most discriminating [3].
- **Heaton 2001:** deficits stable over 1.6 and 5 years [3].
- **Green 1996/2000:** cognition predicts functional outcome more than positive symptoms do.
- **Wykes 2011; Vita 2021:** CRT meta-analyses; effect on cognition d ≈ 0.45, on function d ≈ 0.4.

**Conclusion.** Cognitive impairment is a central, enduring and disabling dimension of schizophrenia. It is neurodevelopmental in origin and largely untouched by D2-blocking drugs. Routine cognitive assessment and remediation should be part of standard care. New drugs that act on muscarinic and E/I mechanisms are the most promising pharmacological avenue.

## In depth

### Background
- **Kraepelin (1919)** named the illness *dementia praecox*, placing cognitive decline at its centre. **Bleuler** emphasised loosening of associations and attention.
- From the 1980s, neuropsychology (Saykin 1991, Heinrichs 1998) showed deficits across domains.
- Green (1996) linked cognition to function.
- The **NIMH MATRICS initiative (2003–2008)** produced the MCCB and a regulatory path for cognitive drugs. TURNS (Treatment Units for Research on Neurocognition in Schizophrenia) tested candidate drugs.
- DSM-5 considered, but did not add, cognitive impairment as a diagnostic criterion because it is not specific enough (it also occurs in bipolar disorder) [1].

### Epidemiology
- About 80% of patients perform below their expected premorbid level. About 25–30% have scores in the "normal" range but below what would be predicted for them ("neuropsychologically normal" subgroup).
- Unaffected first-degree relatives show milder deficits (an endophenotype).
- 80–90% of people with schizophrenia are unemployed for most of adult life; cognitive and negative symptoms explain much of this [1].
- About 65% smoke. Nicotine briefly improves sensory gating and attention, but long-term smoking worsens cognition and late-life decline [1].

### Classification and criteria
- ICD-11 includes a **"cognitive symptoms" specifier** among the symptom specifiers for primary psychotic disorders (6A25.5), rated as none, mild, moderate or severe.
- DSM-5-TR uses the Clinician-Rated Dimensions of Psychosis Symptom Severity, which includes impaired cognition (Section III).
- **Neurocognition** (attention, memory, speed, executive) vs **social cognition** (emotion processing, theory of mind, social perception, attributional style). Social cognition is separable and has its own effect on function [1].
- **Relation to PANSS factors:** cognition is distinct from positive and negative factors and only modestly related to the disorganisation factor [1].

### Mechanism in detail

| System | Abnormality | Cognitive effect |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Dopamine | Striatal hyperdopaminergia (positive symptoms); **cortical hypodopaminergia** (D1) [1] | Working memory, executive dysfunction |
| Glutamate | NMDA hypofunction on interneurons (ketamine model; anti-NMDAR encephalitis) [1] | Disinhibition, impaired plasticity |
| GABA | Low GAD65/67, fewer parvalbumin interneurons [1] | Abnormal gamma oscillations |
| Acetylcholine | Reduced M1/M4 receptors (post-mortem, PET); anticholinergic drugs worsen cognition [1] | Attention, memory |
| Synaptic | Reduced dendritic spines and synaptic density (SV2A PET) [1] | Pyramidal hypofunction |
| Networks | Aberrant resting gamma power, reduced task-induced gamma; reduced MMN; altered fMRI networks [1] | Working memory precision |

There is an open question about whether interneuron loss is primary (so boost inhibition) or compensatory for weak pyramidal activity (so boosting inhibition would harm). The answer will shape drug development [1].

**Genetics:** polygenic risk for schizophrenia correlates negatively with cognitive ability; bipolar-specific alleles do not (they may even be linked to better cognition). Within patients, cognition tracks polygenic scores for IQ and educational attainment rather than schizophrenia polygenic score [1].

### Clinical features in detail
- **Subjective vs objective deficits:** weakly correlated. Poor insight and disorganisation reduce self-awareness of deficits. Subjective complaints are more common in people at clinical high risk and in those with depression [1].
- **Heterogeneity:** variability between and within individuals is greater than in controls. Intellectually "preserved" and "deteriorated" subgroups exist [1].
- **Premorbid vs current IQ:** patients matched on current IQ score higher on premorbid-sensitive tests (e.g., reading) but lower on working memory, which suggests decline from their premorbid level [1].
- **Functional consequences:** cognition correlates with UPSA functional capacity (r = 0.4–0.8) more than with interview-based measures (r = 0.1–0.3). It also predicts adherence, admission and length of stay [1].

### Assessment in detail

| Tool | Notes |
| --- | --- |
| MCCB | Gold standard; 10 tests, 7 domains, ~65 minutes; Indian norms are limited |
| BACS | ~35 minutes; sensitive; Hindi and Kannada versions available |
| NIMHANS Neuropsychology Battery | Indian norms; widely used in Indian practice |
| Schizophrenia Cognition Rating Scale (SCoRS) | Interview-based, informant |
| MoCA / ACE-III | Screening, especially in older patients |
| UPSA | Functional capacity |
| Social cognition: TASIT, Hinting Task, Faux Pas, facial emotion recognition (e.g., TRENDS, Indian) | Social cognition |

Medical work-up when decline is atypical: thyroid, B12, HIV/VDRL, MRI (vascular, other), sleep apnoea and metabolic syndrome.

### Differential diagnosis

| Cause | Clue |
| --- | --- |
| Primary schizophrenia cognitive deficit | Premorbid onset, stable, generalised |
| Drug effect | Anticholinergic load, benzodiazepines, high-dose antipsychotics; improves on reduction [1] |
| Depression (post-psychotic) | Subjective complaints, slowness, low mood |
| Negative symptoms | Apathy affects effort on tests; effort testing helps |
| Substance use | Cannabis, alcohol, inhalants |
| Comorbid neurological disease or dementia | Progressive late decline, focal signs |
| Metabolic/vascular | Diabetes, hypertension, metabolic syndrome worsen cognition [1] |
| Intellectual disability with psychosis | Lifelong low IQ, adaptive deficits from childhood |

### Management details
- **Antipsychotic choice:** all antipsychotics have similarly small cognitive effects. Early claims for "atypicals" were trial artefacts. Cariprazine showed some advantage on PANSS cognitive items [1].
- **Anticholinergic burden:** calculate (Anticholinergic Cognitive Burden scale); taper trihexyphenidyl where possible [1].
- **Cognitive remediation:** computer-assisted or therapist-led (e.g., NEAR, CIRCuiTS); 20–40 sessions; best combined with psychosocial rehabilitation and active strategy coaching.
- **Exercise:** aerobic exercise 2–3 times a week improves global cognition (Firth 2017 meta-analysis).
- **Physical health:** metformin and weight control, diabetes and BP control, smoking cessation (which improves cognition) [1].
- **Sleep:** treat insomnia and sleep apnoea [1].
- **Older adults:** screen for dementia; avoid anticholinergics.

### Critical appraisal
- **Practice effects** inflate apparent improvement in longitudinal and CRT studies [1].
- **Global vs specific:** the deficit may be mostly generalised; current tests may be too coarse to isolate specific processes [1].
- **Processing speed** is partly a drug effect. In drug-naïve samples it is no worse than verbal or working memory deficits [1].
- **Failed drug development:** cholinesterase inhibitors, nicotinic agonists (varenicline), mGlu2/3 agonists, glycine agents and memantine have not worked in large trials [1].
- **Measurement:** cognition is rarely assessed in routine care because of time and training constraints [1].
- **Specificity:** cognitive deficits are more severe and earlier in schizophrenia than in bipolar disorder or depression, but not unique [1].

### Indian context
- NIMHANS and PGIMER have contributed neuropsychological norms and studies; the **NIMHANS Neuropsychology Battery** is the Indian standard.
- Cognitive remediation adapted for Indian settings has been trialled (NIMHANS); group and family-assisted formats suit limited resources.
- Disability certification under the **RPwD Act 2016** uses IDEAS. Cognitive disability can support benefit claims.
- Low education and literacy affect test norms; use culturally adapted tests.
- Better employment outcomes in Indian cohorts (Madras: 76% of men working at 20 years) show that informal jobs can accommodate cognitive limits [4].

### Recent advances
- **Established:** cognitive remediation as a recommended intervention (EPA 2021 guidance).
- **Emerging:**
  - **Xanomeline–trospium** (approved for schizophrenia, 2024), with cognitive signals in post hoc analyses.
  - **Iclepertin (BI 425809)**, a GlyT1 inhibitor. *Check current status*: the phase 3 CONNEX programme reported negative results in 2025.
  - TAAR1 agonists (ulotaront, ralmitaront), whose development is uncertain after phase 3 failures.
- **Investigational:** cannabidiol, M1-selective positive allosteric modulators, rTMS (minimal effect) [1,5], digital cognitive training, and E/I biomarker-guided treatment.
