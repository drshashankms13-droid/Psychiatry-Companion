## Exam answer

**Case summary.** A 27-year-old obese woman has 6 years of suspiciousness and self-absorbed behaviour. She now presents with severe agitation, suspiciousness, hallucinations and **intense suicidal ideas**. She refuses admission and medication because of past **galactorrhoea**, which was most likely antipsychotic-induced hyperprolactinaemia. The likely diagnosis is **schizophrenia** (paranoid, multiple episodes or continuous), with high risk, impaired capacity, a valid adverse-effect concern, and sex-specific issues (obesity, prolactin, reproductive health).

### (a) What additional information is needed to make the diagnosis?

| Area | Information required | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Onset and course | Insidious or acute; continuous or episodic; interepisode function; deterioration in work/self-care | Schizophrenia vs ATPD vs mood disorder |
| Phenomenology | Nature of voices (third-person, running commentary), delusions (persecutory, reference, control), thought disorder, negative symptoms ("self-absorbed" = autism/withdrawal) | ICD-11/DSM criteria |
| Mood symptoms | Depressive or manic episodes, their timing relative to psychosis; post-psychotic depression | Schizoaffective, bipolar or psychotic depression; suicide risk |
| Suicide | Intent, plan, means, past attempts, command hallucinations, hopelessness, access to pesticides or drugs | Risk management |
| Menstrual and reproductive history | Menstrual relation of symptoms (perimenstrual worsening), menarche, pregnancy and **postpartum** episodes, contraception, current pregnancy possibility, lactation | Women's psychosis course; drug choice [1] |
| Galactorrhoea details | Which drug, dose, duration; amenorrhoea, sexual dysfunction; whether it resolved on stopping | Prolactin-elevating drug (risperidone, amisulpride, FGA) [2] |
| Treatment history | Drugs, doses, duration, adherence, response, side effects (weight gain with olanzapine?), LAI use | Treatment resistance; choice of drug |
| Substance use | Cannabis, alcohol, stimulants | Substance-induced psychosis |
| Medical | Thyroid, epilepsy, head injury, autoimmune symptoms; **causes of obesity and hyperprolactinaemia** (PCOS, hypothyroidism, pituitary tumour — headache, visual field defects) | Organic exclusion |
| Family history | Psychosis, mood disorder, suicide | Genetic risk |
| Premorbid personality and social context | Schizoid/schizotypal traits; marital status, stressors, abuse, support | Prognosis; safeguarding |
| Insight and capacity | Understanding of illness and treatment; ability to weigh information | MHCA 2017 decisions |

**Investigations:** CBC, glucose/HbA1c, lipids, LFT/RFT, TSH, **serum prolactin** (morning, minimal stress), **pregnancy test (β-hCG)**, urine drug screen, ECG; MRI pituitary if prolactin is very high (>3,000 mIU/L) without a causative drug, or if there are visual symptoms [2].

### (b) Acute and long-term management

**Acute phase**
1. **Safety first:** risk of suicide and agitation. Do not discharge an acutely suicidal, psychotic patient without a safety plan. Observe and remove means.
2. **Legal (MHCA 2017):**
   - If she lacks capacity and has severe illness with risk of self-harm, use **supported admission** (Section 89, up to 30 days; nominated representative involved).
   - **Emergency treatment** (Section 94, up to 72 hours) for imminent risk.
   - Respect any **advance directive** where valid. Document capacity assessment. She has the right to information and to be heard about galactorrhoea.
3. **Engagement:** acknowledge her galactorrhoea experience as real and offer a **prolactin-sparing drug**. This turns refusal into negotiation.
4. **Rapid tranquillisation if needed:** oral lorazepam 1–2 mg, or oral aripiprazole/quetiapine. IM: lorazepam ± promethazine. Avoid IM haloperidol if possible (raises prolactin, EPS) but use it if necessary for safety [2].
5. **Antipsychotic choice:** **aripiprazole** (10–30 mg) is prolactin-sparing, weight-neutral and available as an LAI. Alternatives: quetiapine (prolactin-sparing but weight gain), cariprazine, brexpiprazole. **Avoid** risperidone, paliperidone, amisulpride and FGAs (high prolactin) and olanzapine/clozapine first (obesity) [2,3].
6. **Suicidality:** close observation. ECT is an option if suicide risk is extreme or catatonia develops. Treat comorbid depression. Clozapine reduces suicidality in schizophrenia (InterSePT) if resistance is established.

**Long-term**
- Maintenance antipsychotic at the lowest effective dose; consider **aripiprazole LAI** if adherence is poor.
- Prolactin monitoring: baseline, at 3 months, then yearly; ask about menstrual and sexual symptoms [2].
- Metabolic monitoring and management: weight, waist, HbA1c, lipids; lifestyle programme; **metformin** if weight rises or BMI ≥30 [3].
- Bone health: calcium/vitamin D, weight-bearing exercise; DEXA if there was long-standing hyperprolactinaemia [2].
- Reproductive health: contraception counselling (unplanned pregnancy rates 24–48% in women with schizophrenia); pre-conception planning; folate [1].
- Psychosocial: CBTp, family psychoeducation, vocational rehabilitation, suicide prevention plan, crisis contacts (Tele-MANAS 14416).
- Hormonal course: watch for perimenstrual, postpartum and perimenopausal worsening. Consider adjunctive **raloxifene** (60–120 mg) or oestradiol in women with persisting symptoms, especially peri- or post-menopausal (investigational/off-label) [1].
- If two adequate trials fail → **clozapine** (with metformin co-commenced).

### (c) Long-term course and outcome
- **Schizophrenia in women:** later onset (about 5 years later than men; second peak after 40), more affective symptoms, fewer negative symptoms, better social and occupational functioning, and better treatment response before menopause. Worsening occurs perimenstrually, postpartum and after menopause [1].
- **General course:** heterogeneous. Madras 20-year data showed only about 8% continuously ill and 8% fully recovered; most had relapses with full or partial remission; no sex difference in course; GAF >60 in about 75% [4].
- **Poor prognostic factors here:** 6-year duration with insidious onset (suspiciousness, self-absorption → long DUP), current suicidality, poor adherence due to side effects, obesity and metabolic risk, possible poor insight.
- **Good prognostic factors:** female sex, a specific and correctable reason for refusal, response to past treatment (if any).
- **Mortality:** suicide (about 5% lifetime) and cardiovascular disease (obesity) are the main threats. In Madras, 7 of 16 deaths were suicides in patients under 35 [4].

**Conclusion.** Manage the immediate suicide and agitation risk using MHCA 2017 provisions where necessary. Engage her by validating the galactorrhoea complaint and choosing a prolactin-sparing, weight-neutral drug such as aripiprazole, preferably later as an LAI. Then build long-term care around metabolic, bone and reproductive health and the hormonal life course specific to women.

## In depth

### Background
Sex differences in schizophrenia were noted by Kraepelin. Seeman (1980s) and Häfner developed the **oestrogen protection hypothesis**: later onset in women and a second peak at menopause suggest oestrogen buffers vulnerability. Kulkarni's group (from 1996) tested adjunctive oestradiol and SERMs [1].

### Epidemiology
- Male:female incidence ≈1.4:1. Peak onset 20–29 in men; women about 5 years later (20–39), with higher incidence than men after 40 [1].
- Women attempt suicide more often; men complete suicide more often [1].
- 20–32% of women report perimenstrual exacerbation; admissions rise in the perimenstrual phase [1].
- More women than men need admission after age 45, coinciding with perimenopause (Sommer 2023) [1].
- Hyperprolactinaemia in 30–70% of patients on antipsychotics [1].

### Classification and criteria
- **ICD-11 6A20 Schizophrenia:** at least 1 month of two or more core symptoms (persistent delusions, persistent hallucinations, disorganised thinking, experiences of influence/passivity, negative symptoms, grossly disorganised behaviour, psychomotor disturbance), at least one from the first four. Specifiers: episode course; symptom domains including positive, negative, depressive, cognitive.
- **DSM-5-TR:** 6-month duration including at least 1 month of active symptoms; functional decline.
- **Hyperprolactinaemia:** above the laboratory upper limit (typically >25 ng/mL in women); symptomatic vs asymptomatic.

### Mechanism in detail
- **Oestrogen and psychosis:** oestradiol modulates D2 sensitivity, serotonergic and glutamatergic systems, and hippocampal plasticity. It is neuroprotective (anti-excitotoxic, antioxidant, mitochondrial). Falling levels (perimenstrual, postpartum, menopause) lead to symptom worsening. Low oestrogen also reduces CYP activity, changing drug levels [1].
- **Antipsychotic hyperprolactinaemia:** D2 blockade in the tuberoinfundibular pathway removes dopamine's inhibition of lactotrophs → raised prolactin → GnRH suppression → low oestrogen (amenorrhoea, infertility, bone loss) and galactorrhoea. **Low oestrogen may itself worsen psychosis**, creating a vicious cycle [1,2].
- **Stress hyperprolactinaemia:** drug-naïve patients may have raised prolactin from stress; this may be stronger in women [1].

### Clinical features in detail
- Women: more affective symptoms, comorbid depression and anxiety, better verbal memory [1].
- Hyperprolactinaemia features: galactorrhoea, oligo/amenorrhoea, breast enlargement, sexual dysfunction, reduced libido, infertility, possibly delusions of pregnancy; long-term osteoporosis and possibly breast cancer [2].
- Obesity in schizophrenia: from illness, lifestyle and drugs. Women with PCOS may also have raised prolactin.

### Assessment in detail
- **Prolactin interpretation:** take blood in the morning with minimal stress. Exclude pregnancy, hypothyroidism, renal failure, seizures and prolactinoma. A raised level on a prolactin-sparing drug suggests another cause [2].
- **Capacity assessment (MHCA Sec 4):** can she understand information relevant to the decision, appreciate foreseeable consequences, and communicate a decision?
- **Suicide risk:** structured assessment (C-SSRS) plus clinical formulation.
- **Bone:** DEXA if amenorrhoea lasted more than 6–12 months [2].

### Differential diagnosis

| Condition | Points for/against |
| --- | --- |
| Paranoid schizophrenia | Long duration, suspiciousness, hallucinations, withdrawal (most likely) |
| Schizoaffective disorder | Requires concurrent prominent mood episodes |
| Psychotic depression | Suicidal ideas prominent; check pervasive low mood |
| Bipolar mixed state | Agitation + suicidality; past mania? |
| Delusional disorder | Hallucinations not prominent; better functioning |
| Organic (autoimmune, thyroid, epilepsy, prolactinoma with psychosis) | Atypical features, neurological signs |
| Substance-induced | Temporal relation, toxicology |
| Schizotypal disorder | Long-term oddness without frank psychosis (not the case now) |

### Management details

| Strategy for hyperprolactinaemia | Detail |
| --- | --- |
| Switch | To aripiprazole, brexpiprazole, cariprazine, quetiapine, clozapine (prolactin-sparing) [2] |
| Adjunct (first choice) | Aripiprazole 5 mg/day (3–6 mg effective) [2] |
| Second choice | Vitamin B6 600 mg/day [2] |
| Third choice | Cabergoline, bromocriptine, amantadine (may worsen psychosis), metformin 2.5–3 g/day [2] |
| Avoid high-prolactin drugs | Under 25 years, young women, osteoporosis, hormone-dependent breast cancer [2] |

- **Oestrogen strategies (investigational/adjunctive):** transdermal oestradiol 100 µg (Kulkarni trials); raloxifene 60–120 mg/day (meta-analysis positive for PANSS total, positive and negative symptoms; Brand 2023 suggests benefit for negative symptoms only in women); bazedoxifene under trial [1].
- **Contraception:** prefer LARC (IUD, implant, depot) over daily pills when adherence is uncertain; older ethinylestradiol pills may worsen mood [1].
- **Pregnancy plans:** aripiprazole and quetiapine have reasonable safety data; avoid valproate; plan postpartum relapse prevention.

### Critical appraisal
- **Coercion vs autonomy:** her refusal has a rational basis. MHCA 2017 favours least-restrictive options; coercion should be the last resort and time-limited, with reasons recorded.
- **Sex differences:** some are robust (age of onset); others vary across cultures. The Madras cohort found no sex difference in course [4].
- **Oestrogen therapy:** promising, but risks (thrombosis, breast and endometrial effects) and heterogeneous response limit routine use. Raloxifene trials are mixed [1].
- **Prolactin:** many patients have asymptomatic hyperprolactinaemia. Treat according to symptoms and long-term risk, not the number alone [2].

### Indian context
- **MHCA 2017:**
  - Supported admission (Sec 89/90).
  - Emergency treatment (Sec 94).
  - Nominated representative (Sec 14).
  - Advance directive (Sec 5).
  - Decriminalisation of attempted suicide (Sec 115), which places a duty on government to care for people who attempt suicide.
- **National Suicide Prevention Strategy 2022;** Tele-MANAS (14416).
- Social context: stigma around marriage for women with schizophrenia; illness often concealed at marriage (Madras) [4]; caregiver burden falls on parents. Safeguarding against domestic violence and abandonment matters.
- Pesticide access is the major means of suicide in rural India; counsel the family on restricting means.
- Aripiprazole is affordable as a generic; aripiprazole LAI is costly.

### Recent advances
- Prolactin-sparing, weight-neutral options are expanding: cariprazine, brexpiprazole, lumateperone, and xanomeline–trospium (prolactin-sparing; US-approved) [2].
- **SERMs:** bazedoxifene trials in women with schizophrenia [1].
- Women's mental health services and perinatal psychiatry units are expanding in India (NIMHANS perinatal service).
