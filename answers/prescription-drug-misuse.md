## Exam answer

**Introduction.**
- **Prescription medication abuse has increased dramatically** over the last two decades [1].
- The US has **5%** of the world's population but consumes **80%** of global opioids [1].
- **Main drug classes:** **opioid analgesics**, **benzodiazepines and z-drugs**, **gabapentinoids**, and **stimulants** (for example, ADHD stimulants misused for weight loss) [1, 2].
- **Sources:** legitimate prescription, **diversion**, the illicit market and **Internet purchasing**, which is thought to be rising [2].

### Key concepts [1, 2]
- **Physical dependence is not addiction:**
  - dependence follows simple repeated exposure, and most people with withdrawal take drugs **as prescribed** ("iatrogenic dependence");
  - **addiction** involves **craving and compulsion**, and occurs in only about **2%** of benzodiazepine users [2].
- **Aberrant medication-taking behaviour vs addiction:**
  - about **50%** of chronic pain patients on opioids show aberrant behaviour, but only about **10%** of these have SUD [1];
  - **pseudoaddiction** is drug-seeking caused by **undertreated pain**, and it **abates** once pain is controlled [1].
- **Patterns of misuse:** taking more than prescribed, from fear of withdrawal, tolerance or addiction, or from greater perceived need. These concerns are themselves a risk factor [1].
- **Iatrogenic vs non-iatrogenic benzodiazepine dependence:**
  - iatrogenic: low doses for years;
  - non-iatrogenic: high doses (often over 100 mg diazepam-equivalent), illicit and intermittent [2].

### Drug classes
**1. Opioids [1]:**
- **Rates:**
  - severe SUD in **7–20%** of chronic pain patients;
  - addiction rates on opioids range from 5% to 50%;
  - synthesis: about **50%** aberrant behaviour, **20%** abuse, **10%** addiction.
- **Risk:** highest in the **first 5 years** of treatment, and with a past SUD or psychiatric comorbidity. A past SUD raises **relapse** risk when opioids are prescribed.
- **Course:** most chronic pain patients entering SUD treatment got their **first opioids from a clinician**. Many then obtain them illegally and change the **route** of use.
- **Screening tools:** **ORT, SOAPP, COMM** (self-report, with limitations).
- **Rapid-onset drugs** (oxycodone) may carry a higher abuse risk. Tramadol and tapentadol cause tolerance, dependence and abuse.

**2. Benzodiazepines and z-drugs [2]:**
- Recommended use is limited to **2–4 weeks**, yet in the UK **300,000** adults take them for more than 12 months.
- **Novel "designer" benzodiazepines:** diclazepam, **etizolam**, flualprazolam, flunitrazolam, norfludiazepam.
- **Harms:** cognitive deficits, falls, accidents, possible dementia risk, **disinhibition**, suicidality.
- **Withdrawal:** in one study 90% had withdrawal symptoms; it can be **protracted** beyond a year; seizures with abrupt cessation.
- Benzodiazepine misuse is common in **polysubstance use**, often with opioids as the primary drug.

**3. Gabapentinoids [2]:**
- They cause **addiction, physical dependence and withdrawal**.
- UK prescriptions rose **sevenfold** (2010–2020); 1.5 million people in England are prescribed them.
- Linked to **suicide, unintentional overdose, road traffic accidents and injuries**.
- Danger in combination with opioids (respiratory depression); fatalities increasing.
- **Do not stop pregabalin abruptly** (seizures).

**4. Eating disorders [2]:** patients misuse **laxatives, diuretics, ADHD stimulants, slimming pills, semaglutide and caffeine** to control weight.

### Prevention and safe prescribing [1, 2]
- **Before opioids:**
  - discuss risks and benefits;
  - a signed **opioid consent form**;
  - a **function-based plan with an exit strategy**;
  - **random urine toxicology**;
  - frequent follow-up [1].
- **Benzodiazepines:** prescribe short-term only, and avoid more than **30 mg diazepam-equivalent a day** [2].
- **Integrated or stepped care** for pain and substance use [1].
- Avoid **opioid–benzodiazepine** combinations: benzodiazepines are frequently implicated in opioid deaths [1].

### Management [1, 2]
- **Benzodiazepines:**
  - **shared decision-making** (NICE);
  - switch to **diazepam**;
  - **gradual hyperbolic taper** (proportionate reductions);
  - **CBT** alongside reduction;
  - **no pharmacological add-on**; do not substitute another dependence-forming drug, valproate or buspirone;
  - substitute prescribing does not reduce misuse [2].
- **Opioids:**
  - long-acting opioids (methadone), limited breakthrough doses, adjuvants (SNRIs, antiepileptics);
  - **scheduled toxicology with contingency plans** in addicted patients with pain [1];
  - opioid substitution or naltrexone for opioid use disorder [2].
- **Gabapentinoids:** taper on the same principles as benzodiazepines [2].
- **Pregnancy:** taper benzodiazepines over as short a time as is safe. Consider stabilising on diazepam first [2].

**Conclusion.** Prescription drug misuse is a growing, largely iatrogenic problem. Key points:
- separate **dependence, pseudoaddiction and addiction**;
- prescribe safely: short durations, consent, monitoring, exit plans;
- use **slow, supported tapering** rather than abrupt withdrawal.

## In depth

### Adverse effects of long-term benzodiazepines (Box 3.11) [2]
- **Emotional:** depression, numbness, lability, irritability.
- **Can mimic psychiatric illness:** anxiety, panic, restlessness, inter-dose withdrawal.
- **Cognitive:** memory and attention deficits, slower reaction time. Some persist after stopping.
- **Behavioural:** disinhibition, aggression, suicidality, avoidance.
- **Morbidity:** accidents, falls, delirium in the elderly; possible links to dementia, cancer and infection.
