## Exam answer

**Definition.** Two related terms:
- **Hypersomnia (hypersomnolence)** means **excessive daytime sleepiness (EDS)**: an inability to stay awake during the day, with unintended lapses into sleep. It may come with **excessive sleep quantity** (prolonged night sleep or long naps) and/or **sleep inertia** (difficulty waking, "sleep drunkenness") [1].
- **Fatigue or tiredness** without a propensity to fall asleep is different. A careful history separates the two.

**Causes, in order of frequency:**
1. **Insufficient sleep** (the commonest cause).
2. **Sleep-related breathing disorders (OSA).**
3. Medications and substances.
4. Psychiatric disorders.
5. Circadian rhythm disorders.
6. Medical and neurological conditions.
7. **Central disorders of hypersomnolence:** narcolepsy types 1 and 2, idiopathic hypersomnia, Kleine–Levin syndrome [1].

### Mechanism
Sleepiness results from:
- **↑ homeostatic pressure** (sleep deprivation, fragmented sleep from OSA or PLMD);
- **circadian misalignment**;
- **↓ wake-promoting drive** (loss of orexin neurons in narcolepsy type 1; sedating drugs blocking H1, α1, D2 and muscarinic receptors);
- **psychiatric illness** (reduced psychomotor activation, atypical depression, negative symptoms) [1].

In narcolepsy type 1, orexin loss destabilises the **sleep–wake flip-flop switch**. This causes intrusions of REM sleep into wakefulness (cataplexy, sleep paralysis, hypnagogic hallucinations) [1].

### Evaluation: stepwise (IPS CPG algorithm) [1]
1. **Confirm sleepiness, not fatigue:** **Epworth Sleepiness Scale** (0–24; >10 excessive) [1].
2. **Sleep history:**
   - 2-week **sleep diary or actigraphy**;
   - total sleep time and weekday/weekend difference (insufficient sleep);
   - shift work and sleep timing (circadian);
   - snoring, witnessed apnoeas, obesity (**STOP-BANG**);
   - restless legs or limb jerks;
   - **cataplexy** (emotion-triggered weakness), sleep paralysis, hypnagogic/hypnopompic hallucinations, disturbed nocturnal sleep;
   - unrefreshing long sleep with severe inertia (idiopathic hypersomnia);
   - recurrent episodes with hyperphagia or hypersexuality (Kleine–Levin) [1].
3. **Psychiatric evaluation:**
   - **Atypical or seasonal depression** (about 15% of depressed patients have hypersomnia) [1].
   - **Bipolar depression.**
   - Negative symptoms of schizophrenia.
   - Substance use and withdrawal (stimulant crash, cannabis).
   - Dissociative or conversion "sleep attacks".
4. **Drug review:** antipsychotics (clozapine, olanzapine, quetiapine), mirtazapine, TCAs, benzodiazepines, antiepileptics, antihistamines, opioids, lithium.
5. **Medical:**
   - hypothyroidism; post-traumatic brain injury or post-encephalitic states; Parkinson's disease; tumours of the hypothalamus;
   - renal or hepatic failure; anaemia; diabetes;
   - **Prader–Willi syndrome, myotonic dystrophy** [1].
6. **Investigations:**
   - **Nocturnal PSG**, followed next day by an **MSLT**: 5 naps at 2-hourly intervals.
     - **Mean sleep latency <8 minutes** = objective sleepiness.
     - **≥2 SOREMPs** (REM within 15 minutes of sleep onset) supports narcolepsy [1].
   - **MWT** (4 × 40-minute trials) to assess treatment response or driving fitness [1].
   - **CSF hypocretin-1 ≤110 pg/mL** (narcolepsy type 1); **HLA DQB1\*06:02**; TSH, blood tests, MRI brain if a secondary cause is suspected [1].

### Key disorders

| Disorder | Key features | Diagnosis |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Narcolepsy type 1** | EDS + **cataplexy** (brief bilateral loss of tone, consciousness preserved, emotion-triggered, reflexes lost) ± sleep paralysis, hallucinations (each about a third), fragmented night sleep | Cataplexy + MSLT (MSL <8 min + ≥2 SOREMPs, or a SOREMP on PSG) **or** CSF hypocretin ≤110 pg/mL [1] |
| **Narcolepsy type 2** | EDS for at least 3 months; no cataplexy | MSLT as above; hypocretin normal or not measured [1] |
| **Idiopathic hypersomnia** | Long, unrefreshing sleep; severe sleep inertia; long unrefreshing naps | MSL <8 min with <2 SOREMPs, or total sleep time ≥11 h over 24 h (PSG/actigraphy) |
| **Kleine–Levin syndrome** | Recurrent episodes (≥2 days, at least once every 18 months) of hypersomnia + cognitive change, derealisation, apathy ± hyperphagia, hypersexuality; normal between episodes; adolescent males | Clinical [1] |
| **OSA** | Snoring, apnoeas, unrefreshing sleep, morning headache, obesity, hypertension | PSG/HSAT: obstructive RDI ≥5 with symptoms or ≥15 [1] |
| **Hypersomnia associated with a mental disorder** | Atypical/bipolar depression, seasonal affective disorder; complaint of long sleep but **MSLT is usually normal** | Clinical; PSG and MSLT to exclude organic causes |

### Management
1. **Treat the cause:**
   - Sleep extension for insufficient sleep.
   - **CPAP** and weight loss for OSA.
   - Rationalise sedating drugs: dose timing at night, switching (e.g., aripiprazole instead of a sedating antipsychotic).
   - Treat depression, hypothyroidism.
2. **Behavioural measures:** regular sleep–wake schedule, **planned 15–20-minute naps** (narcolepsy), sleep hygiene, avoid alcohol and sedatives, **driving advice** [1].
3. **Wake-promoting drugs** [1]:
   - **Modafinil 100–400 mg** or armodafinil 150–250 mg (first-line).
   - Methylphenidate 20–40 mg (10–60 mg) or amphetamines.
   - **Pitolisant** (H3 inverse agonist, 9–36 mg).
   - **Solriamfetol** (NDRI, 75–150 mg).
   - **Sodium oxybate** 4.5–9 g/night in two doses (EDS and cataplexy).
4. **Cataplexy, sleep paralysis, hallucinations:** venlafaxine 75–375 mg, fluoxetine 10–40 mg, clomipramine/imipramine; sodium oxybate; pitolisant [1].
5. **Kleine–Levin syndrome:** **lithium** (300–900 mg) to reduce relapses; stimulants for sleepiness during episodes [1].
6. **Hypersomnia in psychiatric conditions:**
   - Treat the disorder: activating antidepressants (bupropion, SSRIs), light therapy for seasonal depression, behavioural activation, a fixed wake time.
   - Modafinil augmentation has some evidence for residual sleepiness and fatigue in depression.
   - Review sedating medication.
   - **Avoid stimulants in mania or psychosis.**

### Key evidence
- **MSLT** standardised by Carskadon and the AASM (Kushida 2005; Littner 2005) [1].
- Narcolepsy type 1 was linked to **hypocretin deficiency** (Nishino 2000; Thannickal 2000).
- **RCTs** support modafinil, sodium oxybate (Xyrem International Study Group), pitolisant (HARMONY 1) and solriamfetol (TONES 2) for EDS in narcolepsy.

**Conclusion.** Evaluating hypersomnia means separating sleepiness from fatigue, then working systematically through the common causes (insufficient sleep, OSA, drugs, psychiatric disorders) before the central hypersomnias. PSG followed by MSLT is the key objective test. In psychiatric patients, sleepiness is usually multifactorial (illness, drugs, obesity-related OSA), so **screen for OSA and rationalise medication** before attributing it to the mental disorder. Stimulant-class drugs should be reserved for confirmed central hypersomnia or carefully selected residual sleepiness.

## In depth

### Background
- **1880:** Gélineau named "narcolepsy". **1957:** Yoss and Daly described the "narcoleptic tetrad".
- **1960s–70s:** sleep-onset REM was discovered (Vogel 1960); the MSLT was developed by Carskadon and Dement (1977–86).
- **1998–2000:** orexin/hypocretin was discovered (de Lecea; Sakurai). Canine narcolepsy was linked to the *HCRTR2* mutation. Hypocretin neurons were found lost in human narcolepsy [1].
- **Classification:** ICSD-3 (2014) renamed the disorders narcolepsy types 1 and 2. DSM-5 separated narcolepsy from hypersomnolence disorder [1].

### Epidemiology
- EDS occurs in about 10–20% of adults (mostly insufficient sleep and OSA).
- **Narcolepsy** affects about 25–50 per 100,000. Onset peaks at 15–25 years. It is often diagnosed after a delay of about 10 years, and is frequently misdiagnosed as depression, epilepsy or psychosis.
- **Narcolepsy genetics:** heritability is only about 1–2%; most cases are sporadic. Associated with **HLA DQB1\*06:02** (and DRB1\*15:01 in white populations) [1]. A spike followed the 2009 H1N1 Pandemrix vaccine, which supports an autoimmune trigger.
- **Depression:** insomnia in about 70% and **hypersomnia in about 15%** of patients [1]. Hypersomnia is more common in atypical, bipolar and seasonal depression and in adolescents.
- **Kleine–Levin syndrome:** about 1–2 per million; mainly adolescent males.

### Classification and criteria
| ICSD-3-TR | DSM-5-TR | ICD-11 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Central disorders of hypersomnolence: NT1, NT2, idiopathic hypersomnia, Kleine–Levin syndrome; hypersomnia due to medical disorder / medication or substance / psychiatric disorder; insufficient sleep syndrome [1] | Hypersomnolence disorder (EDS ≥3/week for ≥3 months despite ≥7 h sleep, with recurrent lapses into sleep, long unrefreshing sleep >9 h, or sleep inertia); narcolepsy (types by hypocretin/cataplexy) [1] | Hypersomnolence disorders: narcolepsy, idiopathic hypersomnia, Kleine–Levin syndrome, hypersomnolence due to medical condition / medication or substance / mental disorder, insufficient sleep syndrome [1] |

### Mechanism in detail
- **Arousal network:** ARAS monoaminergic nuclei (LC noradrenaline, DR serotonin, TMN histamine, vPAG dopamine), cholinergic PPT/LDT and glutamatergic parabrachial nuclei. Their activity is stabilised by **orexin** neurons of the lateral hypothalamus [1].
- **Narcolepsy type 1:** autoimmune loss of about 90% of orexin neurons → unstable switching between wake, NREM and REM → EDS plus REM-intrusion phenomena. Cataplexy is REM atonia (SLD → medullary inhibitory interneurons) triggered by amygdala activity during emotion [1].
- **Idiopathic hypersomnia:** mechanism unknown. A possible GABA-A potentiating factor in CSF has been proposed (clarithromycin and flumazenil responses).
- **Psychiatric hypersomnia:** a "pseudo-hypersomnia" with long time in bed, clinophilia (staying in bed) and anergia. Objective MSL is often normal. Circadian delay and reduced activity also contribute.

### Clinical features in detail
- **EDS:** sleep attacks during monotonous situations, automatic behaviour, memory lapses, refreshing short naps (narcolepsy) vs unrefreshing naps (idiopathic hypersomnia) [1].
- **Cataplexy:** brief (under 2 minutes); triggered by laughter or joking; partial cataplexy (jaw sagging, head drop, knees buckling) is commonest. Status cataplecticus can occur on withdrawal of antidepressants. It usually appears within about 1 year of EDS onset [1].
- **Psychiatric comorbidity in narcolepsy:** depression, anxiety, social phobia, panic attacks, cognitive complaints. Vivid hypnagogic hallucinations can be **misdiagnosed as schizophrenia** [1].

### Assessment in detail
**MSLT protocol** [1]:
- **Before:** 2 weeks of adequate sleep documented by diary or actigraphy; stop REM-suppressing drugs, stimulants and hypnotics where possible (usually about 2 weeks; fluoxetine longer); urine drug screen.
- **Night before:** PSG with at least 7 h in bed and at least 6 h sleep.
- **Day:** 5 naps, the first about 2 h after waking; 20-minute nap opportunities; continue 15 minutes after sleep onset to detect SOREMPs.
- **Interpretation:** MSL <8 minutes is sleepy. ≥2 SOREMPs supports narcolepsy, but SOREMPs also occur in OSA, sleep deprivation, shift work and antidepressant withdrawal.

**MWT:** 4 trials of 40 minutes; ends after 40 minutes awake, 3 epochs of N1, or 1 epoch of any other stage. Used for safety-critical occupations [1].

**Scales:** ESS (8 items, 0–24); Stanford Sleepiness Scale (momentary, 1–7) [1].

### Differential diagnosis
| Condition | Distinguishing feature |
| --- | --- |
| Fatigue (depression, CFS, anaemia) | Tired but not falling asleep; ESS normal |
| Insufficient sleep syndrome | Short weekday sleep, catch-up at weekends; resolves with extension |
| OSA | Snoring, apnoeas, obesity; PSG |
| Delayed sleep–wake phase disorder | Morning sleepiness only; late sleep timing |
| Drug-induced | Temporal link to sedatives |
| Depression with hypersomnia | Long time in bed, anergia, normal MSL; mood symptoms |
| Epilepsy (absence, complex partial) | Automatisms, EEG |
| Conversion or dissociative "sleep" | Eyes resist opening, wake-pattern EEG |
| Cataplexy vs syncope, atonic seizure, functional collapse | Emotion trigger, preserved consciousness, brevity |
| Narcolepsy hallucinations vs psychosis | Occur at sleep–wake transitions; insight preserved |

### Management details
- **Modafinil:**
  - Start 100–200 mg in the morning; may split the dose.
  - Induces CYP3A4, so it **reduces the effectiveness of oral contraceptives**.
  - Side effects: headache, anxiety; rare SJS.
  - Abuse potential is low.
- **Stimulants:** monitor BP, appetite and psychosis; risk of misuse; caution in bipolar disorder.
- **Sodium oxybate (GHB):**
  - Taken at bedtime and again 2.5–4 h later.
  - Improves EDS, cataplexy and night sleep.
  - Respiratory depression with alcohol or sedatives; misuse potential (scheduled in many countries).
  - Sodium load; low-sodium oxybate exists.
- **Pitolisant:** no abuse potential; reduces hormonal contraceptive effectiveness.
- **Solriamfetol:** raises BP. Also licensed for residual EDS in OSA on CPAP.
- **Anticataplectic antidepressants:** abrupt withdrawal causes rebound cataplexy, so taper.
- **Kleine–Levin syndrome:** lithium may reduce episode frequency and duration; stimulants have limited effect [1].
- **In psychiatric patients:**
  1. Screen every patient on clozapine or olanzapine with weight gain for OSA (STOP-BANG).
  2. Shift sedating doses to bedtime; consider a lower-sedation alternative.
  3. For depression with hypersomnia: behavioural activation, a fixed wake time, light therapy, activating antidepressants.
  4. Modafinil/armodafinil adjuncts in bipolar depression have mixed evidence (Frye 2007 positive; armodafinil phase 3 trials mixed).
- **Driving:** advise not to drive until sleepiness is controlled; follow local regulations.
- **Children and pregnancy:** specialist input. Oxybate and stimulants in pregnancy need risk–benefit discussion.

### Critical appraisal
- **MSLT is imperfect:** test–retest reliability for NT2 and idiopathic hypersomnia is poor, and SOREMPs are non-specific. The NT2/idiopathic hypersomnia boundary is under debate.
- **Psychiatric hypersomnia:** subjective sleepiness and long sleep without objective MSL abnormality. It is uncertain whether this is a true hypersomnia or a manifestation of anergia and avoidance.
- **Drug-free testing** is often impractical in psychiatric patients; stopping antidepressants risks relapse.
- **Evidence gaps:** few trials in hypersomnia associated with mental disorders.
- **Diagnostic delay** in narcolepsy stems from under-recognition and misattribution to psychiatric illness.

### Indian context
- The **IPS Clinical Practice Guidelines for sleep disorders** (Gupta et al. 2017) give a diagnostic algorithm and drug table for hypersomnia [1].
- **Access:** polysomnography and MSLT are mostly available only in tertiary centres (NIMHANS, AIIMS, PGIMER) and private sleep labs. CSF hypocretin assays are rarely available.
- **Drug availability:** modafinil and armodafinil are widely available. Sodium oxybate, pitolisant and solriamfetol may not be marketed in India (check current status). Methylphenidate is controlled under the NDPS Act.
- **OSA burden:** OSA is common and under-diagnosed in India (prevalence estimated at about 7–13% of adults in urban studies). It is relevant in psychiatric inpatients with metabolic syndrome.

### Recent advances
- **Established:** pitolisant and solriamfetol; low-sodium oxybate (also approved for idiopathic hypersomnia) [1].
- **Emerging:** **orexin-2 receptor agonists** (e.g., oveporexton/TAK-861), which show large effects in narcolepsy type 1 in phase 2–3 trials; immunotherapy approaches for early narcolepsy (investigational).
- **Research:** ICSD-3-TR refinement of NT2 vs idiopathic hypersomnia; actigraphy-based total sleep time criteria.
