## Exam answer

**Introduction [1].**
- After detoxification, **relapse-prevention ("anticraving") medicines** are used **with psychosocial treatment**.
- Benzodiazepines have **no place** beyond acute alcohol withdrawal.
- Treatment is started by a **specialist service**. After **12 weeks**, prescribing may move to primary care.
- Many new and repurposed agents are being evaluated.

### A. Alcohol dependence
**1. Acamprosate (first line):**
- **Mechanism:** a synthetic **taurine analogue**. It is a **functional NMDA (glutamate) antagonist** and increases GABA function.
- **Efficacy:** **NNT 9–11** for maintaining abstinence.
- **Timing:** start as soon as abstinence is achieved. The BAP suggests starting **during detoxification** for a possible neuroprotective effect.
- **Dose (NICE CG115, Box 4.1):** **1998 mg/day (666 mg three times a day) if over 60 kg**; 1332 mg/day if under 60 kg.
- **Duration:** up to **6 months** with monthly supervision, or longer if beneficial (the SPC says 1 year).
- **Stop** if drinking continues **4–6 weeks** after starting.
- **Adverse effects:** diarrhoea, abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting, pruritus.
- **Contraindicated** in severe renal or hepatic impairment; avoid in pregnancy and breastfeeding. Check LFT and RFT at baseline.

**2. Naltrexone (first line):**
- **Mechanism:** a non-selective **opioid antagonist**. It prevents the alcohol-induced rise in dopamine and so **reduces reward**. It significantly reduces **relapse to heavy drinking**.
- **Dose (Box 4.2):** **50 mg/day**, after a 25 mg trial dose for 2 days. US studies used 100 mg.
- **Duration:** up to 6 months. Stop if drinking continues for 4–6 weeks or she feels unwell.
- **Can be started while still drinking** or during withdrawal.
- **Adverse effects:** nausea (early), headache, abdominal pain, reduced appetite, tiredness.
- **Monitoring:** baseline and follow-up LFTs. **Avoid in acute liver failure** (hepatotoxicity at high doses).
- **No opioid analgesics**; if opioids are needed, start them **48–72 hours after stopping naltrexone**.
- **Long-acting injectable** form improves adherence; NICE judged the evidence insufficient for routine use.

**3. Nalmefene:**
- An opioid antagonist recommended by NICE for **reducing consumption** in alcohol dependence.
- One meta-analysis found it superior to naltrexone for heavy drinking; another found limited efficacy. Its place is **not established**.

**4. Disulfiram (second line, aversive):**
- **Mechanism:** inhibits **aldehyde dehydrogenase**, so **acetaldehyde accumulates** and causes the alcohol–disulfiram reaction. Much of the effect comes from **anticipating** the reaction.
- **NICE (Box 4.3):**
  - for patients who want **abstinence** and for whom acamprosate or naltrexone is unsuitable, or who prefer disulfiram;
  - start **at least 24 hours after the last drink**;
  - **supervised by a family member**;
  - monitor every 2 weeks for 2 months, monthly for 4 months, then 6-monthly.
- **Dose:** 800 mg first dose, then **100–200 mg/day** maintenance. **Sudden death** is commoner above 1000 mg.
- **Reaction (Table 4.8):**
  - mild: flushing, sweating, nausea, hyperventilation, dyspnoea, tachycardia, hypotension;
  - severe: heart failure, MI, arrhythmia, bradycardia, respiratory depression, severe hypotension.
- **Contraindications:**
  - alcohol within 24 hours;
  - cardiac failure, CAD, hypertension, cerebrovascular disease;
  - pregnancy and breastfeeding;
  - liver disease, peripheral neuropathy;
  - **severe mental illness** (contraindicated in psychosis).
- **Adverse effects:** halitosis; rare **hepatotoxicity** (stop if jaundice appears).
- Its evidence is weaker than for acamprosate or naltrexone, but its effect size may be larger.

**5. Baclofen (off-label, second line):**
- A **GABA-B agonist**.
- Cochrane (2023): it may help maintain abstinence in detoxified patients.
- More adverse effects: depression, vertigo, somnolence, numbness, rigidity.

**6. Antiseizure drugs:**
- **Topiramate** (GABA/glutamate modulator) may be as effective as naltrexone and reduces heavy drinking.
- Evidence for gabapentin, valproate and levetiracetam is insufficient, so they are not routine in the UK.

**Special situations [1]:**
- **Pregnancy:** no relapse-prevention drug has been evaluated.
- **16–19-year-olds:** naltrexone is best supported. These drugs are **not licensed under 18**.
- **Depression with AUD:** **sertraline 200 mg plus naltrexone 100 mg** beat either drug alone (Pettinati). Citalopram added nothing. Acamprosate helps mood indirectly through abstinence.
- **Bipolar disorder with AUD:**
  - **naltrexone early**, then acamprosate, then disulfiram;
  - adding valproate to lithium improves drinking outcomes;
  - avoid lithium in binge drinkers.
- **PTSD with AUD:** naltrexone or disulfiram, alone or combined.
- **Schizophrenia with AUD:** naltrexone or acamprosate. **Disulfiram is contraindicated.**
- **Cocaine plus alcohol:** naltrexone 150 mg helped men only; topiramate was ineffective.

### B. Opioid dependence: naltrexone [1]
- Trial evidence for relapse prevention (oral or sustained-release) is **inconclusive**.
- **NICE:** cost-effective for those who choose an abstinence programme, are well informed and highly motivated, and have a **supportive partner**.
- **Risk:** mortality is **threefold** relative to methadone **after naltrexone is stopped**, from overdose with lost tolerance. Supervise it, and select patients carefully.
- **Washout before starting:** up to **10 days after methadone**; up to 7 days after heroin, morphine, fentanyl or buprenorphine (over 2 mg). A **naloxone challenge** (0.2–0.8 mg IM) can be given first.

### C. Nicotine [1]
- **Varenicline:** a nicotinic acetylcholine receptor **partial agonist**. It causes sustained mesolimbic dopamine release and **blocks the dopamine response** to further nicotine.
- **Network meta-analysis odds ratios:** vaping 2.37, **varenicline 2.33**, cytisine 2.21, combination NRT 1.93.
- Behavioural support plus varenicline, combination NRT or vaping is more effective than bupropion or single NRT.

**Conclusion.** For alcohol:
- **acamprosate and naltrexone are first line**, with psychosocial treatment, for about 6 months;
- **disulfiram is second line**, supervised and aversive;
- **nalmefene** is for reducing drinking;
- **baclofen and topiramate** are off-label alternatives.

Choose by goal (abstinence or reduction), liver and kidney function, comorbidity and pregnancy.

## In depth

### Choosing an alcohol anticraving drug [1]
| Situation | Prefer | Avoid |
|---|---|---|
| Liver disease, acute liver failure | Acamprosate (unless severe hepatic impairment) | Naltrexone at high dose; disulfiram |
| Severe renal impairment | Naltrexone (check function) | Acamprosate |
| Needs opioid analgesia | Acamprosate | Naltrexone |
| Psychosis | Naltrexone or acamprosate | Disulfiram |
| Goal is reduction, not abstinence | Nalmefene (or naltrexone) | Disulfiram |
| Pregnancy | No drug evaluated; psychosocial care | Acamprosate, disulfiram |
