## Exam answer

**Introduction.** Dopamine agonists act directly on dopamine receptors and bypass the degenerating nigrostriatal neurones [1]:
- **ergot-derived:** bromocriptine, pergolide, cabergoline, lisuride;
- **non-ergot:** pramipexole, ropinirole;
- **apomorphine:** subcutaneous; D1 and D2 agonist.

**Uses:**
- early monotherapy to **delay levodopa dyskinesias**;
- adjuncts for "off" time.

**Behavioural cost:** they carry **more psychiatric adverse effects than levodopa** [1, 2].

### 1. Psychosis [1, 2]
- **Two to three times more likely than with levodopa** in trials comparing early agonist vs levodopa therapy [1].
- Reported with bromocriptine, lisuride, pergolide and apomorphine.
- Psychiatric adverse effects are **the commonest reason for stopping agonists** [1].
- Agonist psychoses are said to be **more florid and more persistent** than levodopa psychoses [1].
- The relationship is **not simply pharmacological**: few patients given agonists for other indications become psychotic. Host factors matter, especially age, illness duration and cognitive impairment [1].
- **Features:** typically visual hallucinations in clear consciousness, then persecutory delusions.
- Anticholinergics and agonists are more psychotogenic than levodopa or COMT inhibitors [2].

### 2. Impulse-control disorders and related behaviours [1, 2]
- **ICDs:** dopamine agonists **"increase the risk of impulse control disorders"**. This is the main caution when using pramipexole to augment antidepressants in PD [2].
- **Hypersexuality and paraphilias:** increased libido, sometimes paraphilia, noted after introduction of dopaminergic treatment [1]. Reducing agonists and high-dose levodopa is a first step for sexual disinhibition [2].
- **Dopamine dysregulation syndrome** [1]:
  - compulsive overuse of dopaminergic drugs, mostly short-acting levodopa;
  - **agonist misuse occurs but rarely in isolation from levodopa**.
  - Criteria (Giovannoni):
    - escalating doses;
    - drug-seeking and demanding more despite dyskinesias;
    - unwillingness to reduce;
    - social or occupational impairment;
    - mania-like states;
    - withdrawal syndrome;
    - lasting ≥6 months.

### 3. Mood effects [1, 2]
- **Hypomania or euphoria** at dose peaks; levodopa or dopaminergics may boost energy [1].
- **Withdrawal:** dysphoria, anhedonia, anxiety and irritability on reduction [1]. **Depression after agonist withdrawal** [2].
- **Paradox:** agonists are **effective antidepressants** in PD (pramipexole), alone or as augmentation [2].

### 4. Other neuropsychiatric effects [2]
- **Agonists:** sedation, psychomotor agitation, anxiety, akathisia, sleep disturbance, psychosis, cognitive impairment, delirium, visual hallucinations.
- **Apomorphine:** drowsiness [1].

### 5. Management [1, 2]
1. **Recognise:** ask specifically, including the carer, about hallucinations, sexual behaviour and medication-seeking.
2. **Exclude delirium** and other organic causes.
3. **Rationalise drugs** together with the neurologist. Withdraw in order [1]:
   - recently added drugs;
   - anticholinergics;
   - **dopamine agonists**;
   - COMT inhibitors.
   Balance against mobility [2].
4. **Psychosis persisting:** quetiapine trial, then **clozapine** (6.25 mg start; 25–35 mg/day) or pimavanserin. Avoid risperidone and typicals [2].
5. **Dementia:** cholinesterase inhibitor [2].
6. **On reduction:** watch for withdrawal dysphoria and depression [1, 2].

**Conclusion.** Dopamine agonists:
- spare levodopa and delay dyskinesias;
- carry greater risk than levodopa of psychosis (two- to threefold), ICDs, hypersexuality and withdrawal mood disturbance;
- are the commonest psychiatric reason for stopping antiparkinsonian drugs.

Management is careful dose reduction, then clozapine or pimavanserin for persistent psychosis.

## In depth

### Ergot-specific non-behavioural risks [1]
- Pergolide and cabergoline cause **serosal fibrosis** (e.g., retroperitoneal) and **valvular heart disease** (one-third on pergolide in one study).
- Bromocriptine: caution in peripheral vascular disease; contraindicated in ischaemic heart disease; postural hypotension and nausea (domperidone helps).

### Not covered by the sources (deliberately left blank)
- **ICDs:** prevalence (e.g., figures from DOMINION), specific types (pathological gambling, compulsive buying, binge eating, punding, hobbyism), risk factors (young age, male sex, novelty seeking, prior addiction), screening (QUIP), and management (agonist taper, amantadine, CBT).
- **Dopamine agonist withdrawal syndrome (DAWS)** as a defined syndrome. Maudsley cites Rabinak 2010 but gives no details.
- Agonist-related **sudden sleep attacks**.
