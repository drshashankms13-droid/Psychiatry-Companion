## Exam answer

**Introduction.**
- **What it is:** vascular dementia (VaD), or **vascular cognitive impairment (VCI)**, is cognitive decline caused by cerebrovascular disease [1].
- **Frequency:**
  - **second commonest** dementia: 15–20% clinically (up to 40–50% in some series) [1]; 10–50% [3];
  - **very frequently mixed with AD**.
- **A "concept in transition":** a separate entity with distinct profile, risk factors and pathology is "no longer sustainable" [1].

### Pathophysiology [1, 2]
- **Mechanisms** [2]:
  - hypoperfusion and hypoxia;
  - oxidative stress and inflammation causing **endothelial dysfunction**;
  - impaired **autoregulation** and **neurovascular coupling**.
- **Subtypes (Lishman; Kalaria Box 9.3)** [1]:

| Subtype | Features |
| --- | --- |
| **Multi-infarct dementia** (large or several infarcts; >50 mL tissue loss) | Cortical/subcortical infarcts from large-vessel or embolic disease |
| **Small-vessel disease** (commonest cause of VCI) | **White matter lesions** (periventricular "leucoaraiosis", deep white-matter hyperintensities) and **lacunes** in the central grey; hyalinisation, arteriolosclerosis. Includes **Binswanger's disease**: diffuse white-matter demyelination with lacunes, arcuate fibres and cortex spared; hypertensive, 50s–60s; subacute progression of focal deficits with plateaus |
| **Strategic infarcts** | Thalamus, hippocampus |
| **Hypoperfusion** | Hippocampal sclerosis, ischaemic–anoxic damage |
| **Haemorrhages** | Including cerebral amyloid angiopathy |
| **Cerebrovascular disease with AD pathology** | Mixed dementia |
| **Post-stroke dementia** | Up to one-third of over-55s within 5 years of stroke (incidence about 9× expected). Strategic, multiple or small-vessel lesions, or unmasked AD |
| **Specific genetic syndromes** | **CADASIL** (*NOTCH3*; autosomal dominant; migraine with aura, recurrent subcortical infarcts, TIAs, mood disturbance; onset in the 40s; no vascular risk factors). **Hereditary cerebral haemorrhage with amyloidosis** (Dutch type; APP); Icelandic, British and Danish types |

- **Neuropathology:**
  - complete infarcts 75%; lacunar and small infarcts 50%; cystic 25%;
  - **amyloid angiopathy** 10%; haemorrhages 2%.
- **Risk factors:**
  - **stroke**, white-matter disease;
  - **hypertension** (mid-life), **diabetes**;
  - hypercholesterolaemia and adverse lipids;
  - smoking, some genetic variants.
  - Late-life hypertension and cholesterol may paradoxically be "protective" in some studies.
- **Relationship with AD:**
  - shared risk factors;
  - amyloid angiopathy may promote ischaemia;
  - ischaemia may promote AD pathology;
  - the most parsimonious view is **independent but synergistic pathologies**: more impairment for a given AD load when vascular disease is present.

### Clinical features (classical multi-infarct picture) [1]
- **Onset:** late 60s–70s, M ≥ F; often **abrupt**, or after a clinical stroke.
- **Early non-cognitive features:**
  - personality or emotional change;
  - somatic complaints (headache, dizziness, tinnitus, syncope).
- **Course:**
  - **stepwise deterioration** with **fluctuation**, **apoplectiform episodes** (hemiparesis, dysphasia, visual disturbance), partial recovery and remissions;
  - **but** many have gradual progression, and progression may be indistinguishable from AD.
- **Patchy deficits:**
  - **personality and judgement preserved**;
  - **insight retained** (hence **depression and anxiety**);
  - **emotional lability / emotional incontinence** (pathological laughing and crying);
  - explosive outbursts;
  - nocturnal confusion.
- **Neurological signs:**
  - focal signs (asymmetric reflexes, extensor plantars);
  - **pseudobulbar palsy** (dysarthria, dysphagia, emotionalism);
  - parkinsonism; **marche à petit pas**;
  - **seizures in about 20%**; syncope.
- **Small-vessel/subcortical type** [1, 2]:
  - **slowed processing speed**, **executive dysfunction**, apathy;
  - gait disturbance, **early urinary frequency or incontinence**, falls;
  - depression and motor deficits with deep white-matter lesions.

### Diagnosis [1, 2]
- **Hachinski ischaemic index:**
  - abrupt onset (2), stepwise deterioration (1), fluctuating course (2), nocturnal confusion (1), preserved personality (1), depression (1), somatic complaints (1), emotional incontinence (1), hypertension (1), history of strokes (2), atherosclerosis (1), focal symptoms (2), focal signs (2);
  - **≥7 suggests multi-infarct dementia; ≤4 suggests non-vascular (AD)**;
  - overlap is common in the elderly.
- **NINDS-AIREN (probable VaD):**
  1. dementia (memory plus two or more domains, with ADL impairment);
  2. cerebrovascular disease (**focal signs** plus **imaging**: multiple large infarcts, a strategic infarct, multiple lacunes, or extensive white-matter lesions);
  3. **a relationship**: onset **within 3 months of stroke**, or abrupt/stepwise deterioration.
  - **Supportive:** early gait disturbance, falls, urinary symptoms, pseudobulbar palsy, mood change, psychomotor slowing.
  - **High specificity, low sensitivity.**
- **DSM-5-TR vascular NCD:**
  - NCD **plus** a vascular aetiology: onset temporally related to cerebrovascular events, **or** prominent decline in **complex attention/processing speed and frontal-executive function**;
  - evidence of cerebrovascular disease sufficient to explain deficits;
  - **probable** if supported by imaging, temporal link, or genetic evidence (CADASIL).
- **Investigations:**
  - **MRI preferred** (CT misses lacunes);
  - EEG: focal or asymmetric slowing; sometimes normal;
  - vascular work-up: BP, glucose, lipids, ECG/rhythm, carotids (cardiovascular appraisal) [1].

### Management [1, 2, 3]
1. **Treat underlying cerebrovascular risk** (the cornerstone) [3]:
   - **strict BP control**;
   - antiplatelets or anticoagulants as cardiac and stroke indications dictate;
   - diabetes and cholesterol control;
   - corticosteroids for vasculitis [1].
   - **But:** no conclusive evidence that statins or aspirin alter VaD incidence or progression, and little evidence that lowering BP benefits the dementia itself [1, 3].
2. **Cognitive enhancers:**
   - **none is licensed for VaD** [3];
   - **donepezil 5–10 mg and galantamine 16–24 mg** give a **slight** cognitive benefit of doubtful clinical importance, with more adverse effects (Cochrane) [3];
   - memantine: small benefit in global status;
   - **NICE:** consider AChE-Is or memantine **only if comorbid AD, PDD or DLB is suspected**;
   - **BAP:** no first choice (some benefit with donepezil 10 mg) [3];
   - mixed dementia: AChE-Is first choice [3].
3. **Depression and emotionalism:** SSRIs (as for dementia generally); attention to insight-related distress [1].
4. **BPSD:** non-pharmacological first; cautious drugs. Antipsychotics carry stroke and mortality risk.
5. **Physical activity and rehabilitation:** exercise and physiotherapy; OT assessment [1].
6. **Psychosocial and care:** as for dementia generally (information, carers, legal planning, residential care).
7. **CADASIL:** family identification and **presymptomatic counselling and testing** [1].

**Conclusion.** Vascular dementia (VCI) is the second commonest dementia and is usually mixed with AD. It arises from:
- multi-infarct disease;
- small-vessel (subcortical) disease;
- strategic infarcts;
- post-stroke dementia;
- hypoperfusion;
- haemorrhage or genetic arteriopathies (CADASIL).

The classical picture is abrupt, stepwise and fluctuating, with focal signs, preserved personality and insight, emotionalism, pseudobulbar palsy and executive slowing. Diagnosis uses Hachinski, NINDS-AIREN or DSM-5-TR criteria with MRI. Management centres on vascular risk control and rehabilitation. Cognitive enhancers offer at most slight benefit (mainly in mixed disease).

## In depth

### White matter lesions [1]
- Present in over 90% of the elderly (only 8% are free of them); predicted by mid-life hypertension.
- **Periventricular** lesions are more linked to dementia and atrophy.
- **Deep** white-matter lesions are more linked to diabetes, depression and motor deficits.
