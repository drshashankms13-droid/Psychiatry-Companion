## Exam answer

**Definition.** Bipolar depression is a major depressive episode in a person with bipolar I or II disorder. It meets the same criteria as unipolar depression but differs in course and drug response. Compared with unipolar depression, episodes are of more rapid onset, more frequent, more severe, shorter, more often psychotic, and more often have **reversed vegetative symptoms** (hypersomnia, hyperphagia). Depression accounts for most of the time spent ill in bipolar disorder, and about 15% of patients die by suicide [1].

### Mechanism (drug targets)
- **Quetiapine:** norquetiapine noradrenaline reuptake inhibition + 5-HT2A/H1 → antidepressant.
- **Lurasidone:** 5-HT7 antagonist, 5-HT1A partial agonist.
- **Lumateperone:** 5-HT2A, D1/glutamate, SERT.
- **Cariprazine:** D3-preferring partial agonist.
- **Olanzapine–fluoxetine:** synergistic noradrenaline/dopamine release in prefrontal cortex.
- **Lamotrigine:** sodium-channel blockade → reduced glutamate.
- **Lithium:** GSK-3 inhibition, neuroprotection, anti-suicidal effect.

Antidepressant efficacy in bipolar depression **does not track D2 blockade**: aripiprazole and most D2 blockers are ineffective [1].

### Evidence-based options

| Agent | Dose | Evidence and notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Quetiapine** | 300–600 mg at night | Five large RCTs (BOLDER I/II, EMBOLDEN I/II); bipolar I and II; prevents both poles; sedation and weight gain [1] |
| **Lurasidone** | 20–120 mg with food | Monotherapy and adjunct to lithium/valproate; weight-neutral; nausea, akathisia [1] |
| **Olanzapine + fluoxetine** | 6/25–12/50 mg | Remission 49% vs 25% placebo; most effective in the 2023 network meta-analysis; metabolic burden [1,2] |
| **Cariprazine** | 1.5–3 mg | Effective (1.5 mg most consistent); smallest effect size among effective drugs [1] |
| **Lumateperone** | 42 mg | Effective in bipolar I and II; little weight gain [1] |
| **Lamotrigine** | Titrate to 200 mg (25 mg ×2 weeks → 50 mg ×2 weeks → 100 → 200) | Better for prevention of depression; slow titration; risk of Stevens–Johnson rash [1] |
| **Lithium** | Level 0.6–1.0 mmol/L | Acute efficacy modest; strong anti-suicidal effect and prevention of relapse [1] |
| **Antidepressant + antimanic** | SSRI or bupropion | Limited, conditional role; avoid monotherapy, TCAs and venlafaxine [2] |
| **Valproate** | — | Limited evidence; avoid in women of childbearing potential [1] |

**Ineffective or doubtful:** aripiprazole, ziprasidone, risperidone, carbamazepine (weak data) [1].

### Assessment
- Confirm bipolar diagnosis (past hypomania/mania; MDQ, collateral history).
- Severity (MADRS/HAM-D); suicide risk; psychotic and mixed features; rapid cycling.
- Substance use; physical health (thyroid, metabolic, renal, pregnancy).
- Current medication, levels and adherence; previous responses.

### Management algorithm
1. **Optimise existing treatment:** check lithium level and adherence; stop antidepressant monotherapy.
2. **First-line monotherapy** (CANMAT/ISBD 2018; BAP 2016): quetiapine, lurasidone, lithium or lamotrigine. NICE 2014/2023: olanzapine–fluoxetine or quetiapine; lamotrigine second [1].
3. **Partial response:** combine (lithium/valproate + lurasidone; lithium + lamotrigine; quetiapine + lamotrigine). Add an SSRI or bupropion to an antimanic drug in selected cases [1,2].
4. **Treatment-resistant:** ECT (rapid, high response, safe in pregnancy); IV ketamine (0.5 mg/kg; now standard for refractory bipolar depression in Maudsley); pramipexole adjunct; T3; tranylcypromine; clozapine [1].
5. **Psychosocial:** psychoeducation, family-focused therapy, IPSRT, CBT.
6. **Continuation and maintenance:** continue the effective agent (quetiapine, lamotrigine, lithium); taper adjunctive antidepressant after 2–4 months [2].

### Key evidence
- **BOLDER I/II (Calabrese 2005; Thase 2006):** quetiapine 300 and 600 mg superior to placebo in bipolar I and II.
- **PREVAIL-1/2 (Loebel 2014):** lurasidone monotherapy and adjunct effective.
- **Tohen 2003:** olanzapine–fluoxetine superior to olanzapine and placebo [2].
- **STEP-BD (Sachs 2007):** adjunctive antidepressants no better than placebo [2].
- **Network meta-analysis (2023, 101 RCTs):** olanzapine–fluoxetine > quetiapine > olanzapine > lurasidone > lumateperone > cariprazine > lamotrigine [1].

**Conclusion.** Bipolar depression should be treated with agents proven in bipolar depression (quetiapine, lurasidone, olanzapine–fluoxetine, cariprazine, lumateperone, lamotrigine, lithium), not by default with antidepressants. Choose by efficacy, tolerability (especially metabolic), pregnancy potential and maintenance needs, with lithium kept in mind for suicide prevention. ECT and ketamine are effective options for resistant cases.

## In depth

### Background
Bipolar depression was long treated like unipolar depression with TCAs. Concerns about switch and rapid cycling (Wehr & Goodwin 1987) and evidence that antidepressants added little (STEP-BD 2007) changed practice. Quetiapine (2006) and olanzapine–fluoxetine (2003) were the first FDA-approved treatments for bipolar depression, followed by lurasidone (2013), cariprazine (2019) and lumateperone (2021).

### Epidemiology
- In long-term follow-up, patients spent about 32% of weeks depressed vs about 9% hypomanic or manic in bipolar I, and about 50% vs 1% in bipolar II (Judd 2002, 2003) [3].
- About 15% die by suicide [1]; most suicides occur during depressive or mixed phases.
- Bipolar depression carries greater socioeconomic burden than mania or unipolar depression [1].
- Misdiagnosis as unipolar depression is common (mean diagnostic delay 6–10 years) [3,4].

### Classification and criteria
- **DSM-5-TR:** major depressive episode in bipolar I (with or without a manic history) or bipolar II (with hypomania). Specifiers: with mixed features, anxious distress, rapid cycling, psychotic features, seasonal pattern, peripartum onset.
- **ICD-11 6A60/6A61:** bipolar type I or II disorder, current episode depressive (mild, moderate, severe, with or without psychotic symptoms).
- **Treatment resistance in bipolar depression:** failure of two adequate trials of approved agents (CANMAT/ISBD definition).

### Mechanism in detail
- Possible mechanisms: mitochondrial dysfunction, circadian dysregulation, inflammation, and reduced neuroplasticity (BDNF).
- **Lithium:** GSK-3β inhibition, inositol depletion, increased BDNF and Bcl-2, increased hippocampal neurogenesis, possible neuroprotection [1].
- **Lamotrigine:** works more for prevention than acute treatment; the slow titration limits short trials [1].
- **Why D2 blockade is not antidepressant:** antidepressant effect correlates with 5-HT2A/5-HT7, NET and other actions [1].

### Clinical features in detail

| Feature | Bipolar depression | Unipolar depression |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Onset | Earlier (<25), abrupt | Later, gradual |
| Episodes | More, shorter | Fewer, longer |
| Symptoms | Hypersomnia, hyperphagia, leaden paralysis, retardation, psychotic features | Insomnia, anorexia, anxiety |
| Mixed features | Common | Less common |
| Postpartum onset | More common | Less |
| Family history | Bipolar disorder | Depression |
| Antidepressant response | Poor, wear-off, switch | Better |

Sources: [1,3,4].

### Assessment in detail
- **Scales:** MADRS, HAM-D, QIDS; YMRS for mixed symptoms; MDQ/HCL-32 for past hypomania [2,4].
- **Mixed features:** look for racing thoughts, irritability, agitation, decreased need for sleep alongside depression (DSM-5 "with mixed features" needs 3 or more hypomanic symptoms).
- **Investigations:** TSH (hypothyroidism common in lithium-treated and rapid-cycling patients); lithium level; renal function; metabolic profile; pregnancy test.

### Differential diagnosis

| Condition | Clue |
| --- | --- |
| Unipolar depression | No past (hypo)mania (screen carefully) |
| Mixed episode | Concurrent manic symptoms → avoid antidepressants |
| Borderline personality disorder | Reactive, short mood shifts; chronic emptiness |
| Substance-induced | Alcohol, stimulant crash |
| Hypothyroidism | Lithium-treated; TSH |
| Dysthymia/cyclothymia | Chronic subthreshold |
| Schizoaffective disorder | Psychosis outside mood episodes |

### Management details
- **Pregnancy:** quetiapine, lurasidone and lamotrigine are preferred. Lithium can be used with care (Ebstein anomaly risk is small; monitor levels; adjust at delivery). **Avoid valproate.** ECT is safe.
- **Elderly:** lower doses; lithium levels 0.4–0.6 mmol/L; lamotrigine well tolerated.
- **Adolescents:** lurasidone (FDA-approved 10–17 years), olanzapine–fluoxetine (10–17 years).
- **Rapid cycling:** avoid antidepressants; lamotrigine, quetiapine, lithium + valproate; check thyroid.
- **With anxiety:** quetiapine; avoid benzodiazepine dependence.
- **Lamotrigine interactions:** valproate doubles lamotrigine levels (halve dose); carbamazepine halves them; oral contraceptives lower them.
- **Ketamine:** 0.5 mg/kg IV over 40 minutes; dissociation is common but brief; switch risk is remote [1].
- **ECT:** effective in bipolar depression with response rates similar to or higher than in unipolar depression; consider early if psychotic, suicidal or catatonic.

### Critical appraisal
- Most effective drugs are antipsychotics with metabolic costs (quetiapine, olanzapine). Lurasidone, cariprazine and lumateperone are better tolerated but less available in India or more costly.
- Placebo response is high in bipolar depression trials; effect sizes are moderate (SMD ≈0.3–0.5).
- Lithium's acute antidepressant evidence is weak, but its anti-suicidal and prophylactic value justify its use [1].
- Guidelines differ (NICE vs BAP vs CANMAT vs RANZCP), reflecting uncertainty and different weighting of network meta-analyses [1].
- There are few long-term head-to-head trials.

### Indian context
- IPS CPG for bipolar disorder (2017) is broadly similar to CANMAT: quetiapine, lurasidone, lamotrigine and lithium as first line; avoid antidepressant monotherapy.
- Quetiapine, olanzapine, fluoxetine, lamotrigine and lithium are inexpensive generics; lurasidone is moderately priced and widely available; cariprazine is available; lumateperone is not.
- ECT is widely accessible in Indian teaching hospitals; ketamine is used in some centres.
- Lithium monitoring is challenging in rural areas; build in laboratory access through DMHP and NCD clinics.

### Recent advances
- **Established:** lumateperone and cariprazine for bipolar depression [1].
- **Emerging:** ketamine/esketamine (low certainty) [1]; pramipexole [1]; adjunctive light therapy (midday bright light); anti-inflammatory adjuncts (N-acetylcysteine, minocycline: mixed results).
- **Investigational:** psilocybin (excluded from most trials because of switch risk); TMS for bipolar depression (moderate evidence, HF left DLPFC per the IPS CPG) [5].
