> **Source note:** the user's sources cover the general suicide framework (theories, risk factors, scales), Indian policy (NSPS 2022) and late-life depression treatment (Maudsley 15e). Specific late-life suicide epidemiology and trial data are **general knowledge**, marked as such. Verify them before relying on this answer.

## Exam answer

**Introduction.** Older adults (usually 60 or 65 years and above) have the **highest suicide rates of any age group** in most countries. Older men are the highest-risk group of all [general knowledge].

Maudsley notes that "suicide is relatively more common in older people" and that "mortality is reduced by effective treatment of depression" [1]. The seminar lists the elderly among the **three high-risk age groups** (with adolescents and young adults). It includes "Age: elderly" in **SAD PERSONS** [2].

### Distinctive features of late-life suicide [general knowledge]
1. **High lethality:**
   - the attempt-to-suicide ratio is about **4:1** in the elderly, compared with about **100–200:1** in adolescents;
   - methods are more lethal and planned (hanging, pesticides, jumping, firearms where available);
   - there are fewer "cries for help".
2. **Less communication** of intent; ideation is often passive ("tired of life").
3. **Frequent contact with primary care before death:**
   - about **45–75% see a GP in the preceding month**, often for physical complaints;
   - **fewer than 20% are in contact with mental health services**.
4. **Depression is the main psychiatric correlate** but is **under-recognised** because of masked, somatic or "pseudodementia" presentations and ageist attribution ("it's normal to be sad when old").
5. Physical frailty means a **non-fatal attempt is more likely to become fatal**.
6. "**Silent suicides**" (indirect self-destructive behaviour): refusing food or medicines, self-neglect. These are often not counted.

### Epidemiology
- **Global:** WHO 2014 estimated about **800,000 suicides a year** (1.4% of all deaths) [2]. Rates are highest above 70 years in almost all regions [general knowledge].
- **India:**
  - the bulk of suicides are among **18–45-year-olds (65%)** [3];
  - but the **rate** among older people is substantial and probably under-reported. Elderly deaths are often recorded as "illness" or accidents [general knowledge].
  - NSPS figures: **illness accounts for about 18%** of suicides and **family problems for about 34%** [3]. Both are especially relevant in late life.

### Risk factors
| Domain | Risk factors |
| --- | --- |
| **Psychiatric** | **Depression** (present in about 60–90% of late-life suicides on psychological autopsy) [general knowledge]; previous attempt; **alcohol use disorder**; anxiety; psychotic depression; **early dementia or MCI** (especially soon after diagnosis); personality traits (anankastic, rigid, low openness) |
| **Physical** | **Chronic illness, pain, disability**, cancer, COPD, stroke, visual loss, epilepsy; **sleep disturbance**; recent hospital discharge [general knowledge]; "Sickness" in SAD PERSONS [2] |
| **Social** | **Bereavement (especially widowerhood)**; living alone; **social isolation** and loneliness; **"No spouse"** [2]; elder abuse; financial strain and dependence; loss of role after retirement; migration of children ("empty nest" in India); institutionalisation |
| **Demographic** | **Male sex**; very old age; unmarried |
| **Access to means** | Stockpiled medicines (TCAs, benzodiazepines, opioids), pesticides, firearms |
| **Psychological** | Hopelessness; **perceived burdensomeness**; loss of autonomy; feeling a burden to family |

**Protective factors:**
- religiosity and spirituality;
- family connectedness and the joint family (weakening in India);
- a sense of purpose;
- treatment of depression;
- access to care [general knowledge; 2].

### Theories applied to late life [2]
- **Interpersonal theory (Joiner):**
  - **perceived burdensomeness** (dependency, illness costs) and **thwarted belongingness** (widowhood, isolation);
  - **acquired capability** may increase through chronic pain and illness.
- **Shneidman's psychache:** loss of autonomy, love and meaning.
- **IMV model (O'Connor):** defeat and entrapment from chronic illness. Access to means turns intent into action.
- **Durkheim:**
  - **egoistic** suicide from social isolation;
  - **anomic** suicide from sudden loss of status or role, such as retirement or widowhood.
- **Erikson:** "**integrity vs despair**".

### Assessment [1, 2]
1. **Ask directly** about ideation in every depressed older person and in those with new disability or bereavement. Asking does not "plant" the idea [2].
   - Start: "Have you ever felt that life was not worth living?" Then: "Did you ever wish you could go to sleep and not wake up?" [2]
2. **Psychiatric evaluation:** depression (GDS, PHQ-9), psychosis, alcohol (AUDIT), **cognition (MMSE/HMSE/MoCA)**, anxiety.
3. **Physical:** pain, disability, sensory loss, medicines (deliriogenic or depressogenic drugs), terminal illness.
4. **Social:** living situation, recent losses, elder abuse, finances, family attitudes.
5. **Means:** stockpiled tablets, pesticides at home.
6. **Scales:** **C-SSRS**, **Beck SIS** after an attempt, **BHS**, **Geriatric Suicide Ideation Scale**, SAD PERSONS.
   - Note: "standardised risk scales are not predictive of risk, with low positive predictive values" [2].
   - Clinical inquiry is the most important instrument [2].

### Management
**Immediate:**
- Medical stabilisation after an attempt; a serious attempt in an older person is presumed high-intent.
- **Decide the setting:** admission if there is high intent, psychotic depression, poor support or living alone. The decision also depends on family support and outpatient access [2].
- **MHCA 2017:**
  - **s115:** attempted suicide is presumed to be "severe stress", with a State duty of care;
  - **s94:** emergency treatment for 72 hours where needed;
  - **s89/s90:** supported admission if capacity is impaired [general knowledge; see `decriminalisation-of-suicide`].
- **Means restriction:** a family member keeps medicines; supply limited quantities; secure pesticides.

**Treat depression vigorously [1]:**
- Response rates in older adults are about **51%**, similar to younger adults [1].
- **Start with an SSRI (sertraline is commonly first line).** Then switch to another SSRI or **mirtazapine**. Then venlafaxine or lofepramine [1].
- **Augmenting with aripiprazole** or bupropion beat switching in treatment-resistant geriatric depression (**OPTIMUM** trial) [1].
- Monitor **hyponatraemia, falls, GI bleeding** (SSRIs) [1].
- **Avoid TCAs** (cardiotoxic, lethal in overdose) [1].
- **Continue treatment:** over 60% relapsed within 2 years when antidepressants were withdrawn after 2 years of treatment [1].
- **ECT:** the most effective treatment for severe, psychotic or suicidal depression in the elderly. It reduces short-term suicidal ideation [2; general knowledge].
- **Lithium** has anti-suicidal effects in mood disorders (use cautiously because of renal function and interactions) [2].
- **Ketamine/esketamine** are rapidly effective in people over 65 without worsening cognition (emerging evidence) [1].

**Psychological and social:**
- problem-solving therapy;
- CBT; IPT (grief, role transitions);
- **reminiscence and life-review therapy**;
- **collaborative care in primary care**: PROSPECT and IMPACT trials reduced suicidal ideation [general knowledge];
- telephone outreach (the Italian **"TeleHelp/TeleCheck"** programme reduced suicides [general knowledge]);
- day care, senior clubs;
- elder-abuse protection;
- **Maintenance and Welfare of Parents and Senior Citizens Act 2007** [general knowledge];
- pension and benefits.

**Palliative and medical:** pain control; palliative care; treat sensory loss.

**Prevention (population level):**
- **GP training** in recognising late-life depression;
- **community gatekeepers**: ASHAs, pension workers, faith leaders [3];
- means restriction;
- **reducing loneliness** (befriending);
- NSPS district mental-health services [3].

**Conclusion.** Late-life suicide is **high-lethality, low-communication and closely tied to depression, physical illness, bereavement and isolation**. Most victims see a primary-care doctor shortly before death. Prevention therefore depends on **asking every depressed or newly disabled older person about suicide**, treating depression to remission (SSRI, augmentation, ECT, continuation) [1], restricting means and rebuilding social connectedness [2, 3].

## In depth

### Background
- Durkheim (1897) already described rising suicide with age and in widowed men.
- Classic **psychological autopsy studies** (Conwell, Waern, Beautrais) established depression and physical illness as dominant correlates [general knowledge].

### Epidemiology in detail [general knowledge unless cited]
- **Global:** rates in men aged 75+ are often more than 3 times those in young adults.
- **Gender:** the male:female ratio is wider in late life (about 3–4:1).
- **India:**
  - NCRB data under-represent the elderly because of misclassification and stigma;
  - Kerala and Tamil Nadu report high elderly suicide rates;
  - farm distress affects older farmers;
  - "**granny dumping**" and abandonment are emerging concerns.
- **NMHS 2015–16** (quoted in the seminar): suicidality 5,106.7, attempts 349.5 and deaths 24.09 per 100,000 [2; figures as given on the slide; verify].

### Classification of late-life suicidal behaviour
- **Passive ideation:** wish to be dead. It is common and **not benign** in the elderly; it predicts later active ideation.
- **Active ideation with plan.**
- **Attempt:** often near-lethal.
- **Indirect self-destructive behaviour:** refusing food, fluids or medicines; neglect of illness.
- **Suicide in the context of terminal illness:** relevant to debates on assisted dying. Physician-assisted suicide is illegal in India; passive euthanasia is permitted under *Common Cause* (2018) [general knowledge].

### Mechanism: neurobiology [general knowledge]
- **Vascular depression:** white-matter hyperintensities and fronto-striatal disruption cause executive dysfunction. This contributes to **poor problem-solving and impulsivity**.
- **Serotonergic dysfunction** (low CSF 5-HIAA) and **HPA-axis** dysregulation.
- **Neurodegeneration:** suicide risk peaks soon after a diagnosis of dementia (insight phase), especially in younger-onset dementia and Huntington's disease.

### Clinical features: presentation clues
- Somatic complaints, insomnia, weight loss; "nothing can be done".
- **Giving away belongings, revising wills, saying goodbyes.**
- Refusal of food or treatment.
- Alcohol escalation after bereavement.
- The seminar case: a **65-year-old widower living alone**, unemployed through drinking, depressed, overdosed on lorazepam with alcohol. He was unhappy it failed and "nobody can help me". This is a **classic high-risk profile** [2].

### Assessment: specific tools
| Tool | Use |
| --- | --- |
| GDS-15 | Depression screening (fewer somatic items) |
| Geriatric Suicide Ideation Scale (GSIS) | Ideation, death ideation, loss of personal and social worth, perceived meaning in life |
| C-SSRS | Severity and behaviour [2] |
| Beck SIS | Intent after an attempt (15–19 low, 20–28 medium, 29+ high) [2] |
| BHS | Hopelessness (15+ severe) [2] |
| Reasons for Living Inventory (older-adult version) | Protective factors [2] |
| HMSE/MoCA | Cognition (affects capacity, safety planning) |

### Differential: suicidality vs related phenomena
- **Death wishes in terminal illness** vs depression: treatable depression is often present.
- **Delirium** with impulsive self-harm.
- **Psychotic depression** (nihilistic or Cotard delusions): very high risk, so give ECT.
- **Dementia** with disinhibition or accidental self-harm: intent is unclear.

### Management details: pharmacological safety in suicidal older patients [1]
- Prefer drugs **safe in overdose** (SSRIs, mirtazapine). **Avoid TCAs**.
- **Dispense small quantities**; the family supervises.
- **Benzodiazepines:** avoid. They cause falls and disinhibition, and in combination with alcohol they are lethal (as in the seminar case) [2].
- Interactions with polypharmacy; check sodium at 2–4 weeks on SSRIs.
- **Lithium:** reduce the dose; monitor eGFR; interactions (diuretics, ACE inhibitors, NSAIDs).

### Evidence table
| Study | Finding |
| --- | --- |
| Maudsley 15e [1] | Antidepressant response about 51% in the elderly; over 60% relapse within 2 years if withdrawn; OPTIMUM augmentation |
| PROSPECT (Bruce 2004, *JAMA*) [general knowledge] | Care management in primary care reduced suicidal ideation in older depressed patients |
| IMPACT (Unützer 2006) [general knowledge] | Collaborative care reduced suicidal ideation |
| De Leo 2002 TeleHelp/TeleCheck [general knowledge] | Fewer suicides than expected in elderly users |
| Oyama (Japan) community programmes [general knowledge] | Depression screening plus follow-up reduced elderly suicide, especially in women |
| Kellner 2016 PRIDE [general knowledge] | ECT plus venlafaxine effective in geriatric depression; continuation ECT reduces relapse |

### Critical appraisal
- **Psychological autopsy** depends on informant recall.
- Indian data are sparse and probably underestimate the problem.
- Collaborative-care trials are underpowered for suicide deaths.
- Ageism and **"rational suicide"** narratives can lead clinicians to under-treat.

### Indian context
- **Demographic transition:** the elderly are about 10% of the population, projected to reach about 20% by 2050 [general knowledge].
- **Changing family structure:** the joint family is giving way to nuclear families and migration of children.
- **Legal and policy:** the Maintenance and Welfare of Parents and Senior Citizens Act 2007; National Programme for Health Care of the Elderly (NPHCE); MHCA s115 [general knowledge].
- **NSPS:** gatekeeper training of community and faith leaders; DMHP suicide-prevention clinics [3].
- **Elderline 14567** helpline [general knowledge; verify].

### Recent advances
- **Ketamine/esketamine** in older adults [1].
- **Digital outreach** and loneliness interventions (befriending apps).
- **Machine learning** on primary-care records to flag risk.
- Brief contact interventions after attempts.
- Recognition of **suicide risk after a dementia diagnosis**: post-diagnostic support.
