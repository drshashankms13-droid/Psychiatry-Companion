## Exam answer

**Introduction.** Since the 1950s — chlorpromazine, imipramine, lithium — and especially since the "decade of the brain" (1990s), psychiatry has emphasised neurobiology, pharmacotherapy and neuromodulation. The statement claims that psychosocial interventions have consequently been sidelined. The evidence shows this is **partly true in practice and in perception, but false in terms of evidence and guidelines**: psychosocial treatments are as effective as drugs for many disorders, are first-line for several, and add to medication in most [1].

### Arguments that psychosocial interventions have "taken a back seat"

1. **Biomedical framing of illness:** mental disorders increasingly conceptualised as brain diseases; research funding and journal space dominated by neuroscience, genetics and pharmacology.
2. **Service realities:** medication is quicker and cheaper per consultation; psychiatrists in many countries (including India) spend most time prescribing; trained therapists are scarce.
3. **Help-seeking pattern:** most people with mental illness are now treated in **general medical settings** rather than by mental health specialists [1].
4. **Paradoxical stigma:** negative attitudes towards seeking mental health care have **increased** over five decades, partly because a medical conceptualisation implies permanence [1].
5. **Industry influence:** pharmaceutical marketing has no equivalent for psychotherapy.
6. **New biological treatments** (ketamine/esketamine, rTMS, xanomeline–trospium, psychedelics) attract attention and investment.

### Arguments against — psychosocial interventions remain central

**a. Evidence of efficacy and effectiveness [1]**
- Hundreds of meta-analyses: psychological treatments are **far more effective than no treatment**, **about as effective as biomedical treatments for most disorders**, and roughly equivalent to each other.
- A review of **269 meta-analyses**: CBT has medium–large effects for depression and most anxiety disorders, strong effects for insomnia, anger and bulimia, and small–medium effects for substance use, chronic pain, bipolar disorder and schizophrenia (with medication).
- Effectiveness studies show manualised therapies work in community settings with similar effect sizes; about **50% improve by session 8 and 75% by session 26**.

**b. Combined treatment is superior** — "perhaps the most consistent finding": psychotherapy and pharmacotherapy are equivalent at end of treatment and follow-up, and **combined treatment beats either alone** [1].

**c. Durability** — cognitive therapy has **better long-term outcomes** and lower relapse than medication in depression; psychodynamic therapy gains may persist after treatment ends [1].

**d. First-line status in guidelines** (author's knowledge, NICE and others)

| Condition | Psychosocial first line or essential |
| --- | --- |
| Mild–moderate depression | CBT, behavioural activation, IPT |
| Anxiety disorders, PTSD | CBT, exposure, trauma-focused CBT, EMDR |
| OCD | Exposure and response prevention |
| Insomnia | CBT-I before hypnotics |
| Borderline personality disorder | DBT, MBT — no drug licensed |
| Eating disorders | CBT-E, family-based treatment |
| Childhood disorders | Parent training (ADHD, ODD), behavioural interventions for autism |
| Schizophrenia | Family intervention, CBTp, supported employment (IPS), cognitive remediation alongside medication |
| Bipolar disorder | Psychoeducation, family-focused therapy, IPSRT, CBT alongside mood stabilisers |
| Substance use | Motivational interviewing, CBT, contingency management, mutual help |

**e. Recovery and function** — symptoms respond to drugs, but **disability, relationships, work and quality of life** depend on rehabilitation, family work and social inclusion.

**f. Common factors and the alliance** — the therapeutic alliance consistently predicts outcome across treatments, and also improves medication adherence [1]. Even drug treatment is delivered through a relationship.

**g. Biology and psychotherapy are not opposites** — psychotherapy produces measurable brain changes (e.g. normalisation of fronto-limbic activity after CBT), and environment affects gene expression. The biopsychosocial model (Engel, 1977) remains the organising framework.

**h. Patient preference** — many patients, especially older adults, prefer therapy to medication [1].

### Where psychosocial care is genuinely under-used

- Access: long waits, cost, too few therapists; minority and poorer patients drop out more and benefit less from conventional, culturally unadapted therapy [1].
- Training: psychiatry residents get less psychotherapy training than before.
- Research: psychotherapy trials are harder to fund and blind.

### The way forward

Task-sharing and lay-counsellor models (e.g. **Healthy Activity Programme**, **Friendship Bench**), digital and tele-delivered therapy, stepped care, culturally adapted interventions, measurement-based care, and training psychiatrists to integrate psychotherapy with prescribing.

**Conclusion.** The statement describes a real **imbalance in research funding, service delivery and public perception**, but not the evidence: psychosocial interventions are as effective as medication for many disorders, first line for several, essential for recovery in severe mental illness, and best combined with biological treatments. The task is not to choose between biology and psychotherapy but to make evidence-based psychosocial care accessible — particularly in India, where the treatment gap is wide and family and community resources are strong.

## In depth

### Background

- The 1950s psychopharmacological revolution and the 1980 DSM-III shift to descriptive diagnosis moved psychiatry away from psychoanalysis.
- **Engel's biopsychosocial model (1977)** argued for integrating biological, psychological and social factors.
- Since then, CBT, IPT, DBT, MBT, family interventions and IPS have built strong evidence bases through manualised RCTs [1].

### Efficacy vs effectiveness — why it matters for this debate [1]

| Type | Purpose | Method | Limitation |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Efficacy | Can the treatment work under ideal conditions? | RCT, screened patients, manuals, integrity checks | Limited generalisability |
| Effectiveness | Does it work in routine practice? | Community settings, comorbid patients, flexible length | Confounding |

Manualised treatments found efficacious in RCTs have been shown to be effective in community settings [1].

### Specific therapies — evidence summary [1]

| Therapy | Evidence |
| --- | --- |
| Cognitive therapy | 325 studies reviewed; well established for mood and anxiety; comparable to medication in depression, better tolerated, better long-term outcome; maintenance sessions reduce relapse |
| Behaviour therapy | Exposure and response prevention for OCD "extremely effective"; exposure for GAD, PTSD, panic, phobias; behavioural interventions for autism, ADHD, ODD, enuresis, self-injury in intellectual disability |
| CBT | Most extensively studied; less support as monotherapy for bipolar disorder and schizophrenia |
| Third-wave (ACT, DBT, mindfulness) | ACT for depression, OCD, substance use, PTSD; DBT reduces self-injury in BPD; mindfulness for depression, anxiety, substance use |
| IPT | Large effect vs placebo for depression; IPT plus medication prevents relapse better than medication alone; adapted for adolescents, postpartum depression, bulimia, BED |
| Psychodynamic | As effective as CBT in trials; gains may be better retained |

### Treatment variables [1]

- **Alliance** predicts outcome regardless of therapy type; ruptures and repairs matter; early alliance predicts outcome better than late.
- **Therapist effects:** about **30% of therapists consistently outperform** colleagues; a small number account for most harmful outcomes; empathy, genuineness and warmth predict success; experience alone does not.
- **Patient factors:** motivation, expectation of help, attachment style; personality disorder predicts poorer outcome; higher baseline severity needs longer treatment.
- **Matching:** ethnic match improves retention; shared beliefs about causes and treatment of illness may matter more — therapists should respect religious and indigenous explanations [1].

### Critical appraisal

- **Allegiance bias, unblinded designs and waitlist controls** inflate psychotherapy effects; drug trials have their own biases (industry funding, publication bias).
- **Dodo bird verdict** ("all have won") — equivalence between therapies supports common factors but may also reflect weak trial designs.
- **Generalisability:** most evidence comes from Western, educated samples; 90% of US psychotherapy patients were European American in national surveys [1].
- **Cost-effectiveness:** psychotherapy has higher upfront costs but lower relapse; task-shared models reduce costs substantially.

### Indian context

- Psychiatrist-to-population ratio is low and clinical psychologists fewer still; outpatient consultations are short, favouring prescriptions.
- Strong family involvement makes **family psychoeducation** and **community-based rehabilitation** natural fits.
- **Indian evidence for task-shared psychosocial care** (author's knowledge — verify): Healthy Activity Programme for depression and Counselling for Alcohol Problems (PREMIUM trials, Goa, Lancet 2017); COPSI community care for schizophrenia (Lancet 2014); MANAS lay-counsellor trial (Goa, 2010).
- **Tele-MANAS** and NIMHANS digital programmes increase access to brief psychological support.
- The **Mental Healthcare Act 2017** guarantees the right to access mental health care, including community-based and psychosocial services, and the National Mental Health Policy 2014 emphasises them.

### Recent advances

- Digital CBT and app-based interventions with guided support.
- Measurement-based care and routine outcome monitoring improve psychotherapy outcomes.
- Precision approaches: predicting who responds to CBT vs medication (e.g. imaging and clinical predictors) remain research tools.
- Psychedelic-assisted psychotherapy illustrates the convergence of biological and psychological treatment.
