## Exam answer

### Introduction [1, 2]
- Inadequate acute treatment of migraine carries a large socioeconomic burden and is associated with **transition from episodic to chronic migraine**.
- Recent advances fall into five areas:
  1. **new mechanism-based drugs** (CGRP pathway, 5-HT1F);
  2. **neuromodulation devices**;
  3. **new formulations and delivery**;
  4. **better treatment strategies** (stratified care, outcome measurement, lifestyle and comorbidity);
  5. **new understanding of the attack phases**, which opens new targets.

### 1. CGRP-targeted therapy [1, 2]
- **Rationale:** a large body of research implicates **calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP)** in migraine pathophysiology.
- **Two drug types:**
  - **small-molecule receptor antagonists ("gepants")**;
  - **monoclonal antibodies** against either the **CGRP receptor** or the **CGRP ligand**.
- **Prevention, anti-CGRP monoclonal antibodies:**
  - the **only drug class designed specifically to prevent migraine**;
  - phase 2 and 3 trials show **efficacy, tolerability and safety in episodic and chronic migraine**;
  - **erenumab** (a **CGRP-receptor** antibody): **FDA-approved May 2018**; **70 mg or 140 mg monthly**;
    - no contraindications on the FDA label;
    - common adverse effects: **injection-site reactions, constipation**;
  - **ligand-targeting antibodies** were in the pipeline in 2018.
- **Acute, gepants:**
  - **ubrogepant** was the only gepant in **phase 3** for acute treatment;
  - phase 2b: **2-hour pain freedom 25.5% vs 8.9%** with 100 mg.
- **Cardiovascular advantage:** CGRP antagonism **does not cause vasoconstriction**, so these drugs may suit patients with cardiovascular risk who cannot take triptans or ergots.

### 2. 5-HT1F agonists ("ditans") [1]
- **Lasmiditan**: no vasoconstriction; anti-inflammatory.
- Phase 3: **2-hour pain freedom 32.2% vs 15.3%** at 200 mg.
- Possible use in patients with **cardiovascular risk**. Further research is needed.

### 3. Neuromodulation (non-invasive and invasive) [1, 2, 6]

| Device | Use | Evidence (per source) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Single-pulse TMS** | Acute treatment, **migraine with aura**; also cleared for prevention | FDA 2014; 2-hour pain-free **39% vs 22%** sham |
| **Non-invasive vagus nerve stimulation** | Acute treatment (and preventive data) | **FDA approval January 2018** (migraine pain); open-label studies |
| **Transcutaneous supraorbital nerve stimulation** | Acute treatment and **prevention** (FDA-cleared for prevention) | Open-label: pain reduced 57% at 1 hour |
| **Caloric vestibular stimulation** | Prevention | FDA-cleared, not yet marketed in 2018 |
| **Occipital, sphenopalatine and supraorbital implanted stimulators** | Very severe refractory migraine | Limited role; more studies needed |

- Neuromodulation and **peripheral nerve blocks** have **promising safety in pregnancy** [6].

### 4. New formulations and procedures [1]
- **Breath-powered intranasal sumatriptan powder (22 mg)**.
- **Diclofenac potassium powder for oral solution**: faster onset; 2-hour pain freedom better than tablets.
- **Sumatriptan/naproxen 85/500 mg**: superior to either drug alone, and effective even in **triptan non-responders**.
- **Withdrawn:** the **sumatriptan iontophoretic patch** (burns, scarring).
- **Peripheral nerve blocks** (greater occipital, supratrochlear, supraorbital):
  - limited controlled evidence, but used by **69%** of headache specialists;
  - **long-lasting benefit** and **reversal of allodynia** reported.

### 5. Smarter use of existing treatment [1, 2]

**Acute treatment:**
- **Stratified care**: the patient matches treatment to attack severity (NSAID for mild, triptan for severe). It beats step care across or within attacks, with **higher satisfaction and lower cost**.
- **Evidence-based first line (AHS 2015 / CHS 2013):** all **triptans**, **acetaminophen**, **aspirin, ibuprofen, naproxen, diclofenac**, **intranasal dihydroergotamine**.
  - Triptans: 2-hour pain relief **42–76%** vs 27% on placebo.
- **Recommend against routine use:** opioids, butalbital; butorphanol is strongly discouraged by the CHS.
- **Emergency department (AHS):** **should offer metoclopramide, prochlorperazine, subcutaneous sumatriptan**; may offer others; **opioids may be avoided**.
- **Measure response with the mTOQ-4** (4 items, 0–8). **Poor scores predict progression to chronic migraine** within a year.
- **Troubleshoot before switching:**
  - common failures are under-dosing, late treatment, no second dose, wrong route (use **nasal or injectable when there is vomiting**) and rapid time to peak;
  - try **2 adequate trials** before changing drug.

**Prevention:**
- **Offer prevention** when any of these apply:
  - **3 or more moderate–severe disabling headache days a month** not consistently responsive to acute treatment;
  - **6–8 or more headache days a month**;
  - contraindications to acute drugs;
  - **brainstem aura or hemiplegic migraine**;
  - significant life impact;
  - risk of **medication-overuse headache**.
- **Most favourable options** (efficacy plus tolerability):
  - **amitriptyline**, **β-blockers** (propranolol 80–240 mg, metoprolol 100–200 mg), **topiramate** (50–200 mg), **candesartan** (16–32 mg);
  - **onabotulinumtoxinA** (155 units every 12 weeks, **chronic migraine only**);
  - **coenzyme Q10, magnesium citrate, riboflavin** (nutraceuticals);
  - **biofeedback, CBT, relaxation**.
- **Valproate**: Level A efficacy but only a **"weak" CHS recommendation** because of **teratogenicity and liver toxicity**.
- **Butterbur** guideline retired (manufacturing safety concerns).
- **Benefit takes 2–3 months** at target dose. **Adherence is poor**: 26–29% at 6 months and 17–20% at 12 months for oral preventives in chronic migraine, so check adherence before calling a drug a failure.
- **Combination therapy:** use complementary mechanisms. **Topiramate plus propranolol was no better than topiramate alone** in chronic migraine. Don't add a drug just to treat another drug's side effects.

**Lifestyle and modifiable risk factors:**
- regular sleep, meals and exercise; **150 minutes a week of moderate aerobic exercise**;
- headache diary;
- **caffeine cessation for 2–3 months** (taper);
- **treat sleep disorders** (CBT-I and CPAP reduce headache frequency);
- weight loss.
- **Medication-overuse thresholds:**
  - **15 or more days a month** for simple analgesics;
  - **10 or more days a month** for triptans, ergots, combination analgesics, opioids or mixed classes;
  - **butalbital and opioids** are risky even below 10 days.

### 6. Pathophysiology-driven advances [3, 4, 5]
- **Premonitory phase:**
  - functional imaging (since 2014) shows **hypothalamic, substantia nigra, dorsal pontine and limbic (anterior cingulate)** activation **before pain**, with altered hypothalamic–brainstem connectivity;
  - this points to **dopaminergic and hypothalamic targets** and to treating attacks **early**.
- **Aura:**
  - aura spreads **linearly along gyri or sulci**, not as a concentric wave;
  - **cortical spreading depression** is a valid animal model but **not proven in humans**, and aura does not appear to trigger the headache;
  - **"migraine with brainstem aura"** replaces "basilar migraine" (ICHD-3);
  - aura is linked to **patent foramen ovale and stroke**;
  - **triptans are not contraindicated in aura** (no evidence of harm).
- **Postdrome:**
  - **81–94%** have postdrome symptoms (tiredness, poor concentration, neck stiffness);
  - widespread reduced blood flow on imaging;
  - **triptans do not switch off** the underlying diencephalic and brainstem activity;
  - recognising the postdrome avoids **unnecessary investigation**.
- **Pipeline targets beyond CGRP:** **PACAP**, **kappa-opioid receptors**, **nitric oxide synthase**, **orexins**, **glutamate**.

### 7. Special populations [1, 6, 7]
- **SSRIs/SNRIs with triptans:**
  - the AHS position paper found that **only 10 of 27** FDA-alert cases met serotonin syndrome criteria;
  - estimated annual incidence **under 0.03%**, life-threatening **under 0.002%**;
  - a **Level U** recommendation: data are **inadequate to support added risk**;
  - children on SSRIs/SNRIs generally **need not be denied triptans** [7].
  - ⚠️ This differs from Maudsley 15e, which lists SSRI plus triptan as a serotonin-syndrome interaction (see the migraine answers). **Not reconciled; flagged for the reviewer.**
- **Pregnancy:**
  - acetaminophen first line; **metoclopramide** the preferred antiemetic;
  - **avoid ergots**, NSAIDs in the first and third trimesters, and **butalbital** (cardiac defects);
  - triptans may be **safer than once thought**;
  - **nerve blocks and neuromodulation** are attractive options;
  - migraine is a risk factor for **pre-eclampsia**;
  - in breastfeeding, **eletriptan** is most compatible.
- **Children and adolescents:**
  - four triptans are FDA-labelled for adolescents; **rizatriptan from age 6**;
  - the **CHAMP** approach (lifestyle, optimal acute treatment, preventive) improves about **60%**;
  - **CBT plus drug prevention** for chronic migraine (ages 10–17).

**Conclusion.** Up to 2018, the main advances in migraine management were:
- the first **mechanism-specific preventive**, the **anti-CGRP monoclonal antibody** (erenumab, May 2018), with ligand-targeted antibodies following;
- **non-vasoconstrictive acute agents** (gepants such as ubrogepant; ditans such as lasmiditan) for patients with vascular risk;
- **FDA-cleared neuromodulation** (sTMS, nVNS, supraorbital stimulation);
- new delivery systems (nasal powder, diclofenac solution);
- a more strategic approach: stratified care, mTOQ-4 monitoring, defined prevention thresholds, medication-overuse limits, lifestyle, sleep and weight management, behavioural therapy, and attention to adherence.

Imaging of the premonitory phase (hypothalamus) and the postdrome is reshaping the model of the attack and identifying new targets (PACAP, orexin, glutamate). **Developments after 2018 are not covered by this source.**
