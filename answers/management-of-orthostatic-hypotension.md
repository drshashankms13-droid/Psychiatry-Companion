## Exam answer

**Definition [1].**
- Orthostatic (postural) hypotension is a **fall of 20 mmHg or more in systolic BP and/or 10 mmHg or more in diastolic BP within 3 minutes of standing**.
- **Symptoms:** dizziness, light-headedness, asthenia, headache, visual disturbance.
- **Subjective postural dizziness correlates only weakly** with the measured BP fall, so **measure BP** (supine and standing) when it is suspected [1, 2].
- **Consequences:** reduced quality of life, and a higher risk of **falls, cardiovascular disease, depression and death** [1].

### Causes
**Drug-related (the commonest concern in psychiatry) [1, 3]:**
- It is **one of the commonest cardiovascular adverse effects of antipsychotics** and some antidepressants. It usually appears during **initial titration**, but can be chronic.
- **α1-adrenergic blockade** is the mechanism. Incidence:
  - highest with **clozapine (24%), quetiapine (27%), iloperidone (19.5%)**;
  - lowest with lurasidone and asenapine (under 2%);
  - among FGAs, **low-potency phenothiazines** (chlorpromazine).
- **TCAs** cause orthostatic hypotension, exaggerated with diuretics, vasodilators or benzodiazepines; trazodone and nefazodone too [2].
- **Levodopa** when it is started in Parkinson's disease [3].
- Antihypertensives, diuretics and **alcohol** [3].

**Autonomic failure [3, 4]:**
- **multisystem atrophy** (especially MSA-A, Shy–Drager), with ED, loss of sweating and urinary symptoms;
- autonomic neuropathy (**diabetes, amyloid, alcohol**);
- Parkinson's disease.

**Volume depletion and dehydration** [1, 3].

### Risk factors (Table 1.40) [1]
- **Treatment:**
  - **IM route** (higher, faster peaks);
  - **rapid dose increases**;
  - antipsychotic **polypharmacy**;
  - **interactions**: TCAs, antihypertensives (especially α-blockers, β-blockers, diuretics; the *number* of antihypertensives may matter more than the class).
- **Patient:**
  - **old age** (the young may show tachycardia with little BP change);
  - autonomic dysfunction (Parkinson's disease);
  - dehydration;
  - cardiovascular disease.

### Assessment [1, 2]
- **Supine and standing BP**, and an ECG.
- **Tilt testing** can show orthostatic hypotension, but its reproducibility, sensitivity and specificity are disappointing [2].
- Distinguish it from other causes of **syncope**: vasovagal, cardiac arrhythmia or structural disease (urgent if syncope is exertional), carotid sinus hypersensitivity [2, 3].
- Review **all drugs** and their timing.

### Management (Table 1.41) [1, 2]
**1. Minimise drug risk:**
- start low and **titrate slowly**; most people **develop tolerance** to the hypotensive effect;
- **temporary dose reduction** if hypotension develops;
- reduce peaks with **smaller, more frequent doses** or **modified-release** forms;
- switch to an agent with lower α1 affinity where possible.

**2. Non-pharmacological:**
- **rise slowly**: sit on the edge of the bed for several minutes before standing in the morning;
- **abdominal binders and compression stockings** [1, 2];
- **fluids 1.25–2.5 L/day** unless fluid-restricted;
- avoid precipitants: alcohol, sleep deprivation, fasting, dehydration, **prolonged standing**; lie down when presyncopal [2].

**3. Pharmacological** (when treatment is compelling, for example with clozapine, and other measures fail) [1, 2]:
- **sodium chloride** supplementation (may help antidepressant-induced cases);
- **fludrocortisone** for clozapine-induced orthostatic hypotension. Monitor electrolytes and BP; **long-term use is not recommended**;
- **midodrine** (an α1 agonist): **second line** for clozapine-induced cases or where fludrocortisone is unsuitable. It is linked to **acute dystonia** with antipsychotics;
- other sympathomimetics: evidence is lacking. **Etilefrine** has an unfavourable risk–benefit balance;
- K&S lists **compression stockings, α-adrenergic agonists, salt supplements or fludrocortisone** [2].

**Prognosis [2]:** unlike syncope from heart disease, orthostatic hypotension shows **no increase in long-term mortality or MI** in follow-up data.

**Conclusion.** Confirm with standing BP. Then:
- identify drug and autonomic causes;
- titrate psychotropics slowly and use divided or modified-release doses;
- use posture, fluid, stocking and binder measures;
- reserve salt, fludrocortisone or midodrine for refractory cases, such as clozapine-treated patients.
