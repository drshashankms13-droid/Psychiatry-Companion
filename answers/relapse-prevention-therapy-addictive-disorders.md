## Exam answer

**Definition.** **Relapse prevention (RP)** is a cognitive-behavioural treatment developed by **G. Alan Marlatt and Judith Gordon (1985)**. It teaches people with addictive behaviours to **anticipate, recognise and cope with high-risk situations**, and to manage a **lapse** so that it does not become a full **relapse**. It is used as a stand-alone treatment or as the maintenance phase after detoxification.

Its basic assumption is that treatment effects do not automatically endure. On returning to the old environment, skills are forgotten, motivation falls and the problem re-emerges, so maintenance has to be actively trained [1].

- **Lapse:** a single, initial episode of use after a period of abstinence.
- **Relapse:** return to the previous pattern of use. RP treats relapse as a **process**, not an event [1].

### Mechanism: cognitive-behavioural model of relapse
**High-risk situation (HRS)** →
- **Effective coping response** → ↑ self-efficacy → ↓ probability of relapse.
- **No or ineffective coping** → ↓ self-efficacy + **positive outcome expectancies** ("a drink will help me relax") → **lapse** → **abstinence violation effect (AVE)**: guilt, self-blame and loss of perceived control ("I've blown it anyway") + perceived positive effects → ↑ probability of **relapse** [1].

**Covert antecedents** set up the HRS:
- **Lifestyle imbalance** (too many "shoulds", too few "wants") → desire for indulgence → urges and cravings → rationalisation, denial → **seemingly irrelevant decisions (SIDs)** that lead into the HRS.
- Example of an SID: someone whose daytime drinking triggers binges agrees to an afternoon meeting at a restaurant that serves alcohol [1].
- **Problem of immediate gratification:** short-term positive effects are weighed above long-term harm [1].

### Determinants of relapse (Marlatt's taxonomy)

| Intrapersonal | Interpersonal |
| --- | --- |
| **Negative emotional states** (anger, anxiety, depression, boredom, loneliness, fatigue) — the commonest precipitant | **Interpersonal conflict** (family, work) |
| Negative physical states (pain, withdrawal) | **Social pressure** to use: direct (offers) or indirect (modelling, a friend ordering a drink) |
| Positive emotional states (celebration) | Positive emotional states with others |
| Testing personal control ("I can handle just one") | — |
| Urges and temptations, craving (cue-induced) | — |

Other key intrapersonal factors: **self-efficacy** (higher Situational Confidence Questionnaire scores predict longer time to relapse), outcome expectancies, coping skills, craving and motivation. **Positive social support** strongly predicts abstinence [1].

### Assessment
- Past relapse analysis: triggers, thoughts, feelings and behaviours before lapses [1].
- **Self-monitoring diary** of urges and situations [1].
- Scales: Inventory of Drinking Situations / Situational Confidence Questionnaire, Alcohol Expectancy Questionnaire, Penn Alcohol Craving Scale, readiness to change (SOCRATES).
- Co-morbidity screen (depression, anxiety, personality) [1].

### Management: specific RP techniques
1. **Identify and cope with HRS:** a list of personal high-risk and low-risk situations; learn warning signs (stress, SIDs, positive expectancies) [1].
2. **Coping skills training:** drink or drug refusal (assertiveness), relaxation, meditation, positive self-talk, problem-solving [1].
3. **Enhance self-efficacy:** client and therapist as equal partners; break goals into small achievable steps; positive feedback. Recovery is about **skills, not willpower** [1].
4. **Eliminate myths and placebo effects:** challenge expectancies with a **decisional matrix** of pros and cons of using vs abstaining, in the short and long term [1].
5. **Lapse management:** a contract to limit use, call the therapist promptly, and analyse triggers [1].
6. **Cognitive restructuring of the AVE:** reframe a lapse as a **learning opportunity**, not a failure. Challenge cognitive errors such as "relapse can't happen to me", "a few drinks won't hurt", "my problem is cured" [1].
7. **Balanced lifestyle and "positive addictions":** exercise, hobbies, meditation, daily schedule, time management [1].
8. **Stimulus control:** remove bottles, glasses and paraphernalia; change cue-linked habits (a favourite chair, music); decline drinking events [1].
9. **Urge surfing:** label the urge, observe it rise, peak and pass like a wave, without acting on it or fighting it [1].
10. **Relapse road maps:** map choices and their outcomes to spot SIDs early [1].

RP is combined with **anticraving drugs** (naltrexone, acamprosate, baclofen, disulfiram), **motivational interviewing**, family involvement and mutual-help groups.

### Key evidence
- **Carroll (1996)**, review of 24 RCTs: RP beats no treatment, most strongly for smoking. It is not consistently superior to other active treatments, but it reduces **relapse severity**, has **more durable** effects, and helps patients with more severe dependence or psychopathology [1].
- **Irvin et al. (1999)**, meta-analysis of 26 studies, n=9,504: RP is effective, most for **alcohol and polysubstance use**, when **combined with medication**, and immediately after treatment. It is equally effective individually or in groups, inpatient or outpatient [1].
- **MBRP** (Bowen 2014, n=286): at 12 months, fewer drug-use days and less heavy drinking than RP or treatment as usual [2].

**Conclusion.** Relapse prevention reframes relapse as a predictable, learnable process rather than a moral failure. Its core ideas (high-risk situations, coping, self-efficacy, the abstinence violation effect, lifestyle balance) underpin almost all modern addiction psychotherapy. The evidence supports it as an effective maintenance treatment, especially for alcohol and when combined with anticraving medication, though its taxonomy predicts relapse poorly. The dynamic model and mindfulness-based RP address that weakness.

## In depth

### Background
- **Historical context:** relapse rates of 60–90% within a year were observed across alcohol, tobacco and opioids (Hunt et al. 1971 survival curves). This showed that achieving abstinence is easier than maintaining it.
- **Marlatt and Gordon (1985)**, *Relapse Prevention: Maintenance Strategies in the Treatment of Addictive Behaviors*, built a cognitive-behavioural model drawing on **social learning theory** (Bandura's self-efficacy) and attribution theory [1].
- **2nd edition (2005)** by Marlatt and Donovan. **Witkiewitz and Marlatt (2004)** reconceptualised relapse as a **dynamic, non-linear** process [1].
- **2000s:** **Mindfulness-Based Relapse Prevention (MBRP)** was developed (Bowen, Chawla, Marlatt) [1].

### Epidemiology of relapse
- **Precipitants in Marlatt's original analysis** (alcohol, smoking, heroin, gambling, overeating):
  - Negative emotional states: about 35%.
  - Social pressure: about 20%.
  - Interpersonal conflict: about 16%.
  - Intrapersonal determinants overall: just over half.
- **NIMHANS and Indian follow-up studies** of alcohol dependence show relapse in most patients within 6–12 months of discharge without aftercare. Exact figures vary by setting, so check the original papers.
- **Ongoing depression** increases relapse risk during and after alcohol treatment. Sudden rises in negative affect immediately precede nicotine relapse [1].

### Classification and criteria
- **ICD-11** codes "in early full remission", "sustained partial remission" and "sustained full remission". **DSM-5-TR** specifies "in early remission" (3–12 months) and "in sustained remission" (≥12 months), excluding craving.
- In RP language:
  - **Lapse** = slip.
  - **Relapse** = return to the problem pattern.
  - **Prolapse** = a lapse that leads back towards the abstinence goal, a "learning" outcome.

### Mechanism in detail

| Determinant | Definition | Evidence / clinical point |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Self-efficacy | Confidence in ability to perform a coping behaviour in a specific situation | Situational Confidence Questionnaire predicts time to relapse; the relationship is bidirectional [1] |
| Outcome expectancies | Anticipated effects of use (not necessarily real effects) | Positive expectancies are a risk factor; negative ones are protective; heavy drinkers expect more benefit; "self-medication" in HRS [1] |
| Emotional states | Affect regulation as the motive for use | Baker's **negative-reinforcement model**: escape from negative affect drives use [1] |
| Craving and urge | Craving = cognitive desire; urge = behavioural intention | Triggered by interoceptive or exteroceptive cues; cue-reactivity is not a consistent predictor of relapse; loss of control facilitates relapse [1] |
| Coping | Thoughts and behaviours managing stressful demands | Shiffman: **stress coping** vs **temptation coping**; approach vs avoidance coping; "inaction" or acceptance (urge surfing) [1] |
| Motivation | Motivation to change vs motivation to use | Ambivalence; addressed by motivational interviewing [1] |
| Social support | Positive vs negative | Support from the single most supportive person and number of non-drinking friends predicts abstinence; family withdrawal or avoidance is linked to more drinking [1] |

**Dynamic model (Witkiewitz & Marlatt 2004):**
- **Distal risks:** temperament, personality, family loading, socio-economic background.
- **Proximal risks:** the determinants above.
- These interact **non-linearly**. Small changes, such as a minor rise in stress, can cause sudden large shifts ("catastrophe"-type relapse), with feedback loops between coping, self-efficacy and affect [1].

**Neurobiology:** the classic cue–craving link involves the mesolimbic dopamine system and associative learning (cues gain incentive salience). Stress-induced relapse involves CRF and noradrenaline. Prefrontal hypofunction impairs inhibitory control. These explain why stimulus control, stress management and skills rehearsal help [1].

### Clinical features in detail: the relapse process
A typical chain:
1. Life stress and lifestyle imbalance build up.
2. SIDs follow (a different route home past the liquor shop; "keeping a bottle for guests").
3. These lead to an HRS (a friend's party after a fight with the spouse).
4. There is no rehearsed refusal response, so the person expects alcohol to ease the tension.
5. One drink (lapse) → AVE ("I'm a failure") → binge (relapse).

RP intervenes at every link [1].

### Assessment in detail
- **Functional analysis** of the last 3–5 lapses: antecedents, behaviour, consequences.
- **Instruments:**
  - **Inventory of Drinking Situations (IDS-100)**, which maps onto Marlatt's 8 categories.
  - Situational Confidence Questionnaire (SCQ).
  - Drug-Taking Confidence Questionnaire.
  - Alcohol Expectancy Questionnaire.
  - Obsessive Compulsive Drinking Scale (craving).
- **Ecological momentary assessment** (phone or IVR prompts) captures dynamic real-time risk [1].

### Differential: related psychosocial treatments

| Treatment | Focus | Relationship to RP |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Motivational interviewing / MET | Resolving ambivalence | Often precedes RP |
| CBT coping skills training | Broader skills | RP is a CBT-based form |
| Cue exposure therapy | Extinguishing cue reactivity | Adjunct |
| MBRP | Mindful awareness of urges | Third-wave adaptation of RP |
| 12-step facilitation | Mutual help, spirituality | Complementary |
| Contingency management | Rewards for abstinence | Different (operant) mechanism |
| Community reinforcement approach | Rewarding non-using lifestyle | Overlaps with "lifestyle balance" |

### Management details
- **Format:** 8–12 weekly individual or group sessions, then boosters. Group RP is as effective as individual RP [1].
- **Pharmacological combination** (RP + medication is most effective [1]):
  - Naltrexone 50 mg/day.
  - Acamprosate 666 mg three times daily (weight ≥60 kg).
  - Disulfiram 250 mg/day, supervised by family.
  - Baclofen 30–80 mg/day (especially with liver disease).
  - Topiramate.
  - Opioid substitution (buprenorphine, methadone) for opioid use disorder.
  - Varenicline or NRT for smoking.
- **Family involvement:** in India, family-supervised disulfiram and family sessions on non-confrontational support.
- **Special groups:**
  - Dual diagnosis: integrate RP with treatment of depression or anxiety, since negative affect is the commonest trigger.
  - Adolescents: peer-pressure skills.
  - Women: interpersonal triggers and trauma.
- **RP beyond substances:**
  - **Sex offenders:** a 2003 meta-analysis showed moderate reductions in recidivism. Training significant others and identifying the offence chain were the active elements; boosters and coping skills had weaker effects [1].
  - **Eating disorders**, combined with CBT and problem-solving [1].
  - Gambling.
  - Depression (relapse prevention plans).

### Evidence table

| Study | Design | n | Result |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Carroll 1996 | Review of controlled trials | 24 RCTs | RP > no treatment (strongest for smoking); not consistently superior to active treatments; reduces relapse severity [1] |
| Irvin et al. 1999 | Meta-analysis | 26 studies, 9,504 participants | Effective, especially alcohol/polysubstance, with medication, measured soon after treatment; group = individual; inpatient = outpatient [1] |
| McCrady (review of AUD psychotherapies) | Review | — | RP classified as efficacious [1] |
| Hajek et al. 2013, Cochrane (smoking) | Systematic review | — | Insufficient evidence for any specific behavioural RP intervention after quitting [1] |
| RREP (Longabaugh et al. 1996) | Multisite replication | 3 sites | Marlatt's taxonomy had variable reliability and little predictive validity [1] |
| Bowen et al. 2014 | RCT: MBRP vs RP vs TAU | 286 | RP and MBRP better than TAU; at 12 months MBRP had fewer drug-use days and less heavy drinking [2] |

### Critical appraisal
- **Practice outpaced validation.** RP spread widely in the 1980s before its assumptions were tested [1].
- **RREP findings:** Marlatt's taxonomy had variable inter-rater reliability across sites and little predictive validity from pre-treatment relapse data. An expanded alternative taxonomy added little [1].
- **Linear model too simple.** The dynamic model answers this but is hard to test. It needs intensive repeated measurement such as EMA [1].
- **Specificity:** RP is rarely better than other active CBT or MET, so the "active ingredients" are unclear [1].
- **Smoking:** little evidence for RP after quitting [1].
- **Cultural fit:** individualistic self-efficacy framing may need adapting to family-centred Indian settings.
- **Strengths:** structured, teachable, adaptable to groups and non-specialists; compatible with harm-reduction goals; reduces the stigma of relapse.

### Indian context
- RP is a core component of treatment at NIMHANS (Centre for Addiction Medicine), AIIMS (NDDTC) and PGIMER de-addiction centres, usually alongside anticraving medication and family work. A review of RP in Indian practice appears in the *Indian Journal of Psychiatry* psychotherapy supplement [1].
- **Indian high-risk situations:** festivals and weddings; social drinking at work events; peer drinking among men; easy availability (licensed outlets, illicit liquor); financial stress.
- **Family:** an important protective factor. Expressed emotion and criticism, by contrast, are triggers.
- **Policy:**
  - NDPS Act, 1985, Section 64A: immunity from prosecution for addicts who volunteer for treatment.
  - Drug De-addiction Programme (DDAP).
  - National Action Plan for Drug Demand Reduction (NAPDDR) with Integrated Rehabilitation Centres for Addicts.
  - Nasha Mukt Bharat Abhiyaan.
  - **MHCA 2017** recognises substance use disorders as mental illness, with rights to treatment.
- **Mutual help:** Alcoholics Anonymous groups are available in major cities.

### Recent advances
- **Established:** Mindfulness-Based Relapse Prevention (MBRP) [1].
- **Emerging:**
  - Ecological momentary assessment and smartphone "just-in-time" adaptive interventions to detect rising risk [1].
  - Digital RP apps (for example, A-CHESS).
  - Neurocognitive markers of relapse (impulsivity, cue-reactivity fMRI) and genetic markers [1].
- **Investigational:** identifying CBT "active ingredients"; integrating neural circuitry and genetic markers into relapse models [1].
