## Exam answer

**Introduction.** Many people with depression, bipolar disorder and psychosis/schizophrenia have **raised inflammatory markers**: cytokines (IL-6, TNF-α), acute-phase proteins and activated microglia [1].
- **Effects of chronic low-grade inflammation on the brain** [1]:
  - altered neurotransmitter function;
  - impaired neuroplasticity and neurogenesis;
  - disrupted HPA-axis stress responses;
  - altered neurocircuitry.
- **Why anti-inflammatory treatment makes sense:** conventional psychotropics act mainly on monoamines. Adjunctive anti-inflammatory treatment could address a **contributing cause** in the subset of patients whose illness is inflammation-linked [1].

### Agents studied [1, 2]

| Class | Examples | Source comment |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **NSAIDs / COX-2 inhibitors** | Celecoxib, aspirin, ibuprofen, naproxen | Studied in depression, bipolar disorder and psychosis [1] |
| **Antibiotics with anti-inflammatory/neuroprotective action** | **Minocycline** | Promise in schizophrenia and mood disorders [1]; see Maudsley details below [2] |
| **Other immunomodulators** | Statins, **omega-3 PUFAs**, neurosteroids, cytokine-modulating drugs (experimental) | [1] |
| Other adjuncts with inflammatory properties | **N-acetylcysteine**, oestrogens | Listed by Maudsley as anti-inflammatory adjuncts to antipsychotics [2, p229] |
| **Conventional psychotropics** | Some antidepressants, antipsychotics, mood stabilisers | Lower pro-inflammatory and raise anti-inflammatory cytokines. Part of their benefit may come from this [1] |

### Evidence by disorder

**Depression and bipolar disorder:**
- **Meta-analysis:** as add-ons, anti-inflammatory treatments have a **modest but significant effect** on depressive symptoms versus placebo [1].
- **Who benefits:** celecoxib, minocycline and statins have shown benefit, **especially with raised baseline inflammation** (cytokines, CRP) [1].
- **Maudsley, TRD (Table 3.5):** **minocycline 200 mg/day** has "several positive meta-analyses in both animals and humans", but **recent RCTs failed**. Maudsley lists it under "other reported treatments", with limited support [2, p353].

**Schizophrenia and psychosis:**
- **General evidence:** aspirin, minocycline and COX-2 inhibitors may help, perhaps mostly in early phases or inflammation-associated subtypes [1].
- **Maudsley summary (Table 1.54):** anti-inflammatory adjuncts (NAC, NSAIDs, minocycline, oestrogens, aspirin, omega-3) are a heterogeneous group. They show **possible benefit on negative and cognitive symptoms, but sample sizes are small**. Evidence rating: **"very weak ±"** [2, p229].
- **Minocycline in refractory schizophrenia (Table 1.53):** 200 mg/day plus antipsychotic. An open study (n = 22) and an RCT (n = 54) suggest benefit for negative and cognitive symptoms. An RCT (n = 52) of clozapine augmentation improved some symptoms, and there is RCT evidence of a neuroprotective effect in early psychosis [2, p227]. Minocycline has low toxicity and good tolerability [2, p226].
- **Negative evidence:**
  - a relatively large RCT found adjunctive minocycline **not efficacious for negative symptoms**;
  - the **BeneMin** study found **no protection** against negative symptoms when it was given early in schizophrenia [2, p35];
  - for clozapine augmentation, "minocycline is probably not effective", and N-acetylcysteine is probably of no benefit [2, p222].
- **Omega-3:** discussed by Maudsley in a separate section on schizophrenia, including transition prevention [2, p111]. Not detailed here.

### Cautions [1]
- **Benefit is limited to subgroups**, probably those with raised inflammatory markers. This is not "anti-inflammation for everyone".
- **NSAID risks:** gastrointestinal and cardiovascular harm. NSAIDs may **worsen depression or reduce antidepressant efficacy** in some cases.
- **Short trials:** most last weeks to months. Long-term safety, durability and relapse prevention are uncertain.
- **Inflammation is not always harmful:** immune activity can be protective (repair, neurogenesis, homeostasis), so indiscriminate suppression may backfire.

### Conceptual significance and future directions [1]
- **Beyond monoamines:** the monoamine-only hypothesis is incomplete. A combined model of **neuroinflammation, neurotransmitters, neurocircuits and psychosocial stress** better explains onset, relapse and prognosis.
- **Precision psychopharmacology:** measure **CRP and IL-6** and tailor anti-inflammatory plus psychotropic treatment.
- **Likely target groups:**
  - treatment-resistant depression and bipolar depression;
  - illness with comorbid systemic inflammation;
  - early psychosis with neuroinflammatory markers.
- **Needed:**
  - large, long RCTs;
  - **biomarker-defined immune subtypes** to predict response;
  - long-term safety data (interactions, microbiome);
  - interactions with trauma, early adversity, substance use and medical comorbidity, which are especially relevant in India.

**Conclusion.** Inflammation contributes to some cases of depression, bipolar disorder and schizophrenia. NSAIDs and COX-2 inhibitors, minocycline, statins, omega-3 and NAC show modest adjunctive benefit, mainly in inflamed subgroups. Maudsley rates the evidence in schizophrenia as very weak, with key minocycline trials (including BeneMin) negative. The future lies in biomarker-guided, precision use rather than routine prescription.

## In depth

### Critical appraisal (from the sources)
- **Strength of evidence:** positive meta-analyses coexist with negative large RCTs, notably for minocycline in depression and in schizophrenia negative symptoms [2, pp35, 353].
- **Trial quality:** small samples and short follow-up dominate [1, 2].
- **Biomarker selection:** is rarely used in trials, yet it is the proposed key to response [1].
- **Safety:** NSAIDs carry gastrointestinal and cardiovascular risks and may interact with antidepressant response [1].

### Practical clinical stance (from the sources)
- **Consider inflammatory status** when assessing patients, especially with mood disorders, trauma or biological stressors, where facilities allow [1].
- **Not routine:** these agents are adjuncts in specialist or refractory settings, not routine practice [2].
