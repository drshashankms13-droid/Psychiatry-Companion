## Exam answer

### Definition

Precision psychiatry uses individual characteristics–-symptoms, course, genetics, biomarkers, environment, behaviour, and treatment history–-to improve prevention, diagnosis, treatment selection, and outcome prediction. It does not mean that every patient can currently receive a biologically exact diagnosis or treatment.

### Potential

- **Treatment selection:** Clinical profiles and prior response can guide medication and psychotherapy choices.
- **Pharmacogenomics:** CYP2D6 and CYP2C19 variants affect metabolism of some psychotropics. For selected drug–gene pairs, guidelines may inform dose or alternative selection. Genotype is only one factor; interactions, age, liver function, adherence, and response matter.
- **Longitudinal/digital phenotyping:** Repeated symptom measures, sleep/activity, and digital data may identify within-person change and support early intervention.
- **Biomarkers:** Imaging, electrophysiology, inflammatory markers, and cognition may clarify mechanisms or subgroups in research.
- **Risk stratification:** Integrated clinical/contextual data may inform follow-up intensity and relapse prevention.

### Limitations

Most psychiatric diagnoses lack validated biomarkers that reliably determine individual treatment. Candidate markers may have limited replication, small effects, or poor external validity. Disorders are heterogeneous and polygenic. Machine-learning models may overfit and fail in populations unlike training samples. Prediction is not equivalent to improved outcomes.

### India/low-resource feasibility

High costs of genetic tests, imaging, and digital infrastructure may widen inequality. Many datasets underrepresent Indian linguistic, regional, rural, and socioeconomic diversity. Imported algorithms may be poorly calibrated. Specialist shortages, fragmented records, variable laboratory quality, digital exclusion, and connectivity barriers are practical obstacles. Innovation must not divert resources from essential medication, primary-care teams, psychotherapy, and crisis services.

### Ethics

Genetic and behavioural data raise privacy, consent, data ownership, discrimination, and familial concerns. Digital monitoring can become surveillance. Algorithms may encode bias or be opaque. Clinicians remain accountable; automated scores should not replace judgment or shared decisions.

### Implementation strategy

1. Start with low-cost precision: careful
diagnosis, longitudinal history, measurement-based care, adverse-effect
monitoring, and shared decision-making.
2. Use pharmacogenomic testing
selectively for evidence-supported decisions, not universal panels.
3. Validate tools prospectively in diverse Indian populations; assess
clinical utility, cost-effectiveness, and equity.
4. Build secure,
interoperable data systems and transparent governance.
5. Use
task-sharing and tele-mental health with referral and safety pathways.
6. Evaluate patient-centred outcomes rather than technological novelty.

### Conclusion

Precision psychiatry has potential, especially through better clinical measurement and selected pharmacogenomic applications, but is constrained by weak biomarkers, heterogeneity, cost, bias, and privacy. In low-resource settings, innovation must accompany stronger accessible basic mental-health care.
