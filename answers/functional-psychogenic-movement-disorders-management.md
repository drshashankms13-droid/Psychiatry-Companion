## Exam answer

### Definition and terminology [1]
- **Definition:** a functional movement disorder (FMD) is a subtype of functional neurological symptom disorder (conversion disorder). Its main manifestation is **tremor, dystonia, gait disorder or other abnormal movements**.
- **Nature of symptoms:** they are **inconsistent and incompatible with known neurological disease, but genuine**, and cause distress or impairment.
- **Terminology:** "psychogenic", "hysterical" and "non-organic" are outdated. ICD-11 uses "dissociative neurological symptom disorder".

### Epidemiology [1]
- **Clinical burden:** 2–20% of movement-disorder clinic patients. 37% of patients with fixed dystonia and contractures met criteria for functional dystonia.
- **Demographics:** mean age 40; 60–90% female (myoclonus and parkinsonism show equal sex ratios).
- **Phenotypes** (about 5,000 cases):
  - mixed 23%;
  - tremor 22%;
  - weakness 18%;
  - dystonia 12%;
  - gait 8%;
  - jerks 5%;
  - parkinsonism 2%.

### Aetiology [1]
- **Biopsychosocial model:**
  - **predisposing:** female sex, younger age, personality, neurological or psychiatric comorbidity, ASD, hypermobility, adversity (emotional neglect more than abuse);
  - **precipitating** (50–80%): injury, surgery, anaesthesia, stress, other neurological events;
  - **perpetuating:** pain, fatigue, deconditioning, ongoing stress, untreated psychiatric illness, **alternative illness beliefs**, benefits and litigation.
- **Mechanism:**
  - abnormal self-directed attention and expectations override sensory feedback (predictive coding);
  - this produces movements experienced as **involuntary** through loss of the **sense of agency** (right temporoparietal junction).

### Clinical features [1]

**Clinical characteristics:**
- abrupt onset;
- a precipitating injury or illness;
- fast progression to maximum disability;
- incongruence with known disease;
- variability over time and context;
- waxing and waning ("good and bad days");
- **normalisation with distraction**; **worsening with attention**;
- entrainment;
- comorbid pain, fatigue and cognitive symptoms;
- disability out of proportion to signs.

**Main syndromes:**
- **Tremor:** distractible, variable, entrainable; whack-a-mole sign.
- **Dystonia:** fixed ankle or hand postures; cranial lip-pulling; paroxysmal mixed dystonia.
- **Gait:** dragging, knee-buckling, "walking on ice", astasia-abasia.
- **Myoclonus:** axial jerks.
- **Parkinsonism:** slowness without decrement.
- **Functional tic-like behaviours.**

**Diagnosis:** a **rule-in diagnosis** based on positive signs. It is best made by clinicians with movement-disorder expertise. Electrophysiology helps for tremor and myoclonus.

### Management [1]

**Overall approach:** individualised and **stepped**, by severity and complexity. There is "no one size fits all".

| Step | Who | Intervention |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **1** | All patients | **Diagnosis, education and advice** from the neurologist |
| **2** | More severe, without complex psychiatric comorbidity | **Brief rehabilitation**: physio-, occupational or speech therapy ± psychotherapy |
| **3** | Complex, highly disabled, comorbid, or failed earlier treatment | **Specialised multidisciplinary therapy** (outpatient, day hospital or inpatient) |

**1. Delivering the diagnosis (first-line treatment):**
- **Explain and show:** explain the rationale, **show patients their positive signs**, and convey that recovery is possible.
- **Validate:** it is **not "made up", "in the head" or "put on"**; FMD is real and common. Take complaints seriously and acknowledge previous negative healthcare experiences.
- **Diagnosis as treatment:** some patients improve with a well-delivered diagnosis alone.

**2. Education:**
- **Information:** reliable sources such as the *FND Guide* website.
- **Demonstration:** e.g., the tremor stops while tapping with the other hand, which shows the nervous system can work normally.
- **Bedside self-entrainment** for unilateral hand tremor: the other hand taps at the tremor frequency and then slows until both stop.
- **Analogies:** a "**software, not hardware**" problem; abnormal learned motor programmes.

**3. Role of psychiatry:**
- **What psychiatry offers:** diagnostic elaboration and treatment.
- **Explaining the referral:** frame it as "to understand your brain better", not to confirm the diagnosis.
- **Essential when** there is untreated anxiety or depression, PTSD, personality traits, dissociation, psychosis, trauma, or psychosocial perpetuating factors.
- **Joint working:** a neurology–psychiatry partnership with a shared framework reduces stigma and **counters dualism**.

**4. Motor retraining (specialised physiotherapy, OT, speech therapy)** for those not improved by education:
- **Check barriers first:**
  - severe pain or fatigue;
  - diagnostic disagreement or alternative illness beliefs;
  - ongoing stress.
- **Predictors of response:** **diagnostic agreement, readiness for change and perceived control** predict response better than duration or severity.
- **Principles** (different from standard neurorehabilitation):
  - delivered in a psychologically informed framework, with relaxation and mindfulness;
  - focus on **function** (transfers, walking) rather than impairment;
  - **divert attention** from abnormal movement; notice variability;
  - pacing;
  - limit hands-on treatment; **facilitate rather than support**;
  - early weight-bearing;
  - goal-directed automatic movement;
  - do not reinforce maladaptive postures;
  - **avoid aids and adaptive equipment**.
- **Evidence:**
  - **inpatient RCT** (n = 60, gait disorder): 3 weeks of physiotherapy beat waiting list, with a large effect sustained at 1 year;
  - **outpatient RCT** (n = 355; specialist physio 9 sessions over 3 weeks vs community physio): both improved physical function at 12 months with **no significant difference**. Specialist physio was better on secondary outcomes (motor function, mental health, satisfaction).

**5. Psychological therapies:**
- **Tailoring:** individualised to comorbidity, ideally by a psychiatrist or psychologist familiar with FMD.
- **Timing:** given **with or after** motor retraining.
- **CBT** targets unhelpful thoughts and anxiety. In functional tremor, CBT alone led to complete remission in **73% at 12 weeks** (n = 15). Telehealth CBT is promising.
- **Psychodynamic psychotherapy** has a good evidence base in FND generally. Studies in FMD are more limited, but individual psychotherapy may have a role.
- **When patients decline:** offer therapy as part of medical care, validate symptoms, and use integrated neurology–psychiatry models.
- **Pharmacotherapy** is for comorbid depression or anxiety only.

**6. Multidisciplinary programmes:**
- **Who:** complex or disabled patients.
- **Inpatient evidence:** a 4-week programme (PT, OT, CBT, neuropsychiatry, n = 66) gave large improvements. 66% rated their health "better" at discharge and at 1 year.
- **Specialised FND clinics:** offer pain programmes, long-term or trauma psychotherapy, and vestibular therapy.
- **Other investigated therapies:** **TMS**, hypnosis.

**7. Chronic or refractory FMD:**
- **The problem:** many patients become chronic. Absence of a structural lesion does not mean spontaneous resolution.
- **"Opt-in" therapy:** treating a patient not ready to engage usually fails.
- **When treatment fails:** consider **misdiagnosis and missed comorbidity**.
- **Ongoing care:**
  - periodic reassessment of readiness;
  - reassurance about new symptoms;
  - **prevent iatrogenic harm** (tests, surgery);
  - conservative primary-care management, as in somatisation.

**8. Avoid clinician-perpetuated harm** [2]:
- give a clear, positive diagnosis;
- no excessive investigation;
- one clinician takes primary responsibility;
- avoid opioids, unnecessary surgery, and crutches or wheelchairs that hinder rehabilitation;
- do not misattribute symptoms to incidental findings.

### Culture-sensitive psychotherapy in India [3]
NIMHANS case series: two young perinatal women with dissociative paraparesis (and mutism in one):
- **Alliance first:** a long initial phase built the therapeutic alliance and a narrative history using a "**photo album**" approach. The core conflicts emerged in the middle phase.
- **Abreaction:** uncovering the conflict and abreaction led to collaborative mind–body formulations. Both patients walked soon after the formulations were shared.
- **Skills training (CBT-based):** emotion regulation, communication, problem-solving and **assertiveness** reduce the need to "escape" into symptoms.
- **"Face-saving" physiotherapy** (and speech therapy) let patients shed symptoms without being shamed for "faking".
- **Family psychoeducation** produced systemic change. Termination sessions prepared patients for their return to the same milieu.
- **Lessons:**
  - avoid premature mind–body explanations;
  - therapist reflection and supervision matter;
  - attend to **gender, patriarchy and powerlessness**.
- **Wider evidence:** no well-established intervention exists for dissociation. Only modest RCT evidence supports behaviour therapy and MI-preceded psychotherapy, and relapse is common (Ganslev 2020, cited in [3]).

### Prognosis [1]
- **Outcome:** 40% the same or worse at 7.4 years; **20% complete remission**. Dystonia is worst (about 80% unchanged or worse); tremor 44–90% unchanged or worse.
- **Better prognosis:** early diagnosis, short duration, good response to initial treatment, good therapeutic alliance.
- **Worse prognosis:** multiple symptoms, long duration, personality disorder, belief in irreversible disease, financial benefits.
- **Social impact:** unemployment 23–84%; 35% not working because of ill health (multicentre study).

**Conclusion.** Functional ("psychogenic") movement disorders are common, genuine and disabling. They are diagnosed positively and treated in steps:
1. a clear, validating diagnosis with demonstration of positive signs;
2. FMD-specific physiotherapy;
3. CBT or psychodynamic therapy, plus treatment of comorbidity;
4. multidisciplinary programmes for complex cases.

Throughout, avoid iatrogenic harm. In India, culture-sensitive psychotherapy with family work and "face-saving" physical therapy is especially relevant.

## In depth

### Key trials and data quoted in the source [1]

| Study | Design | Result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Inpatient physiotherapy for psychogenic gait | RCT, n = 60 | Large improvement vs waiting list; sustained at 1 year |
| Specialist vs community physiotherapy (functional motor disorder) | RCT, n = 355 | No difference in primary outcome at 12 months; secondary outcomes favour specialist |
| CBT for functional tremor | Cohort, n = 15 | 73% complete remission at 12 weeks |
| Inpatient multidisciplinary programme | Prospective observational, n = 66 | Large improvement; 66% better at 1 year |
| Prognosis review | 24 studies, n = 2,069 | 40% same or worse; 20% remission |

### Lishman (2009): the older evidence base [4]
- **Frequency:**
  - about one-third of general neurology patients have symptoms at best partly explained by disease;
  - **fewer than 5%** later prove to have a missed organic diagnosis;
  - about **5%** of movement-disorder clinic patients have PMD.
- **Two diagnostic judgements:**
  1. **Unconscious vs conscious** symptom production. Unconscious = conversion (somatoform) disorder.
  2. If feigned: **factitious disorder** (sick-role motive) vs **malingering** (external gain; not a medical diagnosis).
  - Both are better seen as **dimensions**.
- **Fahn–Williams (1988) diagnostic certainty:**

  | Level | Criteria |
  | --- | --- |
  | **Documented** | Persistent relief with psychotherapy, suggestion or placebo; or absent when the patient believes they are unobserved |
  | **Clinically established** | Incongruent or inconsistent **plus** other psychogenic signs, multiple somatisations or obvious psychiatric disturbance |
  | **Probable** | Incongruent or inconsistent, **or** psychogenic signs or multiple somatisations |
  | **Possible** | Evidence of emotional disturbance |

  The first two were later merged as "clinically definite".

- **Profile:**
  - two-thirds female; early 40s;
  - **tremor about 40%**, dystonia about one-third; gait, myoclonus and parkinsonism less common.
- **History clues:**
  - abrupt onset, rapid progression to maximum severity;
  - migrating, variable movements;
  - past medically unexplained symptoms;
  - onset after **minor physical injury** (more often than after psychological trauma).
- **Examination (Stone 2005):**
  - **tremor:** variability, distraction, **entrainment** (a 3 Hz rhythm may be more sensitive), **co-activation** (loading increases a functional tremor);
  - **dystonia:** distraction;
  - **myoclonus:** variable and long (>75 ms) stimulus–jerk latency.
  - **A negative test does not exclude PMD.**
- **Caveats:**
  - placebo or psychotherapy response is not foolproof: cervical dystonia remits transiently in up to 25%;
  - contractures can make psychogenic fixed dystonia persist in sleep or under anaesthesia;
  - a psychiatric history does not prove PMD: organic disorders such as Huntington's have high psychiatric comorbidity.
- **Fixed dystonia (Schrag 2004):**
  - **68%** began after peripheral injury; 44% had CRPS features;
  - 37% clinically definite PMD; 29% somatisation disorder; only 10% with no suggestion of a psychogenic disorder.
- **Investigations:**
  - **DAT-SPECT** (abnormal in over 90% of early PD) for psychogenic parkinsonism;
  - **EMG** (frequency variability, co-activation, entrainment, myoclonus latency);
  - **EEG back-averaging**: a **Bereitschaftspotential** before jerks supports psychogenic myoclonus.
- **Comorbidity (Feinstein 2001, n = 42):**
  - lifetime major depression 43%; anxiety disorders 38%;
  - **personality disorder 45%**; other unexplained symptoms 38%.
- **Mechanism (tremor):** voluntary-like activity, or **co-contraction producing clonus**.
- **Prognosis:**
  - remission in 35% over 6 years (Factor);
  - an intensive programme (psychotherapy, family sessions, hypnosis, placebo, antidepressants in 71%) gave **52% significant benefit and 25% complete resolution** (Williams 1995);
  - but Feinstein found only **4 of 42 remitted** at 3.2 years, with over half the same or worse.
  - **Poor prognostic factors:** long duration, gradual onset, psychiatric comorbidity.
  - Functional motor symptoms are often **replaced or joined by new ones** (83% had significant somatic symptoms at 12.5 years).
- **Management (2009):**
  - no controlled trials then;
  - **careful explanation of the diagnosis is paramount**;
  - identify **maintaining factors**;
  - **antidepressants may help even without depression**;
  - CBT or other psychotherapy;
  - multidisciplinary psychotherapy plus physiotherapy.

### Critical appraisal (from the sources)
- **Weak prognostic data:** most prognostic studies are in untreated cohorts and lack neurological controls [1].
- **Narrow physiotherapy evidence:** the largest RCT showed no primary-outcome advantage for specialist physiotherapy [1].
- **Psychotherapy evidence:** for dissociative disorders it is sparse, mainly case series and few RCTs [3].
