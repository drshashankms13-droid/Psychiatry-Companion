## Exam answer

**Introduction.** The statement "complete abstinence should be the goal in the treatment of substance use disorder" reflects the traditional **disease, moral-reform and Twelve-Step** view. In that view recovery means **complete, sustained cessation**, with:
- detoxification followed by rehabilitation;
- abstinence-contingent therapies;
- AA/NA;
- programmes that make housing or care conditional on sobriety [1].

The opposing **harm-reduction** philosophy is pragmatic and public-health oriented. It aims to reduce **death, disease and social harm whether or not use stops**, through:
- opioid agonist therapy (OAT);
- needle–syringe programmes (NSP);
- naloxone;
- supervised consumption;
- low-threshold access [1].

My position: **abstinence is the best outcome for many people and should always be available, but making it *the* goal — and especially a *precondition* for care — is not evidence-based.** The goal should be **individualised, staged and substance-specific**.

### Arguments supporting abstinence as the goal
1. **Clinical logic.** Abstinence restores physiology, reverses tolerance, repairs relationships and removes relapse triggers. Success is easy to measure [1].
2. **Strong evidence for abstinence-oriented care.** CBT, relapse prevention, **contingency management (abstinence-contingent vouchers)**, residential rehabilitation and **mutual help** produce sustained abstinence in motivated patients with social supports. They are the **best-evidenced treatments for stimulants** (no effective pharmacotherapy) [1].
3. **Severity and specific disorders.**
   - **Severe alcohol dependence with liver disease**, Wernicke–Korsakoff syndrome, pancreatitis, pregnancy or psychosis: "controlled drinking" is unsafe.
   - **Alcohol:** abstinence predicts the best long-term mortality and functioning.
   - **Tobacco:** complete cessation is the only safe level for cardiovascular risk.
4. **Loss of control.** In the disease model, dependence means a person cannot reliably moderate. Some outcome studies (Vaillant's 50-year follow-up) show stable moderation is rare in severe alcoholics [general knowledge].
5. **Legal context.** For illicit drugs (NDPS Act), continued use carries legal risk. Abstinence aligns treatment with the law.
6. **Patient and family preference** in India often favours "complete cure" (*nasha mukti*).

### Arguments against abstinence as the *only* goal
1. **Chronic relapsing nature.** Relapse rates after treatment are **40–60%** in the first year, comparable to hypertension or asthma [general knowledge; McLellan 2000 *JAMA*]. Measuring success only by continuous abstinence labels most patients as failures.
2. **Abstinence as a precondition excludes the highest-risk people:**
   - the homeless;
   - people in the justice system;
   - people with dual diagnosis;
   - people who inject drugs.

   This drives them away from services [1].
3. **Iatrogenic harm.** **Detoxification without maintenance raises post-treatment overdose mortality**, because tolerance is lost while exposure to the illicit supply continues [1].
   - **OAT halves all-cause mortality and cuts overdose mortality** while patients are in treatment (Sordo 2017 *BMJ*: mortality 2–3× higher out of treatment; the first 4 weeks after leaving are highest-risk) [general knowledge].
4. **Harm reduction works on hard outcomes** [1]:
   - OAT: ↓ mortality, ↑ retention, ↓ illicit opioid use, ↓ HIV/HCV, ↓ crime;
   - NSP: ↓ blood-borne virus transmission;
   - naloxone: prevents fatal overdoses;
   - supervised consumption sites: prevent overdose deaths and engage people in treatment.
5. **Reduced drinking is a valid endpoint.**
   - EMA (2010) and FDA guidance accept **reduction in heavy drinking days** as a trial endpoint [general knowledge].
   - A **WHO drinking risk level reduction** of 1–2 levels improves health outcomes (Witkiewitz 2017).
   - **Nalmefene** (as-needed, EU-approved) is a reduction-goal drug.
   - **COMBINE (2006, n = 1,383)** used **percent days abstinent and time to first heavy drinking day** as co-primary outcomes [2]:
     - naltrexone + medical management, CBI + MM, or both: PDA about 77–81% vs 75% with placebo + MM;
     - **naltrexone reduced heavy drinking (HR 0.72)**;
     - **acamprosate had no effect**;
     - **placebo + medical management alone helped**, showing that much of the benefit is in contact and monitoring.
6. **Motivation.** Many people won't accept an abstinence goal at first. Offering **reduction** (goal choice) improves engagement. MI respects autonomy, and people who succeed at reducing often later choose abstinence.
7. **Ethics.** Harm reduction rests on **non-abandonment, autonomy, equity** and the duty to save lives [1]. Withholding OAT or NSP until abstinence is achieved is ethically hard to defend.
8. **Population impact.** Per-person benefit may be smaller, but much larger reach means more total harm averted.

### Critiques of harm reduction (to be balanced)
- **Moral hazard and normalisation:** possible reduced motivation to quit [1].
- **Opportunity costs:** funds diverted from rehabilitation and housing [1].
- **Implementation failures:** without referral pathways, harm reduction becomes a long-term substitute rather than a bridge [1].
- **Distal outcomes:** fewer RCTs show equal gains in employment, family reunification or sustained abstinence [1].
- **Diversion of OAT drugs** (buprenorphine misuse) if poorly supervised.

### Synthesis: what should the goal be?
| Situation | Appropriate goal |
| --- | --- |
| Severe alcohol dependence, organ damage, pregnancy, psychosis, previous failed moderation | **Abstinence** |
| Mild–moderate alcohol use disorder, unwilling to abstain | **Reduction** (WHO risk levels) as initial goal; naltrexone or nalmefene |
| Opioid dependence, injecting | **OAT (methadone/buprenorphine) maintenance** plus NSP and naloxone; abstinence later if desired |
| Stimulants | Abstinence-oriented CM and CBT, plus harm reduction (safer use, PrEP) |
| Tobacco | Cessation; **NRT/e-cigarettes as harm reduction** for people who cannot quit (contested) |
| Cannabis | Abstinence in psychosis and adolescence; reduction otherwise |

**Conclusion.** Abstinence and harm reduction are **not opposites but points on a continuum of care** [1]. Abstinence should remain a **desirable and available goal**, especially in severe disorder, but should **never be a precondition for life-saving care**. The goal must be **individualised** to the substance, severity, comorbidity and patient preference, and reviewed over time. Success should be measured by **mortality, morbidity, functioning and quality of life**, not abstinence alone. So the statement is **partly true but too absolute**.

## In depth

### Background
- **Historical models:**
  - **moral model:** use is a choice, so the response is punishment;
  - **disease model** (Jellinek, AA): loss of control, so the answer is lifelong abstinence;
  - **public health and harm-reduction model** (1980s HIV epidemic: Liverpool and Amsterdam NSPs; methadone, Dole & Nyswander 1965).
- **Sobell & Sobell (1970s)** controlled-drinking controversy.
- **Recovery model:** recovery as a process of improved wellbeing, not only abstinence (SAMHSA definition) [general knowledge].

### Epidemiology
- **India (2019 national survey):**
  - alcohol use 14.6% (about 16 crore people); 5.2% with harmful use/dependence (about 5.7 crore);
  - opioid use 2.1%; about 8.5 lakh people inject drugs [general knowledge; check].
- **Treatment gap above 70%** for alcohol and other drug use disorders (NMHS).

### Classification of treatment goals
1. **Abstinence** (continuous; time to first lapse).
2. **Reduction / controlled use** (percent heavy drinking days; WHO risk levels).
3. **Harm reduction** (safer use; no injecting; no sharing).
4. **Stabilisation / maintenance** (OAT).
5. **Recovery** (functioning, quality of life, citizenship).

### Mechanism: why abstinence fails for many
- **Neuroadaptation** (Koob's allostasis; Volkow's reward-deficit): persistent craving and negative affect.
- Cue-induced reinstatement.
- **Psychosocial determinants:** poverty, housing, trauma.
- Lapse-to-relapse escalation through the **abstinence violation effect** (Marlatt), which is worsened by a rigid "all-or-nothing" goal.

### Clinical features: assessing goal suitability
- Severity (AUDIT, SADQ; DSM-5 criterion count).
- Organ damage (LFT, GGT, ultrasound).
- Pregnancy; psychiatric comorbidity; cognitive impairment.
- Prior moderation attempts; social supports; patient preference.

### Management: integrated stepped model
1. **Engagement:** MI, goal negotiation.
2. **Harm reduction:** naloxone, NSP, safer use, vaccination (HBV), HIV/HCV testing.
3. **Pharmacotherapy:**
   - **alcohol:**
     - naltrexone (reduction or abstinence);
     - acamprosate (abstinence maintenance; not effective in COMBINE but effective in European trials);
     - disulfiram (abstinence, supervised);
     - baclofen, topiramate, gabapentin (off-label);
   - **opioids:** methadone, buprenorphine (OAT), naltrexone (abstinence, motivated patients);
   - **tobacco:** varenicline, NRT, bupropion.
4. **Psychosocial:** CBT, relapse prevention, CM, family therapy, mutual help (AA, NA, SMART Recovery).
5. **Social:** housing, employment (IPS), legal support.
6. **Continuity:** chronic-disease management, recovery check-ups.

### Evidence table
| Study | Finding |
| --- | --- |
| COMBINE 2006, *JAMA* (n = 1,383) | Naltrexone and/or CBI with medical management improved PDA; naltrexone ↓ heavy drinking (HR 0.72); acamprosate ineffective; placebo + MM effective [2] |
| Project MATCH 1997 (general knowledge) | CBT, MET and 12-step facilitation equally effective; 12-step facilitation gave more complete abstinence |
| Sordo 2017, *BMJ* (general knowledge) | OAT retention ↓ all-cause and overdose mortality; highest risk in the first 4 weeks after leaving |
| Mattick 2014, Cochrane (general knowledge) | Methadone and buprenorphine retain patients and suppress heroin use better than placebo or detox |
| Witkiewitz 2017 (general knowledge) | WHO risk-level reductions associated with better health |
| Kelly 2020, Cochrane (general knowledge) | AA/12-step facilitation increases continuous abstinence rates |

### Critical appraisal
- **Measurement bias:** abstinence is a binary, demanding outcome. Many trials now use reduction endpoints, which favour harm-reduction approaches.
- **Selection bias:** abstinence-based residential programmes enrol motivated patients [1].
- **Context:** results from high-income countries with housing and welfare may not transfer to India.
- **Integration:** in modern practice, abstinence and harm reduction are used together (e.g., OAT patients doing CBT for cocaine use). The dichotomy is partly false.

### Indian context
- **Policy:**
  - India's **NDPS Act** is prohibitionist, but the **National AIDS Control Programme (NACO)** has run **targeted interventions for people who inject drugs** since the 1990s: NSP, OST with buprenorphine and methadone (OST guidelines 2014);
  - **NDPS Section 71** allows treatment centres;
  - **MHCA 2017** includes substance use disorders as mental illness, giving access to care [general knowledge].
- **Services:**
  - many private "nasha mukti kendras" are unregulated, abstinence-only and sometimes coercive or abusive [Likely; media and NHRC reports];
  - government **Drug Treatment Clinics** (AIIMS-supported) provide buprenorphine and naloxone OAT in district hospitals.
- **Culture:** family preference for "complete stopping" and stigma toward maintenance therapy ("replacing one drug with another").

### Recent advances
- **Long-acting buprenorphine injections** (monthly) improve retention.
- **Naloxone nasal spray** in community programmes.
- **Contingency management** is scaling up (UK, US VA).
- **Recovery-oriented systems of care** and peer support.
- **Psychedelic-assisted therapy** for AUD (psilocybin; Bogenschutz 2022) is experimental.
