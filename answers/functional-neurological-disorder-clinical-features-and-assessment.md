## Exam answer

### Definition and terminology [1, 2]
- **What it is:** functional neurological symptom disorder (FND; DSM-5 "conversion disorder") presents with **genuine neurological symptoms** arising from **abnormal nervous-system functioning rather than structural disease**.
- **Positive evidence:** examination shows positive evidence of **incompatibility with recognised neurological disease**.
- **Old terms:** "psychogenic", "hysterical" and "non-organic" are out of favour [1].
- **ICD-11** uses "**dissociative neurological symptom disorder**"; older versions used "dissociative disorders" [1].
- **DSM-5 criteria (as summarised in [1]):**
  - altered voluntary motor or sensory function;
  - incompatibility with a recognised neurological or medical condition;
  - not better explained by another disorder;
  - significant distress or impairment.
- **Dissociation** is a partial or complete loss of the normal integration of memory, identity, immediate sensation and control of bodily movement [4].

### Epidemiology [1, 2]
- **Incidence:** 4–12 per 100,000 per year. **9–16% of neurology patients**, making FND one of the commonest neurological presentations [2].
- **Demographics:** mean onset about 40 years; about 70% female; lower socio-economic status [2].
- **Functional movement disorder (FMD):** 2–20% of movement-disorder clinic patients [1].
- **India:** motor dissociative disorders are more prevalent in developing countries. Up to 60% of Indian patients with dissociation are housewives with primary-school education and family stressors [4].

### Clinical manifestations

**Subtypes of FND** (most to least common) [1]: **functional seizures**, weakness and paralysis, abnormal movements, speech symptoms, globus, sensory symptoms, cognitive symptoms.

**1. Functional weakness** [2]:
- **History:** dropping things; dragging the leg or sudden buckling. Hemiparesis is most common; monoparesis or paraparesis also occur.
- **Depersonalisation:** the limb does not feel like "part of me".
- **Obvious inconsistency:** cannot plantar-flex the ankle on the couch but can stand on tiptoe; cannot move the arm on examination but uses it to take things from a bag.

**2. Functional movement disorders** [1]:
- **Frequency of phenotypes** (meta-analysis, about 5,000 cases):
  - mixed 23%;
  - **tremor 22%**;
  - weakness 18%;
  - **dystonia 12%**;
  - gait 8%;
  - myoclonus/jerks 5%;
  - parkinsonism 2%.
- **Mixed presentations:** a quarter to a half of patients have more than one phenotype.

| Phenotype | Characteristic features |
| --- | --- |
| **Tremor** (commonest) | Present at rest, posture and action. Fingers often spared, with movement mainly at the wrist. "Internal tremor". **Distractible, variable, entrainable**. Co-contraction; worse with weight load; "**whack-a-mole**" sign; pauses with a ballistic movement of the other arm |
| **Dystonia** (2nd) | **Fixed focal dystonia**: ankle plantar-flexion/inversion; finger flexion sparing the pincer grasp; marked resistance; pain. Positive signs: dramatic resolution with physiotherapy, placebo or low-dose botulinum toxin; **no overflow or sensory trick**; inconsistency (e.g., improves putting on socks). Often resistant to distraction. Most post-traumatic dystonia (including "CRPS dystonia") is probably functional. **Cranial**: lip pulled down and out to one side, jaw deviation, platysma overactivity, the "other Babinski sign". **Paroxysmal mixed dystonia** |
| **Gait** | Dragging monoplegic gait; knee-buckling; "walking on ice"; astasia-abasia; excessive slowness; uneconomic postures; flailing arms with preserved balance; **swivel-chair sign**. Variability distinguishes it from consistently bizarre dystonic gait |
| **Myoclonus/jerks** | Inconsistent, **axial or truncal**, distractible, spontaneous remissions; acute onset and offset. 57% of "propriospinal myoclonus" is functional |
| **Parkinsonism** | Variable tremor; **slowness without decrement**; paratonia (gegenhalten) rather than rigidity, no cogwheeling; arm-flailing without falling. About one-third precede true PD, so follow up |
| **Tics** | Rare (about 2% of FMD); hard to diagnose because primary tics share sudden onset, suggestibility and fluctuation. **Functional tic-like behaviours** ("TikTok tics", COVID era), mostly in females aged 15–25. ESSTS criteria: onset at 12 or older, rapid evolution, complex rather than simple tics, specific complex movements and words. Not stereotyped; no clear premonitory urge |

**3. Other functional symptoms** [2]:
- **Speech:** prosody problems, foreign-accent syndrome, mutism.
- **Globus:** a lump in the throat, worse *between* swallows.
- **Seizures:** dissociative or functional seizures. Remission occurs in 40% or fewer.

**Shared features of FMD** [1]:
- **Onset:** often **abrupt**: 54% within seconds to minutes, 36% within a day. Rapid progression to maximum disability.
- **Precipitants:** physical injury, surgery, anaesthesia, illness, stress; a panic attack at onset.
- **Course:** **"good days and bad days"**.
- **Associated symptoms:** **fatigue in more than 90%**; pain; cognitive symptoms. Non-motor symptoms cause more disability than motor ones.

### Aetiology: a biopsychosocial model [1, 2]

| Factor | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| **Predisposing** | Female sex; younger age; personality traits or disorders; comorbid neurological disease; psychiatric illness; ASD and joint hypermobility; adverse life events and maltreatment (**emotional neglect 49% vs 20%**, physical abuse 30% vs 12%, sexual abuse 24% vs 10%; OR 2.8 for stressful life events) [2]; TPH2 polymorphism interacting with childhood trauma [1] |
| **Precipitating** (50–80%) | Injury or accident; surgery or procedure; anaesthesia; stressful event; other neurological event (migraine, Bell's palsy) |
| **Perpetuating** | Pain, fatigue, deconditioning; ongoing stress; untreated anxiety, depression or PTSD; **alternative illness beliefs**; litigation and disability benefits; relief of life stress by illness. **Clinician factors** [2]: no clear positive diagnosis; over-investigation; specialist-hopping; opioids and wheelchairs; unnecessary surgery; misattribution to incidental findings |

**Mechanisms** [1, 2, 3, 4]:
- **Neurobiological (predictive coding)** [1]:
  - abnormal **self-directed attention**;
  - strong **prior expectations** override sensory input;
  - loss of **sense of agency** through a mismatch in the right temporoparietal junction "agency network";
  - abnormal limbic–motor interface;
  - imaging shows increased amygdala, striatal and thalamic volume and reduced sensorimotor cortex.
- **Cognitive-behavioural** [2]:
  - a trigger (e.g., migraine weakness) activates a mental representation of paralysis, which is amplified by anxiety and hypervigilance;
  - depersonalisation causes loss of ownership of movement;
  - low awareness of emotion means physical rather than emotional symptoms are reported.
- **Active inference** [2]: the brain's prediction that "the leg is absent" overrides sensory input, the reverse of phantom limb.
- **Psychodynamic** [2, 4]:
  - unconscious conflict is converted into a symbolic somatic symptom (Breuer & Freud);
  - later models emphasise problematic early relationships, with symptoms as a coping response and a way to meet dependency needs;
  - **"escape"** from intolerable circumstances: severe life events precede onset in 56%;
  - empirical support is limited.
- **Dissociation theories** [3]:
  - Janet: failure of integration under "vehement emotion";
  - Hilgard: neo-dissociation, with the executive control system disconnected;
  - structural dissociation;
  - cortico-limbic disconnection.
- **Socio-cultural** [4]:
  - commoner where family expectations are strong and powerlessness high (gender, patriarchy);
  - symptoms may be a culturally sanctioned expression of distress, for example loss of voice after "conflict over speaking out".

### Assessment of suspected motor FND [1, 2]

**Principle:** FND is a **rule-in diagnosis, not a diagnosis of exclusion**. It rests on characteristic history plus **positive signs**. Psychological factors are **common but not required** [1].

**History** [1]:
- all symptoms (neurological, constitutional, psychological);
- **circumstances at onset** (injury, illness, procedure);
- course: variability, triggers, remissions, previous treatments;
- disability (physical, social, occupational);
- **ideas, concerns, expectations**;
- psychosocial functioning;
- **dissociation**;
- family history;
- previous functional symptoms;
- **previous clinical experiences** (misdiagnosis, iatrogenic harm, mistrust);
- recent stressors;
- psychiatric symptoms.

A history-based algorithm correctly classified 91% of 874 hyperkinetic patients. It used:
- early age and abrupt onset;
- more than one movement disorder;
- diurnal fluctuation;
- waxing and waning course;
- pain or fatigue;
- psychiatric features;
- family history.

**Examination: positive signs (Table 4 of [1]):**

| Domain | Signs |
| --- | --- |
| **General** | **Variability**; **distractibility**; **enhancement with attention**; **motor inconsistency**; incongruity; suggestibility (e.g., tuning-fork test) |
| **Weakness** | **Hoover's sign** (hip-extension weakness resolves with contralateral hip flexion against resistance); **hip abductor sign**; finger abductor sign; **give-way/collapsing weakness**; **drift without pronation**; co-contraction. Hoover's, co-contraction, give-way and drift without pronation were the most reliable [2] |
| **Tremor** | Variability; **entrainment**; co-contraction; whack-a-mole |
| **Myoclonus** | Variable duration, distribution and latency; axial or facial predominance |
| **Gait** | Variability; monoplegic dragging; excessive slowness; "walking on ice"; **functional Romberg**; uneconomic postures; knee-buckling; **swivel-chair sign** |
| **Face** | Tonic lip or jaw pulling with platysma overactivity; blepharospasm that resists eyelid opening |
| **Fixed dystonia** | Digits 3–5 flexed with pincer preserved; foot inversion and plantar flexion; laterocollis with shoulder elevation |
| **Parkinsonism** | Hypokinesia without decrement; paratonia; "huffing and puffing" |
| **Tics** | Not stereotyped; interfere with speech or action; no premonitory urge; not suppressible |

**Performance of positive signs:** sensitivity 9–100%, but **specificity 64–100%** (37 of 41 signs above 90%) [1].

**Investigations** [1]:
- **When to investigate:** to exclude *comorbid* disease, not to make the diagnosis.
- **Electrophysiology** (for difficult tremor or myoclonus):
  - **tremor:** EMG/accelerometry shows entrainment, coherence (the same frequency in all limbs), co-activation, and increased amplitude with weight load;
  - **myoclonus:** **long latency of 100–120 ms** (vs 60–70 ms cortical), variable recruitment, habituation, and a **Bereitschaftspotential** on back-averaged EEG;
  - there is no definitive test for functional dystonia.
- **MRI** of brain or spine if a coexisting structural lesion is suspected. Prepare the patient for incidental findings.
- **DaTscan** is not routine and not specific (normal also in drug-induced and vascular parkinsonism).

**Misdiagnosis** is about **4%** since the 1970s, which is no higher than for other disorders. FND can herald later neurological disease, so reassess if symptoms change [1].

### Differentiating functional from primary neurological disorders [1, 2]

| Feature | Functional (conversion) | Primary neurological |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Onset | Abrupt, often after injury, illness or stress | Usually gradual (except stroke and similar) |
| Progression | Rapid to maximum disability | Usually gradual |
| Course | Fluctuating, "good and bad days", spontaneous remissions | Steady or progressive |
| **Distraction** | **Improves or abolishes** the movement | Often *worsens* the movement (e.g., PD dyskinesia) |
| **Attention** | Worsens it | Little effect |
| Consistency | **Internally inconsistent** (normal automatic movements) | Consistent, even when bizarre (e.g., dystonic gait) |
| Pattern | Incongruent with known anatomy or physiology | Fits recognised patterns |
| Tremor frequency | Same in all limbs; entrains | Slightly different oscillators; no entrainment |
| Bradykinesia | Slow **without decrement** | Decrement in speed and amplitude |
| Tone | Paratonia | Rigidity with cogwheeling |
| Weakness signs | Hoover's +, give-way, drift without pronation | Pronator drift; reflex and UMN signs |
| Myoclonus latency | 100–120 ms with Bereitschaftspotential | 60–70 ms (cortical), under 80 ms (brainstem) |
| PD prodrome | Absent (no anosmia, RBD, constipation) | Often present |
| Associated | Multiple functional symptoms, fatigue, pain, dissociation | — |

**Psychiatric differential diagnosis** [1]:
- somatic symptom disorder;
- depersonalisation/derealisation disorder;
- **factitious disorder and malingering** (feigned symptoms).

**Comorbidity** [1, 2]:
- **neurological:** comorbid movement disorders in 15–25%;
- **psychiatric:** depression, anxiety, trauma-related disorders, **cluster B traits**; psychoticism uncommon;
- **other functional syndromes:** chronic pain, IBS.

### Prognosis [1, 2]
- **Overall:** 40–66% persist or worsen. A 14-year study of functional weakness found 20% remitted, 31% improved, 23% persisted and 26% worsened [2].
- **FMD:** 40% the same or worse at 7.4 years; **only 20% remit fully**. **Dystonia is worst** (about 80% same or worse); tremor is also poor [1].
- **Sensory symptoms** do better than weakness, dystonia or tremor [1, 2].
- **Prognostic factors (Table 5 of [1]):**

| Positive | Negative |
| --- | --- |
| Onset in childhood or adolescence | Multiple physical symptoms |
| **Early diagnosis** | **Longer duration of symptoms** |
| Good response to initial treatment | Poor physical functioning |
| Comorbid anxiety or depression | Comorbid personality disorder |
| Subsequent change in marital status | **Belief that symptoms are irreversible and due to disease** |
| **Good therapeutic alliance** | Illness-related financial benefits |

- **Caveat:** most prognostic studies are of untreated cohorts, and evidence on litigation, age, IQ, education and marital status conflicts [1].
- **Social impact:** unemployment 23–84%; 24–55% on or seeking disability benefits [1].

**Conclusion.** FND is a common, genuine disorder of nervous-system function. It is diagnosed **positively**, by history and positive signs (Hoover's, distractibility, entrainment, variability, inconsistency), not by exclusion. It is understood through a biopsychosocial model of predisposing, precipitating and perpetuating factors, with altered attention, expectation and agency at its core. Psychiatric comorbidity is common but not required, and assessment includes the patient's illness beliefs, previous care and socio-cultural context.

## In depth

### Indian socio-cultural perspective [4]
- **Patient profile:** perinatal-onset dissociative paraparesis and mutism in young rural women from authoritarian, male-dominated households.
- **Meaning of symptoms:**
  - **powerlessness** (too many demands, no voice in decisions, unequal sexual partnership) expressed symbolically as loss of voice or limb power;
  - the symptoms provide care and exemption from duties.
- **Implications for assessment:**
  - core conflicts emerged only **in the middle phase** of therapy, not at intake;
  - patients may show **la belle indifference** or deny stress ("everything is good");
  - **premature mind–body explanations may be resisted**, so build the formulation collaboratively.

### Theoretical debates relevant to conversion [3]
- **Continuum vs taxon models** of dissociation (DES vs DES-T).
- **Socio-cognitive critique** (Spanos): suggestion and cultural scripts.
- **Modern classification:** HiTOP places dissociation in the thought-disorder spectrum. RDoC places it under arousal and self-representation systems.
