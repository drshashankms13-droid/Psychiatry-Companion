## Exam answer

**Definition.**
- Parent training programmes are **skills-based** interventions, given **individually or in groups**. They teach parents to **implement behavioural strategies effectively** with their child [1].
- It is an **indirect** intervention: the parent is taught to deliver the intervention to the child [4].
- **Parent management training (PMT)** is the form used for **oppositional and conduct problems** [2, 3].

**Principle to convey to parents.** Referral for parent training "**does not mean that their parenting is faulty**". It helps them learn strategies for "a particularly difficult and extraordinary situation" [1]. The expert consensus notes that the term "parent training" can feel **pejorative**, especially to mothers who already feel guilt or inadequacy [4].

### Indications
1. **ADHD [1, 5]:**
   - **Preschoolers:** there is "enough evidence for group and individual parent training". Medication is **not first line** under 6 years, so preschoolers must **first be offered psychosocial interventions** [1].
   - **School-age, mild–moderate ADHD:** offer parent training and school interventions after environmental modification. **Moderate ADHD:** "parent training where available must be offered first" [1].
   - **Severe ADHD:** medication, then psychosocial help for specific impairments [1].
   - **Maudsley:** drug treatment is indicated when impairment persists despite environmental modification, **parent training (if appropriate)**, parenting advice and school liaison [5].
2. **Oppositional defiant and conduct disorder [2, 3]:** PMT has the **strongest evidence** for CD/ODD (Patterson's Oregon Social Learning model; Webster-Stratton's Incredible Years) [3]. It works best for younger children and parents willing to engage [3].
3. **ASD with ADHD symptoms:** atomoxetine combined with parent training beat atomoxetine alone at 24 weeks [5].

### Format [1]
- **Individual:** 8–10 weekly sessions. **Group:** 10–20 sessions.
- **Indications for individual rather than group training:**
  - comorbid conduct problems or disorder;
  - family psychopathology;
  - intellectual disability in a parent;
  - language difficulties;
  - difficulty with engagement.
- **Involve other family members** if needed, especially **grandparents** in the same household. Training "may fail" if co-resident grandparents are not involved.

### Components [1]
1. **Psychoeducation:** symptoms are "neither the child's fault nor the parents' fault".
2. **Environmental modification:**
   - structured routines and physical environment;
   - time management, reminders and planners;
   - more supervision;
   - fewer distractions while studying;
   - highlighters;
   - short breaks every 15–20 minutes with physical activity;
   - extra time and smaller assignments.
3. **Effective communication:**
   - clear, short instructions, with long ones broken into steps;
   - rephrasing; focusing on positives;
   - **fewer critical comments**;
   - **consistency between what is said and done**: "no empty promises or warnings".
4. **Behavioural management:**
   - clear rules and **consistency**;
   - **immediate rewards**, then **delayed rewards** contingent on desirable behaviour;
   - **withdrawal of rewards or response cost** for undesirable behaviour, proportionate to it;
   - a **token economy** (home point system);
   - **time-out**.
   - Consistency must hold **across time, situations and adults** (both parents and grandparents).
5. **Anticipating and preparing** for unstructured situations.
6. **Self-care** for parents. Focus on the **child's and parents' strengths**; build healthy parent–child interaction; give regular positive feedback.
7. **Liaising with teachers** (teacher–parent note).
8. **Review:** seek feedback and solve implementation problems at every session.

### Steps of PMT for oppositional and conduct problems [2]
1. Promote a **child-centred approach**.
2. **Increase acceptable** child behaviour.
3. Set **clear expectations**.
4. **Reduce unacceptable** behaviour.
5. Strategies for **avoiding trouble**.
6. Identify and correct **long-standing maladaptive patterns**.
- **Before starting:** explore and address parental depression, psychosis, substance misuse and marital discord. **De-stress the family** first. Build on the family's strengths [2].

### Programmes and core skills [3]
- **Programmes:** Triple P, Incredible Years, Parent–Child Interaction Therapy (PCIT), Oregon Social Learning model.
- **Core skills:** positive attention, reward systems, consistent consequences, **planned ignoring**, monitoring, problem-solving.

### Barriers to success [1, 4]
- **Parental ADHD** (attention, organisation, impulsivity) or **depression** may block implementation. Parents may need their own assessment and treatment [1].
- **Parental expectations and beliefs:** some parents see the behaviour as naughtiness and may not accept parent training [1].
- **Mothers with ADHD** often have harsh, lax or negative parenting styles. Life-skills coaching and parenting support help, especially for young or single mothers [4].
- **Grandparents not involved** [1].

**Conclusion.** PMT is a structured, skills-based programme. It is **first line for preschool ADHD**, offered first in **moderate ADHD**, and has the strongest evidence in **oppositional and conduct disorders**. It combines psychoeducation, environmental structure, effective communication and consistent contingency management. It works best when parental psychopathology is treated, the whole household is involved, and parents are reassured that the referral is not a judgement on their parenting.

## In depth

### PGIMER parent-training manual for ADHD (Table 13) [1]
| Module | Content |
|---|---|
| Psychoeducation | Nature of ADHD; no blame |
| Parenting and communication | 3 Ps (patience, persistence, special play time); clear, specific, short instructions; consistency; "catch them good"; cut down criticism |
| Dealing with inattention | Structure and routines; structuring the day; organising the physical environment; visual cues and prompts; preparing for daily routines |
| Sustaining attention | Reduce off-task distraction, increase on-task stimulation; external motivators; immediate, powerful rewards; attention-enhancing tasks |
| Hyperactivity | Environmental modification |
| Home point (token) system | Setting rules; reinforcing desirable behaviour; response cost or penalty; time-out |
| Anticipation | Preparing in advance |
| School | Liaison; teacher–parent note |

### Two kinds of parenting intervention (Young et al.) [4]
1. **Parent/carer support:** meeting and sharing experiences with others.
2. **Parent/carer-mediated intervention ("parent training"):** the parent delivers the intervention.
- Both should include **psychoeducation** for better outcomes.
