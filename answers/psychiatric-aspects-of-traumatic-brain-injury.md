## Exam answer

### Introduction [1]
- **Mental sequelae** (cognitive incapacity, personality change) **far outstrip physical sequelae** as obstacles to rehabilitation, causes of unemployment and family strain.
- **Recovery:**
  - most gains occur in **year 1**, especially the first 6 months;
  - verbal IQ recovers by about 1 year, **performance IQ over about 3 years**;
  - **no firm prognosis before 2–3 years** except in the mildest cases;
  - social adaptation can improve for 10–15 years (Thomsen);
  - a **proportion deteriorate** later (32% vs 15% improved at 18 years: Millar), and deterioration is associated with **depression, anxiety, low self-esteem and alcohol**.
- **Late dementia risk:**
  - moderate injury HR 2.3, severe 4.5 (Plassman);
  - AD odds ratio about 2 in **men** (meta-analysis), possibly APOE ε4-related;
  - attributable risk small.
- **Mortality:** high late mortality from **accidents, alcohol and suicide**.

### Aetiology: the multifactorial model [1]
Brain damage and psychological factors are usually "inextricably combined". Clinicians must weigh each factor in the individual case.

| Factor | Key points |
| --- | --- |
| **Premorbid constitution and personality** | Lewis: chronic post-concussional states arise "in much the same person as develops a psychiatric syndrome anyway". Dencker twins: many "post-traumatic" symptoms are constitutional. Recent studies (Tate) find weaker effects after **severe** injury but stronger effects after **mild** injury (pre-injury psychiatric illness, neuroticism, low education). **Psychiatric illness itself raises the risk of head injury** (Fann) |
| **Age** | Older patients have longer PTA, more cognitive impairment, higher mortality and worse return to work (under 30% of over-50s vs over 70% of under-20s) |
| **Circumstances** | Fear, anger, resentment, blame ("**perception of fault**"), **cryptotrauma** (Pilowsky); violence-related injury |
| **Environment** | Family overprotection, role change, occupational fears, life events |
| **Compensation / litigation** | Contributes in **one-quarter to one-third** (Miller). Claimants' symptoms are about **0.5 SD worse** (Binder & Rohling). Abrupt cure on settlement is rarer than supposed. **Malingering** occurs but is hard to separate from conversion; forced-choice tests (below-chance scores) help. "**Good old days**" bias and "expectation as aetiology" |
| **Post-traumatic epilepsy** | **2–5%** of closed and **over 30%** of penetrating injuries. Raises psychiatric disability (Lishman 1968: especially first-year epilepsy). Switch phenytoin/phenobarbital to carbamazepine or valproate. Prophylaxis does not work |
| **Amount of brain damage** | Correlates with **cognitive impairment, apathy, euphoria, disinhibition, facile behaviour, poor judgement**, but **not** with anxiety, depression, irritability, headache, dizziness, fatigue or noise sensitivity. Overall it explains only about **one-fifteenth** of psychiatric disability (r about 0.25) |
| **Location** | **Left** hemisphere: intellectual disorder (left temporal especially). **Right:** affective and behavioural disorder and somatic complaints. **Frontal:** affective and behavioural disorder, **sexual disturbance**, crime. Overall, the amount of damage matters more than its site |
| **Alcohol and drugs** | About 40% of head injuries involve heavy drinkers. Alcohol worsens injury, atrophy, memory and return to work (40% vs 73%). Watch for **withdrawal** and **Wernicke–Korsakoff** (give thiamine). Drinking often falls after injury but can relapse with cues; about 12% develop new alcohol abuse by 30 years |

### Categories of chronic psychiatric sequelae [1]
**Frequencies:**
- Hillbom (wartime): cognitive impairment 2%, character change 18%, psychoses 8%, severe neuroses 11%.
- Ota (civilian): 3%, 6%, 5%, 22%.
- Structured interviews: major depression 14–61% and anxiety disorders 9–24% depending on the sample; **comorbidity** is common.

**1. Cognitive impairment**
- **PTA over 24 hours** makes full intellectual recovery unlikely.
- **Hallmarks:** **slowed information processing**, attention and concentration problems, **memory** impairment (source memory, intrusions, confabulation, prospective memory), **dysexecutive syndrome**.
- **Testing:** normal IQ can coexist with executive deficits, so test **BADS, WCST, Trails B, fluency, Stroop, PASAT**, and use ecological tests (**Multiple Errands Test**).
- **Language:** word-finding and social-communication problems (pure aphasias uncommon). **Verbal IQ is more than performance IQ.** **Insight is lost early** (affects capacity over finances).
- **Severe end:** post-traumatic dementia; **minimally conscious state**; **persistent vegetative state** (wakefulness without awareness). Differentiate PVS from **locked-in syndrome**.
- **Focal:** dysphasia or a **post-traumatic Korsakoff** syndrome can be mistaken for dementia.

**2. Personality change**
- The most distressing sequel for families; often unrecognised by the patient.
- **Mechanism:** the injury targets the **frontopolar, orbitofrontal and anterior temporal** cortex.
- **Lezak's categories:**
  - impaired social perceptiveness;
  - impaired self-regulation;
  - stimulus-bound behaviour;
  - emotional change (apathy, silliness, lability);
  - inability to profit from experience.
- **Frontal syndromes** (Blumer & Benson):
  - **convexity → "pseudodepressed"** (apathy, indifference);
  - **orbitofrontal → "pseudopsychopathic"** (puerility, euphoria, tactlessness, disinhibition, impulsive antisocial acts). **Anosmia** may flag orbitofrontal damage.
- **Other syndromes:**
  - Roberts' "**fronto-limbic dementia**": euphoria or disinhibition or anergia plus **irritability**;
  - Kretschmer's **basal syndrome**: apathy, mood swings, disturbed drives.
- **Formal personality disorders:** 23% at 30 years (avoidant, paranoid, schizoid). **Borderline:** 40% of borderline men report head injury.
- **Mechanism (cognition and personality as "two sides of one coin"):** impaired **social cognition and decision-making**; Damasio's **somatic marker** hypothesis.
- **Personality change without brain damage:** depression, anxiety, obsessionality, irritability, often **PTSD**; these are more accurately neurotic reactions.

**3. Aggression**
- **33.7% vs 11.5%** in trauma controls at 6 months; about a quarter at 6–60 months, fluctuating between individuals.
- **Predictors:** alcohol, youth, depression, **frontal injury**, pre-injury antisocial behaviour.
- **Organic aggression syndrome (Box 4.3):** reactive, non-reflective, non-purposeful, explosive, periodic, **ego-dystonic** ("episodic dyscontrol"); periamygdaloid involvement suspected.
- Often **pre-existing** aggression blamed on the injury. The family is most at risk (expressed emotion, jealousy).

**4. Psychoses**
- **Causation is uncertain:** direct brain effect, non-specific stress, **reverse causality** (prodromal or accident-prone patients), or coincidence. The longer the interval, the likelier coincidence.
- **Risk:** schizophrenia-like psychosis about **1.5–3×** (ECA OR 1.8; Davison & Bagley 2–3×). **Nielsen** (Danish register) found no excess; Harrison found non-schizophrenic psychosis OR 1.37. Achté: 2.6% of 3552 veterans.
- **Risk factors:** **family history of psychosis**, injury **before age 21**, drug misuse. Mean interval about 4–5 years. Fewer negative symptoms.
- **Phenomenology suggesting organic origin:** evolution from **post-traumatic delirium**, **delusional misidentification** (Capgras, reduplicative paramnesia), **Cotard** with derealisation, theft delusions in amnesic patients.
- **Mania:** 9% early (Jorge), mostly resolving by 6 months (overlaps with PTA agitation). Late mania is uncommon; bipolar OR 1.5 within 5 years. Linked to **orbitofrontal and temporal** damage and epilepsy; **irritability more than euphoria**; rapid cycling reported.

**5. Mood disorders**
- **Major depression:** **26% at 1 month** (left anterior lesions early; psychosocial factors later); 20–30% over year 1 (over 40% at some point); **14%** (ICD-10, Deb); 27–45% at 14–30 years.
- **Discriminating symptoms:** **loss of confidence, hopelessness, self-deprecation**. Fatigue, poor concentration and insomnia are non-specific.
- **Apathy** often co-occurs (60% both). **Hypopituitarism** may contribute.
- **Insight-depression link:** depression rises as **insight improves** ("sadder but wiser"). Depression **worsens cognition** (processing speed, working memory, executive function).
- **Pathological laughing/crying:** about **5%** (brainstem signs).

**6. Anxiety and related disorders**
- **Mixed anxiety–depression:** the **commonest** sequel (with irritability, phobic avoidance; fluctuating).
- **GAD** about 10–15% at 1 year; **panic** 9%.
- **Travel phobia** (20% at 1 year after RTA-related mild TBI, similar to non-head-injured RTA); agoraphobia; "**phobic imbalance**" (vestibular).
- **PTSD:**
  - about 3–14% after mild TBI; over a quarter in a severe-TBI rehabilitation unit;
  - **amnesia is partly protective**, but PTSD occurs via islands of memory and **implicit memory**;
  - less re-experiencing;
  - **do not diagnose by self-report** (40% vs 1 of 34 by interview).
- **Conversion:** fugues, Ganser states, amnesias. Head injury preceded hysteria in 21% (Whitlock).
- **OCD:** uncommon (about 2%); onset within days; aggression and obsessional slowness; more after severe injury.

**7. Suicide**
- About **1% over 15 years**; up to **14% of deaths**.
- **SMR about 4** (contusions/haemorrhage) and **about 2** (concussion); the risk is constant over 15 years.
- Attempts 8.1% vs 1.9%; **overdose** commonest; 48% repeat within a year.
- **Risks:** post-injury psychiatric disturbance **plus** substance abuse (**21×**); depression or hopelessness; relationship breakdown; isolation.

**8. Other**
- **Sexual dysfunction** in 30–60% (low drive, erectile problems; linked to depression and SSRIs).
- **Sexual disinhibition** (7% in outpatient rehabilitation).
- **Sleep:** insomnia 30%; daytime sleepiness.
- **Social isolation**; **family burden** (25–52% distressed; wives more depressed than those of paraplegics).

### Management [1]
**A. Assessment:**
- severity (PTA, imaging); full **neuropsychology including executive tests**;
- premorbid personality and psychiatric history (informant); alcohol and drugs;
- circumstances and attribution; litigation; family;
- epilepsy; hypopituitarism.

**B. Rehabilitation:**
- MDT; ICIDH framework; graded goals;
- compensatory cognitive rehabilitation (NeuroPage, errorless learning);
- **behaviour therapy** (ABC analysis, TOOTS, DRO, token economy);
- holistic inpatient programmes vs **home-based** programmes (Salazar);
- community outreach (Powell);
- **vocational rehabilitation (Box 4.5)**: job coach, supported placement;
- case management.
- (See the cognitive rehabilitation answer.)

**C. Pharmacotherapy (Box 4.6 principles):**
- no knee-jerk prescribing; **start low, go slow**, one drug at a time;
- avoid cocktails, interactions and **PRN short-acting anxiolytics**;
- **stop if ineffective**;
- prefer drugs with less **seizure-threshold lowering**, fewer **EPS and anticholinergic** effects, fewer interactions.

| Target | Recommendations |
| --- | --- |
| **Cognition** | **Methylphenidate** (speed, attention, memory: Whyte RCT); amantadine (early recovery); bromocriptine (executive function). **Cholinesterase inhibitors** (donepezil RCT positive; rivastigmine RCT negative overall): try only in significant memory impairment |
| **Agitation** (early) | **Avoid drugs if possible** (most worsen confusion; akathisia). Wait, or try amantadine or methylphenidate |
| **Aggression** | **Carbamazepine or valproate** first (mood benefit, epilepsy; valproate cognitively safe). Antipsychotics if psychosis, fear or mania (atypicals; risks of TD and NMS, may hinder recovery). Regular **diazepam** rather than PRN. **β-blockers** (best studied; high doses). **Trazodone**; sertraline for irritability |
| **Apathy** | **Bromocriptine** (improves motivation, not depression) |
| **Depression** | **SSRIs first** (sertraline, **citalopram**: few interactions; e.g., fluoxetine–carbamazepine interaction). Moclobemide, **trazodone** (anxiety, insomnia, aggression). **ECT** is not contraindicated. TBI depression may be harder to treat |
| **Emotional lability** | **SSRI** (citalopram as good as paroxetine) |
| **Anxiety, OCD, PTSD** | As for non-injured patients. **Trauma-focused CBT** for PTSD (adapted for cognition). **Early CBT for acute stress disorder** (PTSD 58% → 17%). Supportive psychotherapy, relaxation, **anger management** |
| **Mania** | **Valproate** preferred (**lithium** may worsen confusion, ataxia and seizure threshold) |
| **Psychosis** | Atypical (risperidone, olanzapine, quetiapine, aripiprazole). **Clozapine** if refractory (some add valproate for seizure cover). Confabulation responds poorly; consider a cholinesterase inhibitor or no drug |
| **Sexual** | Medication review; switch SSRI; **sildenafil**; sex and marital therapy. Disinhibition: behavioural methods; **medroxyprogesterone** in severe cases |
| **Sleep** | Sleep hygiene; zopiclone rather than benzodiazepines; trazodone; melatonin or low-dose amitriptyline; **modafinil** for daytime sleepiness |
| **Epilepsy** | Treat as usual; stop prophylaxis by 6 months unless indicated |

**D. Psychotherapy and social interventions:**
- support, reassurance, ventilation; CBT adapted for cognitive impairment;
- **speedy resolution of litigation** where brain damage does not play an identifiable part;
- family education and support (expressed emotion); social worker or case manager;
- supervision of finances when there is irresponsibility.

**E. Suicide prevention:**
- reduce environmental lethality; careful prescribing;
- treat depression and substance misuse;
- **monitor for at least 1 year after an attempt**.

**Conclusion.** Chronic psychiatric sequelae of TBI arise from an interplay of brain damage (strongest for cognition and frontal-type personality change), constitution, circumstances, environment, compensation, epilepsy and alcohol. The main syndromes are:
- cognitive impairment (slowing, attention, memory, dysexecutive syndrome);
- personality change (orbitofrontal "pseudopsychopathic" vs convexity "pseudodepressed") and aggression;
- psychoses (about a twofold risk; misidentification; family history);
- depression (about 25%), mixed anxiety–depression (the commonest), PTSD, OCD;
- a markedly raised suicide risk.

Management is multidisciplinary rehabilitation with cautious, principled pharmacotherapy: methylphenidate or cholinesterase inhibitors for cognition; carbamazepine/valproate, β-blockers or SSRIs for aggression; SSRIs for depression; valproate for mania; atypicals for psychosis; bromocriptine for apathy. Add psychotherapy, family support and vocational rehabilitation.
