## Exam answer

**Introduction.** OCD is usually **persistent**, with severity that fluctuates over time [1, p447]. SSRIs are first line, but response is **slower than in depression** (10–12 weeks) and often needs **higher doses** [1, p447].

In children, randomised trials show that up to **three-quarters** of medicated patients respond adequately. About **a quarter** fail an initial SSRI given for at least 12 weeks at the maximum tolerated dose together with adequate CBT/ERP [1, p590].

### Step 1 — Is it truly resistant?
Before calling it SSRI resistance, confirm the trial was adequate [1, pp447, 589–590]:
- **Dose:** increased towards the maximum tolerated or licensed dose. OCD doses are higher than depression doses, e.g. **fluoxetine 60 mg, paroxetine 40–60 mg**.
- **Duration:** **10–12 weeks** at an adequate dose before judging the response. NICE recommends two SSRI trials of **3 months** each.
- **Adherence:** confirm it. Fluoxetine's long half-life helps poorly adherent patients.
- **Psychological treatment:** an adequate trial of **CBT with exposure and response prevention (ERP)**.
- **Comorbidity:** check for missed conditions such as ASD, ADHD and tic disorders.
- **Insight:** poor-insight OCD is often misdiagnosed as delusional disorder or psychosis and treated with antipsychotics instead of SSRIs. Yet it **may respond to SSRIs as well as good-insight OCD**. Very poor insight may suit the inference-based approach better than standard CBT [3].
- **Panic attacks:** ICD-11's "with panic attacks" qualifier marks greater severity, poorer response and higher suicide risk [3].

### Step 2 — Maudsley Box 3.9 hierarchy [1, pp453–454]

**First line, in order of preference:**
1. **Any SSRI**, up to the maximum licensed dose. If not tolerated or poorly effective, **try an alternative SSRI**.
2. **Clomipramine**, up to 250 mg. Because it is less well tolerated, try at least one SSRI first.

**Second line** (unlicensed or weaker evidence; no order of preference):

| Strategy | Detail |
| --- | --- |
| **Add an antipsychotic to the SSRI** (low to moderate dose) | Most evidence for **aripiprazole or risperidone**; some for haloperidol. This is the usual second-line strategy [1, p447] |
| **Citalopram 40 mg + clomipramine 150 mg** | Small open RCT; recommended by NICE; **ECG monitoring** |
| **N-acetylcysteine**, up to 2,400 mg/day added to an SSRI or clomipramine | Two of five controlled studies negative, but the pooled effect shows benefit; GI side effects |
| **Lamotrigine** 100 mg+ added to an SSRI | Slow titration; may worsen OCD in some |
| **Topiramate**, up to 400 mg added to an SSRI | Poorly tolerated; may help compulsions but not obsessions; two trials negative |

**Experimental:**

| Strategy | Detail |
| --- | --- |
| **High-dose SSRIs** | e.g. escitalopram 25–50 mg, sertraline 250–400 mg; doses up to sertraline 650 mg and fluoxetine 120 mg have been used safely. ECG monitoring |
| **Memantine** 20 mg added to an SSRI | "Good evidence"; the most effective add-on in a 2023 meta-analysis |
| NSAIDs (celecoxib 400 mg) | Some evidence |
| Amantadine 200 mg | One positive RCT |
| SNRIs (venlafaxine up to 375 mg; duloxetine 60 mg) | — |
| Mirtazapine 30–60 mg | Small trial (n=30) |
| 5-HT3 antagonists: granisetron 1 mg + fluvoxamine; ondansetron 4 mg + fluoxetine | Ondansetron may be the more effective; QT risk |
| Pregabalin 75–225 mg + sertraline | One small positive RCT |
| Riluzole 50 mg bd | Variable results |
| Triptorelin (anti-androgen) | Open study of 6 men |
| Psilocybin | Emerging evidence |
| **IV clomipramine** | Faster onset; possibly more effective than oral |
| IV ketamine | Developing evidence |
| Once-weekly morphine | Small study (n=23); effects transient |

### Step 3 — Neuromodulation
- **rTMS (IPS guideline)** [2]:
  - **Moderately positive evidence**: 10–30 sessions over 1–12 weeks; pooled SMD for Y-BOCS **0.50–0.79**.
  - **Effective targets:** low-frequency **right DLPFC** (ranked most effective), high-frequency bilateral DLPFC, and low-frequency supplementary motor area.
  - **Time course:** effects appear by 2 weeks but are short-lived (about 4 weeks). Best results with 800–1,200 pulses per session.
  - **Not supported:** TBS appeared ineffective (insufficient data); deep TMS evidence is inconclusive.
  - **Better response:** younger age and bilateral SMA stimulation.
- **Deep brain stimulation** "could be effective" for resistant OCD [1].

### Duration and relapse [1, pp447, 591]
- **Duration:** continue for **at least 1 year**.
- **Relapse:** in those who continue for 2 years, relapse is **25–40%**, vs **80%** in those who stop after initial response.
- **Lower doses** may be effective for maintenance.
- **Stopping:** taper slowly over months. Anxiety-spectrum patients are especially sensitive to discontinuation symptoms.
- **Children:** continue for at least 6 months after remission.
- **Long-term SSRI:** people are increasingly counselled to consider staying on it to reduce relapse.
- **Relapse response:** see people quickly rather than putting them on a routine waiting list.

**Conclusion.** For SSRI-resistant OCD [1, 2]:
1. Confirm an adequate dose, duration, adherence and CBT/ERP trial, and re-assess comorbidity and insight.
2. Switch to a second SSRI.
3. Then use clomipramine, or augment with low-dose **aripiprazole or risperidone** (the best-supported second-line step).
4. Further options: memantine, NAC, lamotrigine, citalopram + clomipramine, high-dose SSRIs and IV clomipramine; **rTMS** (right DLPFC or SMA) has moderate evidence.
5. Maintain treatment for at least 1 year because relapse after stopping is high.

## In depth

### Background
- **Classification:** in ICD-11, OCD moved from "neurotic, stress-related and somatoform disorders" to the new OCRD grouping [3].
- **Comorbid diagnosis:** ICD-11 now allows OCD to be diagnosed alongside depression, schizophrenia or Tourette syndrome. These are common comorbidities and may need SSRIs or CBT [3].

### Epidemiology of non-response
- **Children:** about 25% fail the first SSRI + CBT. About **40% respond to a second SSRI** [1, p590].
- **Adults:** only **a third** of treatment-resistant adults respond meaningfully to antipsychotic augmentation [1, p590].
- **Not covered by the sources:** adult response-rate figures.

### Classification and criteria
- **Insight:** ICD-11 grades insight as "fair to good" or "poor to absent"; DSM-5 uses good/fair, poor, or absent/delusional [3].
- **Symptom subtypes:** dropped in ICD-11, because most patients have both obsessions and compulsions and subtype does not predict outcome [3].

### Mechanism in detail
- **Circuitry:** OCRDs share frontostriatal and reward circuitry [3].
- **Not covered by the sources:** the pharmacological rationale for each augmenting agent.

### Assessment in detail
- **Measures:** CY-BOCS (children) or Y-BOCS, at baseline and during monitoring [1, p588].
- **Reformulate:** re-examine comorbidities and psychosocial factors; support the family [1, p590].
- **ADHD:** treating it can greatly improve engagement with CBT. But stimulants may increase compulsions in patients who are not "fighting back" [1, p590].

### Management details
- **Antipsychotic augmentation in practice** (child data) [1, p590]:
  - consider after at least 3 months on each of two maximally tolerated SSRIs;
  - doses: **aripiprazole 2.5–5 mg** or **risperidone 0.5 mg**;
  - trial length: **6–8 weeks**; stop if no response;
  - **antipsychotics alone are not effective** for OCD;
  - patients with tic disorders may benefit more.
- **Fluvoxamine + clomipramine:** used in refractory cases. The benefit comes from fluvoxamine altering clomipramine metabolism. Specialist centres only, because of serotonin syndrome risk [1, p590].
- **Clomipramine:** NICE suggests two maximum-dose SSRI trials first, because of side effects and the need for cardiac monitoring [1, p588].
- **SSRI monitoring:** watch for akathisia, increased anxiety and suicidal ideation, especially under 30, with comorbid depression or at high risk [1, p447].

### Evidence table (as reported in the sources)

| Evidence | Finding |
| --- | --- |
| Continuation vs discontinuation [1] | Relapse 25–40% vs 80% |
| Antipsychotic augmentation [1] | About ⅓ of resistant adults respond; aripiprazole has more child data than risperidone |
| Memantine 2023 meta-analysis [1] | Most effective add-on |
| NAC (5 controlled studies) [1] | Pooled benefit; 2 of 5 negative |
| Second SSRI (children) [1] | About 40% respond |
| rTMS: Hyde 2022 (26 studies) [2] | SMD −0.66 |
| rTMS: Fitzsimmons 2022 network meta-analysis (21 studies) [2] | g = −0.50; LF-RDLPFC ranked highest |
| rTMS: Liang 2021 (22 studies) [2] | LF-RDLPFC most effective |

### Critical appraisal
- **Weak second-line evidence:** much of it is unlicensed or based on small or open trials [1].
- **Short-lived rTMS effects** (about 4 weeks); deep TMS evidence is inconclusive [2].
- **Underestimated relapse:** trials exclude comorbid cases, so relapse rates are probably underestimated [1, p591].
- **Insight:** poor insight predicts poor outcome but is not a reason to withhold SSRIs [3].

### Indian context [3]
- **Content varies, form doesn't:** OCD presents similarly worldwide. Symptom content reflects culture (e.g. religious obsessions; "Suchi-bai" washing in Bengali women), but form is universal.
- **Indian research:** there is abundant Indian literature on OCD, including data on insight.

### Recent advances
- **From the sources:** psilocybin, IV ketamine, memantine and 5-HT3 antagonists [1]; rTMS protocols targeting the right DLPFC and SMA [2].
- **Not covered by the sources:** further advances.
