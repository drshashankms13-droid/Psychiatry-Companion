## Exam answer

**Definition.** ICD-11 replaces the ten categorical ICD-10 personality disorders with a **dimensional model**: the clinician first decides whether a personality disorder is present, then rates its **severity (mild, moderate, severe)**, and optionally describes its **trait domains** and a **borderline pattern** [1, 2].

### Why ICD-10 had to change [1]

- **Underdiagnosis** relative to true prevalence in people with other mental disorders.
- Only two types (emotionally unstable–borderline and dissocial) were recorded with any frequency.
- **Extreme co-occurrence:** most people with severe disorder met criteria for several types.
- Frequent use of "unspecified" personality disorder.
- Categories lacked predictive validity for treatment.

### Step 1: general requirements [1, 2]

An enduring disturbance (typically for **2 years or more**) in:
- **Functioning of aspects of the self:** identity, self-worth, accuracy of self-view, self-direction; and/or
- **Interpersonal functioning:** understanding others' perspectives, developing and maintaining close relationships, managing conflict.

The disturbance is manifest in maladaptive patterns of cognition, emotional experience and expression, and behaviour, across a range of situations, is not explained by another disorder, substance or developmental stage, and causes distress or impairment.

### Step 2: severity

| Severity | Description |
| --- | --- |
| **Personality difficulty** (not a mental disorder; coded in "factors influencing health status") | Pronounced traits affecting treatment or services but below disorder threshold [1] |
| **Mild** | Some areas of self and interpersonal functioning affected; problems may be evident in some relationships or roles; usually no substantial harm to self or others |
| **Moderate** | Multiple areas affected; marked problems in most relationships and roles; sometimes harm to self or others |
| **Severe** | Severe disturbance in self and almost all relationships; often harm to self or others; inability or unwillingness to maintain employment or social roles |

Severity is judged by the degree and pervasiveness of self and interpersonal dysfunction, the pervasiveness and chronicity of emotional, cognitive and behavioural manifestations, and the associated distress or impairment [1].

### Step 3: trait domain qualifiers (as many as apply) [1, 2]

| Domain | Core tendency | Facets | Rough ICD-10/DSM equivalents |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Negative affectivity** | Experiencing a broad range of negative emotions | Anxiety, anger, worry, fear, vulnerability, shame, low self-esteem, mistrust | Anxious, dependent; part of borderline |
| **Detachment** | Maintaining social and emotional distance | Social withdrawal, emotional coldness, restricted affect | Schizoid, avoidant |
| **Dissociality** | Disregard for others' rights and feelings | Self-centredness, lack of empathy, entitlement, manipulation, aggression | Dissocial, narcissistic |
| **Disinhibition** | Acting impulsively without regard to consequences | Impulsivity, distractibility, irresponsibility, recklessness | Impulsive; histrionic; part of borderline |
| **Anankastia** | Narrow focus on control and rigid standards | Perfectionism, emotional and behavioural constraint, stubbornness | Anankastic (obsessive–compulsive) |

### Step 4: optional borderline pattern qualifier

Retained to preserve continuity of care and to identify patients who may respond to specific psychotherapies (e.g. DBT, MBT); its added value over the trait domains needs research [1]. Its descriptors closely follow the DSM-5 borderline criteria.

### Worked example

*"Moderate personality disorder, with prominent negative affectivity and disinhibition; borderline pattern."*

### Comparison with DSM-5

| Feature | DSM-5 Section II | DSM-5 AMPD (Section III) | ICD-11 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Model | 10 categories in 3 clusters | Level of personality functioning + 5 trait domains + 6 specific types | Severity + 5 traits + borderline pattern |
| Severity | Not graded | Level of personality functioning scale (0–4) | Personality difficulty, mild, moderate, severe |
| Trait domains | – | Negative affectivity, detachment, antagonism, disinhibition, **psychoticism** | Negative affectivity, detachment, dissociality, disinhibition, **anankastia** |
| Schizotypal | Personality disorder | Personality disorder type | Classified with **primary psychotic disorders** [1] |

### Strengths

- **Simplicity and utility** for non-specialists; severity guides treatment intensity and prognosis [1].
- **Eliminates artificial comorbidity** and the unspecified category [1].
- Better at **tracking change over time** [1].
- Consistent with evidence that personality pathology is dimensional and that general severity predicts outcome best.
- Personality difficulty allows recognition without a disorder label.

### Limitations

- Loss of familiar categories and decades of category-based research and therapy trials (e.g. narcissistic, schizotypal concepts).
- **Reliability of severity ratings** in routine practice needs further study.
- The borderline pattern is a political compromise and partly overlaps with trait domains.
- **No psychoticism domain** (schizotypal features moved to psychosis) [1, 3].
- Training needed; many clinicians still think in categories.

**Conclusion.** ICD-11 diagnoses personality disorder by asking "how severe?" first and "what style?" second. The model fits evidence on dimensionality and improves clinical utility, at the cost of continuity with the categorical tradition, which the optional borderline pattern partly restores.

## In depth

### Background

- ICD-9's personality classification drew heavily on **Kurt Schneider's** ten psychopathic personality types; ICD-10 (1992) moved closer to DSM-III/IV [3].
- Tyrer led the ICD-11 personality working group. Proposals for a purely severity-based system met resistance, leading to the addition of the borderline pattern qualifier late in development [1].
- Cloninger (2000) had proposed the same two-step logic: rate presence and severity of personality disorder, then describe traits [3].

### Severity assessment in detail

| Aspect | Mild | Moderate | Severe |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Self | Some instability of identity or self-worth | Marked problems with identity, self-worth, self-direction | Pervasive instability, unrealistic self-view, inability to set goals |
| Interpersonal | Some relationships affected | Most relationships affected; marked conflict | Almost all relationships severely disturbed |
| Emotion and behaviour | Limited | Pervasive in many situations | Extreme; frequent anger, impulsivity |
| Harm | Not usually | Sometimes | Often (self-harm, violence) |
| Roles | Maintained with some difficulty | Marked impairment | Inability to maintain work or social roles |

### Theoretical links

- K&S notes that the ICD-11 trait domains resemble temperament dimensions in Cloninger's psychobiological model ("the four As": **asthenic/anxious** ≈ high harm avoidance, **adventurous/antisocial** ≈ high novelty seeking, **asocial** ≈ low reward dependence, **anankastic** ≈ high persistence) more than the Five-Factor Model [3].
- DSM-5 Section III's psychoticism domain derives from an abnormal Five-Factor Model; ICD-11 omits it [3].

### Assessment tools

| Tool | Purpose |
| --- | --- |
| Standardised Assessment of Severity of Personality Disorder (SASPD) | Brief severity screen |
| Personality Inventory for ICD-11 (PiCD) | Trait domains |
| Personality Inventory for DSM-5 (PID-5) | Can be mapped to ICD-11 domains |
| Collateral history | Essential for pervasiveness |

### Treatment implications

| Severity | Typical approach |
| --- | --- |
| Personality difficulty | Awareness in clinical care; no specific treatment |
| Mild | Brief, structured psychosocial support; treat comorbidity |
| Moderate | Structured psychotherapy (DBT, MBT, schema therapy, CBT), case management |
| Severe | Intensive multidisciplinary care, crisis planning, possibly day-hospital programmes |

Traits help choose interventions: negative affectivity → emotion regulation; disinhibition → impulse control strategies; dissociality → offender-focused programmes; detachment → engagement and social skills; anankastia → flexibility-focused CBT.

### Critical appraisal

- **Validity:** general severity ("g-PD") predicts outcomes better than specific types in many studies, supporting the ICD-11 approach.
- **Clinical communication:** "moderate personality disorder with negative affectivity" may convey less to colleagues than "borderline PD".
- **Research continuity:** existing trials are type-based; mapping evidence onto severity and traits is ongoing.
- **Stigma:** a single severity label may be less stigmatising than descriptive type labels such as "dissocial".

### Indian context

- Personality disorders are under-diagnosed in Indian services; a simple severity rating may improve recognition by general psychiatrists and primary care.
- Cultural norms (e.g. deference, family interdependence) must be considered before judging interpersonal dysfunction.
- Records, insurance and legal systems still use ICD-10 codes (F60.x).

### Recent advances

- Validation of PiCD and SASPD in several countries.
- Evidence that the borderline pattern is largely captured by negative affectivity and disinhibition.
