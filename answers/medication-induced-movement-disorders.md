## Exam answer

**Definition.** Medication-induced movement disorders are abnormal movements or postures caused by drugs, mainly **dopamine receptor-blocking agents** (antipsychotics, metoclopramide, prochlorperazine) but also antidepressants, lithium, valproate, stimulants and others. DSM-5-TR lists them under "Medication-induced movement disorders and other adverse effects of medication". They are classified by **timing** as **acute** (dystonia, akathisia, parkinsonism, NMS) or **tardive** (TD, tardive dystonia, tardive akathisia), and by **phenomenology** as hypokinetic or hyperkinetic [1].

### Mechanism
Nigrostriatal D2 blockade (>78–80% occupancy) → relative cholinergic excess and indirect-pathway overactivity → **parkinsonism and acute dystonia**. Mesocortical/ventral striatal D2 and serotonergic–noradrenergic imbalance → **akathisia**. Chronic blockade → D2 supersensitivity and GABA dysfunction → **tardive syndromes**. Massive D2 blockade in hypothalamus and striatum + sympathetic dysregulation → **NMS**. Excess serotonin → **serotonin syndrome**. Lithium and valproate → **postural tremor** [1].

### The main syndromes

| Disorder | Onset | Features | Risk factors | Treatment |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Acute dystonia** (~10%) | Hours–days (minutes if IM/IV) | Oculogyric crisis, torticollis, trismus, tongue protrusion, opisthotonos, laryngospasm | Young males, drug-naïve, high-potency FGA | **Procyclidine 5–10 mg IM/IV** or **benztropine** 1–2 mg IM/IV; promethazine; then oral anticholinergic for some days; reduce or switch [1] |
| **Drug-induced parkinsonism** (~20%) | Days–weeks | Bradykinesia, rigidity, tremor (symmetrical), masked face, shuffling gait, sialorrhoea, bradyphrenia; mistaken for depression or negative symptoms | Elderly women, brain damage, high dose | Reduce dose; switch to low-EPS drug (quetiapine, clozapine, aripiprazole); anticholinergic short-term (review every 3 months; not at night) [1] |
| **Acute akathisia** (~25% with FGA) | Hours–weeks | Inner restlessness, compulsion to move, pacing, rocking, crossing legs; linked to agitation, suicidality, aggression, non-adherence | High dose, rapid escalation, polypharmacy; cariprazine, lurasidone, haloperidol (high); quetiapine, olanzapine, clozapine (low) | Reduce dose or switch; **propranolol 30–80 mg**; **mirtazapine 15 mg**; **vitamin B6**; trazodone, mianserin, cyproheptadine; clonazepam; anticholinergics only if part of generalised EPS [1] |
| **Tardive dyskinesia** (~5%/year exposure) | Months–years | Orofacial-lingual chorea, limb choreoathetosis | Elderly, female, mood disorder, early EPS, FGA (6.5%/year vs 2.6% with SGA) | Stop anticholinergic; switch to clozapine/quetiapine; VMAT2 inhibitors; B6, vitamin E, amantadine [1] |
| Tardive dystonia / akathisia | Months–years | Sustained postures (retrocollis); persistent restlessness | Young males | Botulinum toxin, clozapine, tetrabenazine, DBS |
| **NMS** (0.01–0.2%) | Usually first 2 weeks | Fever, **lead-pipe rigidity**, altered consciousness, autonomic instability; raised CK, leucocytosis | Dehydration, rapid escalation, IM depots, high-potency, lithium combination, catatonia | Stop antipsychotic; ICU supportive care; benzodiazepines; dantrolene; bromocriptine; ECT; cautious rechallenge after 2 weeks [1] |
| **Serotonin syndrome** | Hours | Clonus (inducible, ocular), hyperreflexia, agitation, hyperthermia, diaphoresis, diarrhoea | Serotonergic combinations (MAOI + SSRI, tramadol, linezolid) | Stop drugs; benzodiazepines; cyproheptadine 12 mg then 2 mg every 2 hours; cooling |
| **Postural tremor** | Any | Fine postural or action tremor | Lithium, valproate, SSRIs, TCAs, stimulants | Reduce dose; propranolol |
| Rabbit syndrome | Months | Rapid perioral tremor (5 Hz) | FGA | Anticholinergics |
| Antidepressant-induced movement disorders | Variable | Akathisia, bruxism, myoclonus, dystonia, restless legs (mirtazapine) | SSRIs | Reduce or switch |

### Assessment
- Medication history, including timing, dose changes, depots, metoclopramide and over-the-counter drugs.
- Examination using **Simpson–Angus Scale** (parkinsonism), **Barnes Akathisia Rating Scale**, **AIMS** (TD), ESRS. NMS: CK, WBC, renal function, myoglobinuria; exclude infection and other causes [1].
- Baseline examination before starting antipsychotics; repeat after dose changes and at 6–12-monthly reviews.

### Prevention principles
Lowest effective dose; slow titration; avoid polypharmacy and high-potency FGAs in young drug-naïve males (consider prophylactic anticholinergic only when the risk of dystonia is high and short-term); prefer drugs with a low EPS profile; avoid routine long-term anticholinergics (cognitive impairment, worsening TD, misuse); monitor with scales [1].

### Key evidence
- **Huhn 2019** network meta-analysis: EPS risk highest with haloperidol and lowest with clozapine, quetiapine, olanzapine, aripiprazole.
- **Akathisia dose–response meta-analysis (2023):** lowest risk with sertindole and quetiapine, highest with haloperidol and lurasidone; among partial agonists, cariprazine highest and brexpiprazole lowest [1].
- **Adjunctive akathisia treatment meta-analysis (2024):** mirtazapine, biperiden and vitamin B6 most efficacious; B6 best tolerated [1].
- **First-episode drug-naïve patients:** 1% dystonia, 8% parkinsonism, 11% akathisia even before treatment, so baseline assessment is essential [1].

**Conclusion.** Medication-induced movement disorders are common, under-recognised and stigmatising, and they reduce adherence. Recognise each syndrome by timing and phenomenology, measure with standard scales, and manage by dose reduction, switching to lower-liability drugs and targeted add-on treatments. Prevention through careful prescribing is the most effective strategy.

## In depth

### Background
- Delay and Deniker (1952) noted parkinsonism with chlorpromazine; the term "neuroleptic" originally meant "seizing the neuron". Haase proposed the "neuroleptic threshold" (micrographia).
- Acute dystonia was recognised in the 1950s; NMS was described by Delay in 1960; TD was named by Faurbye in 1964.
- Simpson–Angus (1970), AIMS (1976) and Barnes (1989) scales were developed.
- The SGA era reduced the burden of EPS [1].

### Epidemiology
- EPS of any kind occur in more than 30% of community samples [1].
- Spontaneous movement disorders in never-treated psychosis: dyskinesia 9%, parkinsonism 17% [1].
- Substance misuse increases dystonia, akathisia and TD; alcohol is linked to akathisia [1].
- NMS mortality is about 5–10% with modern care.

### Classification and criteria (DSM-5-TR)
- Medication-induced parkinsonism (antipsychotic or other).
- Neuroleptic malignant syndrome.
- Medication-induced acute dystonia.
- Medication-induced acute akathisia.
- Tardive dyskinesia; tardive dystonia; tardive akathisia.
- Medication-induced postural tremor.
- Other medication-induced movement disorder.
- Antidepressant discontinuation syndrome.

**ICD-11:** 8A00.2 drug-induced parkinsonism; 8A02.1 drug-induced dystonia; 8A07 drug-induced chorea/dyskinesia; 8A05 tremor; NMS (8D40.1).

### Mechanism in detail

| Disorder | Mechanism |
| --- | --- |
| Acute dystonia | Abrupt D2 blockade → compensatory dopamine release and cholinergic excess; low drug levels as concentrations fall (theory) |
| Parkinsonism | Striatal D2 occupancy >78–80% (PET); reduced direct-pathway drive |
| Akathisia | Mesocortical D2 blockade; noradrenergic hyperactivity (β-blocker response); 5-HT2A/2C (mirtazapine response); iron deficiency (low ferritin) |
| TD | D2 supersensitivity, GABA loss, oxidative stress |
| NMS | Hypothalamic thermoregulatory D2 blockade + striatal rigidity + sympathoadrenal hyperactivity |
| Serotonin syndrome | 5-HT2A and 5-HT1A over-stimulation |

### Clinical features in detail
- **Oculogyric crisis:** eyes fixed upward, distressing, can recur.
- **Laryngeal dystonia:** a medical emergency.
- **Pisa syndrome:** truncal lateral flexion (tardive or acute).
- **Akathisia vs agitation:** akathisia is mainly in the legs, with a subjective urge, and worsens with dose increases. It is often mistaken for psychotic agitation, which leads to further dose increases [1].
- **Parkinsonism vs negative symptoms or depression:** look for rigidity, tremor and cogwheeling [1].
- **NMS vs serotonin syndrome:** NMS has slow onset (days), bradyreflexia and lead-pipe rigidity; serotonin syndrome has rapid onset (hours), hyperreflexia, clonus and GI symptoms.
- **Catatonia overlap:** malignant catatonia can be indistinguishable from NMS; benzodiazepines and ECT treat both [1].

### Assessment in detail

| Scale | Measures |
| --- | --- |
| Simpson–Angus Scale (SAS) | 10 items: gait, arm drop, rigidity, glabellar tap, tremor, salivation |
| Barnes Akathisia Rating Scale (BARS) | Objective, subjective awareness, distress, global |
| AIMS | Dyskinesia in 7 body areas |
| ESRS / ESRS-A | All EPS |
| Bush–Francis Catatonia Rating Scale | Catatonia / NMS overlap |
| Hunter criteria | Serotonin toxicity (clonus-based) |
| Levenson / DSM-5 NMS criteria | NMS |

### Differential diagnosis

| Presentation | Consider |
| --- | --- |
| Acute dystonia | Tetanus, seizure, conversion, hypocalcaemia, meningitis (neck stiffness) |
| Parkinsonism | Idiopathic Parkinson's disease (asymmetric, DaT scan abnormal), Lewy body dementia (severe antipsychotic sensitivity), vascular parkinsonism, Wilson's |
| Akathisia | Agitation, anxiety, restless legs, withdrawal (alcohol, benzodiazepines, opioids), hyperthyroidism |
| TD | Huntington's, Wilson's, spontaneous dyskinesia, edentulous dyskinesia, tics |
| NMS | Serotonin syndrome, malignant hyperthermia, malignant catatonia, sepsis, meningitis or encephalitis, heat stroke, anticholinergic toxicity, lithium toxicity |

### Management details
- **Anticholinergics:**
  - Trihexyphenidyl 2–6 mg, procyclidine, benztropine, biperiden.
  - Adverse effects: cognitive impairment, dry mouth, constipation, urinary retention, glaucoma, delirium in the elderly, worsening TD, misuse potential (euphoria).
  - Review every 3 months [1].
- **Propranolol** for akathisia: 10 mg three times a day up to 80 mg/day; avoid in asthma, bradycardia, hypotension.
- **Mirtazapine** 15 mg at night for akathisia: weight gain and sedation [1].
- **Vitamin B6** 600 mg (2 × 300 mg) for akathisia [1].
- **NMS:**
  - Stop the antipsychotic and lithium; give IV fluids and cool the patient.
  - Lorazepam.
  - Dantrolene 1–2.5 mg/kg IV; bromocriptine 2.5 mg three times a day (orally or via nasogastric tube).
  - ECT if refractory.
  - DVT prophylaxis; monitor renal function.
  - After recovery: wait at least 2 weeks, then rechallenge with a low-potency or different-class drug, titrated slowly with CK and temperature monitoring.
- **Special groups:**
  - Elderly and Lewy body dementia: extreme sensitivity; avoid typicals; use quetiapine or pimavanserin.
  - Children: higher dystonia risk with high-potency drugs.
  - Intellectual disability: atypical presentations.

### Evidence table

| Study | Design | Result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Huhn et al., 2019 | Network meta-analysis, 32 antipsychotics | EPS ranking |
| Akathisia dose–response meta-analysis, 2023 [1] | 16 SGAs + haloperidol | Liability by drug and dose |
| Adjunct akathisia meta-analysis, 2024 [1] | 15 studies, 10 treatments | Mirtazapine, biperiden, B6 best |
| Poyurovsky et al., 2006 | RCT mirtazapine vs propranolol | Mirtazapine 15 mg ≈ propranolol 80 mg |
| Lerner et al., 2004 | RCT vitamin B6 for akathisia | Significant benefit |
| Carbon et al., 2018 [1] | TD incidence meta-analysis | SGA 2.6% vs FGA 6.5%/year |
| Pileggi & Cook, 2016 | NMS review | Supportive care, dantrolene, bromocriptine |

### Critical appraisal
- Many movement disorders are missed, or misattributed to illness [1].
- Prophylactic anticholinergics: they reduce dystonia in high-risk starts but carry cognitive and TD harms. Avoid routine long-term use [1].
- Evidence for akathisia treatments is weak in the medium and long term [1].
- SGAs are not EPS-free: risperidone at high doses and aripiprazole/cariprazine (akathisia) [1].

### Indian context
- Trihexyphenidyl is very commonly co-prescribed with antipsychotics in India, often indefinitely; it is also misused. Rationalise its use.
- Haloperidol and fluphenazine depot remain widely used; EPS monitoring is essential.
- NMS: dehydration in hot climates and catatonia-prone presentations increase risk; dantrolene may not be available everywhere, so bromocriptine and benzodiazepines are practical.
- Metoclopramide and prochlorperazine from general practitioners can cause acute dystonia in young people.

### Recent advances
- **Established:** VMAT2 inhibitors for TD [1]; vitamin B6 and mirtazapine for akathisia [1].
- **Emerging:** muscarinic antipsychotics (xanomeline–trospium) without EPS; pimavanserin (no D2) for PD psychosis.
- **Investigational:** genetic prediction of EPS (CYP2D6 poor metabolisers with haloperidol); DBS for tardive dystonia [1].
