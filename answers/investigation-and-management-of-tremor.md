## Exam answer

**Introduction.**
- Tremor is a **rhythmical** involuntary movement. In psychiatric practice it is common as a **drug side effect** (lithium, valproate, antipsychotics, SSRIs), in **withdrawal states** (alcohol), in **metabolic and endocrine disorders**, in **neurological disease** (Parkinson's disease, essential tremor, Wilson's disease) and as a **functional (psychogenic)** disorder [1, 2].
- Essential tremor is among the commonest movement disorders. Dystonia is the third commonest after Parkinson's disease and essential tremor [1, p.780].

### Causes of tremor relevant to psychiatry
| Group | Cause | Distinguishing features (source) |
|---|---|---|
| **Physiological, exaggerated** | Anxiety, thyrotoxicosis, drugs | **Fine**, low amplitude. Thyrotoxicosis: **8–10 Hz**, resembles exaggerated physiological tremor [1, p.633] |
| **Parkinsonian** | Idiopathic PD | **Rest** tremor, alternating contractions of opposing muscles, **4–6 Hz**; presenting feature in about **three-quarters**; often unilateral; cogwheel rigidity; worse when walking [1, pp.753–754] |
| **Drug-induced parkinsonism** | Antipsychotics | Tremor **much less common** than in idiopathic PD; late **perioral "rabbit" syndrome**; appears **early** in treatment, then often subsides [1, pp.746, 757]. Prevalence about 20%, more in **elderly women** and those with brain damage [2] |
| **Essential tremor** | Benign essential tremor (Minor's disease) | Any age; **family history**; **improves with alcohol**; **hands** mainly; **disappears at rest**; **head titubation** commoner than in PD; **no bradykinesia or rigidity**; static or very slowly progressive [1, p.757] |
| **Cerebellar** | MS, alcohol, toxins | **Intention** tremor, ataxia, nystagmus [1] |
| **Wilson's disease** | Copper | **Flapping tremor** at the wrists, "**wing-beating**" at the shoulders; tremor predominates in adults [1, p.772] |
| **Metabolic** | Uraemia, hypercapnia, hepatic failure, hypomagnesaemia | **Coarse postural and kinetic tremor**, myoclonus, **asterixis** [1, pp.664, 666–667] |
| **Drug: lithium** | Therapeutic level | **Fine** tremor (dose- and level-related) [2]. A fine tremor "must often be accepted" [1, p.722] |
| **Drug: lithium toxicity** | Level above 1.5 mmol/L | **Coarse** tremor, ataxia, twitching, confusion. **Stop lithium** [1, p.722; 2] |
| **Drug: valproate** | Dose-related | Up to **one-quarter** of patients; mostly **intention or postural**; rarely parkinsonism with cognitive decline, **reversible** on stopping [2] |
| **Drug: SSRIs in PD** | — | May worsen motor symptoms, especially tremor [2] |
| **Drug: others** | β-agonists, cholinesterase inhibitors | β-agonist tremor may be **misdiagnosed as PD**. Tremor is common with cholinesterase inhibitors [2] |
| **Withdrawal** | Alcohol | **Alcoholic tremor** is the **commonest withdrawal effect**, with weakness, nausea and irritability [1, p.693]. Acute tremulousness in 34% of withdrawal cases [1, p.694] |
| **Withdrawal** | Lithium, antidepressants, antipsychotics (cholinergic rebound) | Tremor listed among withdrawal effects [2] |
| **Toxic** | Mercury | Coarse tremor of hands, face and tongue, interrupted by jerks ("**hatters' shakes**") [1, p.727] |
| **Infective** | General paresis | Tremor in about **two-thirds**, with dysarthria and pupillary signs [1, p.426] |
| **Functional** | Psychogenic tremor | Variable, distractible, entrains, increases with loading [1, p.795] |

### Investigation

**1. History [1, 2]:**
- **Onset and course:** abrupt onset, rapid maximal severity and inconsistency over time suggest a **psychogenic** movement disorder [1, p.794]. Very slow progression over decades suggests essential tremor [1, p.757].
- **Family history:** essential tremor; Wilson's disease [1].
- **Response to alcohol:** improvement suggests essential tremor [1, p.757].
- **Drug history:** lithium, valproate, antipsychotics (timing of start or dose increase), SSRIs, β-agonists, cholinesterase inhibitors; recent **withdrawal** of alcohol, lithium or other drugs [1, 2].
- **Alcohol and substance use** [1].
- **Systemic symptoms:** weight loss, palpitations, heat intolerance (thyrotoxicosis); renal, liver or lung disease [1].
- **Psychiatric history:** previous medically unexplained symptoms are "highly suggestive" of a psychogenic movement disorder [1, p.794].

**2. Examination [1]:**
- **Character:** rest vs action; fine vs coarse; distribution (hands, head, lips, tongue).
- **Parkinsonian signs:** bradykinesia, cogwheel rigidity, reduced arm swing [1, p.754].
- **Cerebellar signs:** intention tremor, ataxia, nystagmus.
- **Thyroid signs:** AF, tachycardia over 90, goitre or bruit, exophthalmos, lid retraction, lid-lag, hot hands, fine finger tremor [1, p.634].
- **Kayser–Fleischer ring** (slit-lamp): its absence makes Wilson's disease improbable when there are neuropsychiatric symptoms [1, p.772].
- **Asterixis** (metabolic encephalopathy) [1].
- **Tests for psychogenic tremor (Table 12.3)** [1, p.795]:
  - **variability** in frequency, amplitude or distribution;
  - **distraction** (serial subtraction, rapid alternating movements of the normal limb) improves it;
  - **entrainment** to a rhythm tapped by the normal limb, or the requested rhythm becomes irregular. A 3 Hz rhythm is more sensitive;
  - **coactivation:** loading the limb **increases** functional tremor and **reduces** organic tremor.
  - **A negative test does not exclude a psychogenic cause.**
- **Rating scales:** Simpson–Angus EPS scale for antipsychotic pseudoparkinsonism [2]. CIWA-Ar tremor item in alcohol withdrawal [2].

**3. Laboratory tests [1, 2]:**
- **Serum lithium** (toxicity above 1.5 mmol/L) [2].
- **Thyroid function** (thyrotoxicosis; lithium also causes thyroid disorders) [1, 2].
- **Renal and liver function, electrolytes, magnesium, calcium, blood gases** (uraemia, hepatic failure, hypercapnia, hypomagnesaemia) [1].
- **Serum caeruloplasmin**, confirmed by **liver biopsy**, for Wilson's disease [1, p.593].
- **Syphilis serology** where general paresis is possible [1].

**4. Neurophysiology and imaging [1]:**
- **EMG:** confirms variability in frequency, coactivation and entrainment in psychogenic tremor. Sensitivity and specificity not yet established [1, p.795].
- **DaT SPECT (123I-FP-CIT):** abnormal in over 90% of early PD [1, p.795].
- **18F-dopa PET:** reduced striatal uptake in PD; **not affected in essential tremor** [1, p.143].
- **MRI in Wilson's disease:** symmetrical signal change in the lenticular nuclei; "face of the giant panda" sign [1, p.772].

### Management

**General.** Treat the cause; review drugs; reassure. Physiotherapy and attention to stress and depression also help, because they worsen disability in PD [1, p.757].

**1. Drug-induced tremor [1, 2]:**
- **Lithium:**
  - fine tremor at therapeutic levels may have to be accepted [1];
  - check the level; **propranolol** can be useful [2];
  - coarse tremor or other neurotoxic signs: **stop lithium at once** [1].
- **Valproate:** dose-related. Parkinsonism with cognitive decline reverses on stopping [2].
- **Antipsychotic pseudoparkinsonism** [2]:
  - **reduce the dose**;
  - **switch** to an antipsychotic with lower EPS liability;
  - or add an **anticholinergic**: review at least every **3 months**, do not give at night, and most patients do not need it long term.
  - For **PD psychosis**, clozapine has anti-tremor properties equivalent to benzatropine [2].
- **SSRIs in PD:** sertraline is the preferred SSRI. Monitor for worse tremor [2].
- **β-agonists:** avoid over-diagnosing PD. Caution when combining with cholinesterase inhibitors [2].

**2. Alcohol-withdrawal tremor:** manage the withdrawal syndrome (benzodiazepine regimen, such as starting diazepam 20 mg and reviewing) [2].

**3. Parkinson's disease [1, p.757–761]:**
- **Anticholinergics** in mild cases (trihexyphenidyl, benzatropine, orphenadrine, procyclidine, biperiden): some reduction in rigidity and tremor. Atropine-like effects and **cognitive worsening** limit them.
- **Levodopa:** rigidity helped; tremor improves less consistently. End-of-dose tremor may return.
- **Surgery:** **thalamic (ventral intermediate nucleus) lesions or deep brain stimulation** relieve tremor; pallidotomy improves contralateral tremor.

**4. Essential tremor:**
- **Thalamic VIM lesions** are effective [1, p.761].
- The sources give **no** drug treatment for essential tremor.

**5. Wilson's disease:** copper-related treatment. Tremor, rigidity and dystonia can respond [1, p.773].

**6. Metabolic and endocrine causes:** correct the cause, for example **magnesium replacement** reversed tremor in one hypomagnesaemia case [1, p.664].

**7. Psychogenic tremor [1, p.796]:**
- **Explain the diagnosis carefully:** "of paramount importance".
- **Psychiatric assessment** of maintaining factors.
- **Antidepressants** may help even without depression or anxiety.
- **CBT** or other psychotherapy.
- **Multidisciplinary rehabilitation** with physiotherapy.
- **Prognosis is often poor:** only 4 of 42 remitted at 3.2 years (Feinstein). Long duration, gradual onset and psychiatric comorbidity predict poor outcome. In the Williams programme, 52% benefited and 25% resolved completely.

**Conclusion.** For a psychiatrist, the key steps are:
- separate **fine physiological-type** from **coarse toxic** tremor;
- distinguish **rest (parkinsonian)** from **action (essential, drug-induced, cerebellar)** tremor;
- always check **drugs, alcohol, lithium level and thyroid**;
- exclude **Wilson's disease** in the young;
- use positive bedside signs for **functional tremor**.

Treatment is of the cause: dose reduction, switching or anticholinergics for drug-induced tremor; propranolol for lithium tremor; PD treatment or surgery; and explanation, psychotherapy and rehabilitation for psychogenic tremor.

## In depth

### Diagnostic certainty in psychogenic movement disorder (Fahn and Williams 1988; Table 12.2) [1, p.794]
| Category | Criteria |
|---|---|
| Documented | Persistent relief by psychotherapy, suggestion or placebo, or absent when unobserved |
| Clinically established | Incongruent or inconsistent, plus other psychogenic signs, multiple somatisations or obvious psychiatric disturbance |
| Probable | Incongruent or inconsistent, or psychogenic signs, or multiple somatisations |
| Possible | Evidence of emotional disturbance |
- The first two have since been merged into "**clinically definite**".
- **Comorbidity** in psychogenic movement disorder (Feinstein 2001): MDD 43%, anxiety 38%, personality disorder 45%.

### Mechanisms of psychogenic tremor (Raethjen 2004) [1, p.796]
1. Voluntary-like activity.
2. **Co-contraction** of agonist and antagonist muscles producing physiological **clonus** (as in shivering).
