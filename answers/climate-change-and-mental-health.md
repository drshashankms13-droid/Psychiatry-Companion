## Exam answer

**Definition.** The IPCC defines **climate change** as "a change in the state of the climate that can be identified by changes in the mean and/or the variability of its properties, and that persists for an extended period, typically decades or longer" [1]. The current warming is **unequivocally human-caused** since the mid-20th century and is happening at a rate unprecedented over millennia [1].

Its mental health effects run through **acute** hazards (cyclones, floods, landslides, droughts, wildfires, heat waves) and **chronic** changes (rising temperature, changed rainfall, sea-level rise, ocean acidification, glacier melt) [1].

### Mechanism: exposure pathways (the "mental health cascade") [1]
- **Direct:** disasters → trauma, bereavement, displacement → acute stress, PTSD, depression, anxiety, substance use, suicide.
- **Indirect:**
  - **Biological:** heat, air pollution (PM2.5, NO₂, ozone), toxicants. Heat affects thermoregulation (worse with lithium, antipsychotics and anticholinergics), sleep and serotonergic function.
  - **Social and economic:** crop failure, loss of livelihood and livestock, migration, conflict, violence, poverty, loss of education and income.
  - **Cognitive and emotional:** loss of place and identity ("solastalgia"), helplessness, despair, anger, guilt, **eco-anxiety**.

### Mental health outcomes

| Non-pathological | Pathological |
| --- | --- |
| Helplessness, grief and stress reactions, fear, worry about the future (eco-anxiety), insomnia, irritability and aggression, strained relationships | Anxiety disorders, depression, **PTSD**, adjustment disorder, **substance use**, **suicide and self-harm**, cognitive decline (pollution), worsening of existing SMI, heat-related hospitalisation and mortality in psychiatric patients |

Source: [1].

### Evidence
**Global** [1]:
- A 2021 scoping review (Charlson et al.) found **heat waves** were associated with more psychiatric admissions and distress, more depression, more aggression, domestic violence and self-harm.
- **Air pollution:** long-term PM2.5 and NO₂ exposure is associated with depression. Prenatal particulate exposure is linked to preterm birth and neurodevelopmental problems, including ASD.
- **Floods:** more schizophrenia admissions (Hefei, China).
- **Ozone:** chronic exposure is linked to cognitive decline in older adults (China).
- **Sea-level rise:** 56 of 57 Solomon Islanders reported that worry affected their thinking and behaviour.

**India** [1]:
- **Crop-damaging temperatures and farmer suicides (Carleton 2017, PNAS):**
  - On days above 20 °C in the growing season, a 1 °C rise increases annual suicides by 0.008 per 100,000 (about 67 extra deaths).
  - Warming over 30 years is estimated to explain **about 59,300 farmer suicides** (6.8% of the upward trend).
- **Odisha super-cyclone (1999):** a year later, adolescents showed PTSD 26.9%, major depression 17.6%, GAD 12% (Kar). Among adults, 80.4% had a probable psychiatric disorder.
- **Kodagu floods (2018):** psychiatric problems in 66.7%; depression 33%, anxiety 31%, sleep problems 35.7%.
- **Sundarbans:** MDD, somatoform pain, PTSD after animal attacks; 11.2% had self-harm attempts, mostly with pesticides.
- **Disasters studied:** Uttarakhand floods (2013), the tsunami (2004, Andaman and Nicobar children), Kerala floods (2018), Wayanad landslides (2024).
- **Vulnerability:** **nine Indian states** are among the world's top 50 most climate-vulnerable provinces (XDI 2023), led by Bihar, Uttar Pradesh and Assam.

### Vulnerable groups [1]
- People with **pre-existing mental illness** (heat-stroke risk with psychotropics; disrupted care; neglect).
- Children and adolescents (immature thermoregulation; existential worry).
- The elderly; pregnant women.
- Farmers and fisherfolk.
- Indigenous and tribal communities; the poor; low- and middle-income countries.

### Management and response
**Universal** (whole population) [1]:
- Awareness and literacy.
- Climate content in psychiatric training curricula.
- Advocacy.
- Positive mental health strategies.

**Selective** (at-risk groups) [1]:
- Resilience building; life-skills training; yoga and stress reduction.
- Targeted outreach to SMI, children, the elderly and the homeless.
- **Extreme-weather plans** for psychiatric services, e.g., heat-wave protocols for patients on lithium, clozapine or anticholinergics.

**Indicated** (people with symptoms) [1]:
- Screening, brief intervention and referral (stepped care) for CMDs, suicide, violence and substance use.
- **Psychological first aid.**
- Tele-mental health.

**Systems:**
- **NPCCHH** (National Programme on Climate Change and Human Health) [1]:
  - NIMHANS is the Centre of Excellence for mental health.
  - Health Action Plan and SOPs.
  - Training of state and district nodal officers, MOs, paramedics and communities (5 modules).
  - Surveillance (**climate change–mental health observatories**).
  - Screening tools (MERIT, CSP) for field workers.
  - Vulnerability mapping.
  - Coordination with MoEFCC, NDMA, DMHP, NHM and Ayushman Bharat.
- **Disaster management:** NDMA, SDMA, DDMA, NDRF; NDMA psychosocial support guidelines; community-based disaster management [1].
- **Mitigation co-benefits:** greener health systems; active travel; urban green space.

**Conclusion.** Climate change is a **threat multiplier** for mental health. It acts directly through disasters and heat, and indirectly through economic loss, migration, pollution and uncertainty about the future. India is among the most exposed countries, and its burden falls on farmers, coastal communities, the poor and people with existing mental illness. Psychiatry's response should combine **disaster mental health preparedness, heat-health protocols for psychiatric patients, community resilience, surveillance and advocacy**, integrated with NPCCHH, DMHP and disaster management systems.

## In depth

### Background
- **IPCC Assessment Reports** (AR6, 2021–23) recognise mental health impacts with high confidence.
- **Lancet Countdown** on health and climate change (annual since 2016).
- **WHO policy brief "Mental health and climate change" (2022)** recommends integrating climate into mental health policy, building on international commitments, community approaches and closing the funding gap.
- The term **"solastalgia"** (Albrecht 2005) means distress caused by environmental change to one's home. **Eco-anxiety** and **climate grief** are newer constructs.

### Epidemiology
- **Global warming:** about 1.1–1.2 °C above pre-industrial levels. The 2020s include the hottest years on record [1].
- **Heat:** each 1 °C rise in temperature is associated with about a 1–2% increase in mental-health-related ED visits or mortality in meta-analyses (e.g., Liu 2021; check figures). Suicide rises with heat (Burke 2018: 0.7% in the US per 1 °C monthly increase; check figures).
- **Youth eco-anxiety:** a global survey (Hickman 2021, Lancet Planetary Health, 10,000 people aged 16–25 in 10 countries, including India) found 59% very or extremely worried and over 45% reporting that it affected daily functioning.
- **India:** heat waves (2015, 2019, 2024) with thousands of deaths; recurrent floods and cyclones; rising farmer distress [1].

### Classification and criteria
- **No diagnosis of "eco-anxiety"** exists in ICD-11 or DSM-5-TR. Most worry is proportionate and adaptive, not pathological.
- Disaster-related disorders are diagnosed by standard criteria (PTSD, adjustment disorder, depression, prolonged grief disorder).
- **Non-syndromal vs syndromal effects** (Math et al. 2015): both appear in acute and chronic phases [1].

### Mechanism in detail
| Pathway | Example mechanism |
| --- | --- |
| Heat | Impaired thermoregulation; ↑ irritability and aggression; poor sleep; dehydration → lithium toxicity; anticholinergic and antipsychotic heat-stroke risk; NMS risk |
| Air pollution | Neuroinflammation, oxidative stress, HPA activation → depression, anxiety, cognitive decline, neurodevelopmental effects [1] |
| Disasters | Trauma exposure, bereavement, displacement, loss of social support [1] |
| Economic | Crop failure → debt → farmer suicide [1] |
| Migration and conflict | Acculturation stress, violence, loss of community [1] |
| Moral and existential | Eco-anxiety, guilt, despair; **moral disengagement** explains societal inaction [1] |

### Clinical features in detail: disaster phases
- **Acute (days to weeks):** shock, fear, grief, survivor guilt, insomnia, somatic symptoms, acute stress reactions [1].
- **Intermediate and chronic:** PTSD, depression, adjustment disorder, substance dependence, complicated grief, loss of identity and roles [1].
- **Children:** regression, school refusal, anxiety, PTSD. Indian studies after the tsunami and Uttarakhand floods found high subclinical distress [1].

### Assessment in detail
- **Screening:** PHQ-9, GAD-7, PCL-5, C-SSRS; disaster-specific checklists; Indian SOP screening tools (MERIT, CSP) [1].
- **Heat-risk assessment** for psychiatric patients: medications (lithium, clozapine, anticholinergics, diuretics), age, homelessness, substance use.
- **Community vulnerability mapping** [1].

### Differential: normal vs pathological climate distress
| Feature | Adaptive concern | Clinical disorder |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Proportionality | Proportionate to the real threat | Excessive; pervasive |
| Function | Motivates action | Impairs function |
| Duration | Fluctuates | Persistent (≥2 weeks for depression; ≥1 month for PTSD symptoms) |
| Response | Coping, activism, community | Needs treatment |

### Management details
- **Heat-health action plans** (e.g., the Ahmedabad Heat Action Plan, 2013):
  - Early warnings.
  - Check on vulnerable patients.
  - Cooling centres.
  - Medication review: consider lithium levels and hydration; avoid excess anticholinergics.
- **Psychological first aid** (WHO): look, listen, link [1].
- **Stepped care** after disasters: community support → lay counselling → specialist care. Avoid routine psychological debriefing.
- **Treat PTSD and depression** with evidence-based CBT or EMDR and SSRIs.
- **Address eco-anxiety:** validation; meaning-making; collective action; limit doom-scrolling; CBT for excessive worry.
- **Tele-MANAS** for remote support after disasters [1].

### Evidence table
| Study | Design | Finding |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Charlson et al. 2021 (IJERPH) | Scoping review | Heat waves → admissions, distress, depression, aggression, self-harm [1] |
| Carleton 2017 (PNAS) | Panel data, India | Crop-damaging heat → suicides; about 59,300 farmer suicides over 30 years [1] |
| Kar et al. 2004/2006 | Post-cyclone survey, Odisha | Adults 80.4% probable disorder; adolescents PTSD 26.9% [1] |
| Vert et al. 2017 | Cross-sectional, Barcelona (n=958) | Air pollutants associated with depression history [1] |
| Asugeni 2015 | Solomon Islands survey | 56/57 report sea-level worry affecting behaviour [1] |
| Hickman et al. 2021 (Lancet Planet Health) | Survey of 10,000 young people | Widespread climate anxiety |

### Critical appraisal
- **Evidence is mostly observational and cross-sectional**, with confounding by poverty and pre-existing vulnerability. Causal attribution to climate (rather than to weather or disasters) is difficult.
- **Measurement:** heterogeneous outcomes; few LMIC longitudinal studies.
- **Risk of pathologising** normal concern (eco-anxiety). Most climate distress is a rational response.
- **Systems gap:** disaster risk reduction policy in India is fragmented, with limited resources and stakeholder engagement [1].
- **Strength:** mental health is now recognised within national climate-health planning (NPCCHH), with NIMHANS leading [1].

### Indian context
- **NPCCHH (2019):** awareness, capacity building, health-sector preparedness, partnerships, R&D. State action plans include mental health [1].
- **Disaster management structure:** CCMNC, NCMC, NDMA, NIDM, NDRF, SDMA, DDMA [1]. **NDMA Guidelines on Psychosocial Support and Mental Health Services in Disasters (2009).**
- **Indian expertise:** NIMHANS psychosocial disaster response (tsunami, Uttarakhand, Kerala floods, COVID-19) [1].
- **Farmer suicides:** linked to climate-driven crop loss, debt and pesticide access. **Pesticide regulation** (bans on highly hazardous pesticides) is a proven suicide-prevention measure.

### Recent advances
- **Established:** NPCCHH mental health action plan and SOPs; WHO 2022 policy brief [1].
- **Emerging:**
  - Climate-mental health observatories and surveillance [1].
  - Heat-health early warning integrated with psychiatric services.
  - Climate-informed psychiatry curricula [1].
  - "Planetary health" psychiatry.
  - Low-carbon mental health services (telepsychiatry reduces travel) [1].
