## Exam answer

**Definition.** **Supported employment (SE)** is a vocational rehabilitation approach that helps people with severe mental illness (SMI) obtain and keep **competitive jobs**: regular jobs in the open labour market, at at least minimum wage, alongside non-disabled workers. Placement happens **rapidly, without extended pre-vocational preparation**, and support continues on or off the job for as long as needed. It is a **"place-then-train"** approach, in contrast to the traditional **"train-then-place"** approach [1].

Its driving philosophy is that, given adequate support, everyone who wants to work is capable of competitive employment [1].

### Rationale
- People with SMI have very high unemployment. In India, about **87%** of persons with mental illness are outside the labour force [1].
- Work brings income, structure to the day, social contact, a positive identity, and contribution to the community. It is a core component of **recovery** [1].

### Mechanism
Rapid placement in a preferred job → real-world learning, income and role identity → ↑ self-esteem, social network and daily structure → ↓ symptoms and hospital use; ↑ quality of life. **Continuous support** from an employment specialist integrated with the clinical team counters the **cognitive deficits, negative symptoms, relapses and stigma** that otherwise cause job loss [1, 2].

### Models

| Model | Origin / key idea | Phases / features |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Individual Placement and Support (IPS)** — evidence-based form of SE | Becker & Drake (1990s, Dartmouth) | Rapid job search, integration of vocational and clinical services, follow-along support. Phases: engagement and vocational assessment → employment (job search) → job support [1] |
| **Choose–Get–Keep** (Boston University psychiatric rehabilitation approach) | Anthony, Howell & Danley | Skill-building in career planning. **Choose** (skills, preferences, interests, job development) → **Get** (job-seeking skills) → **Keep** (job-retention skills); practitioner diagnoses, plans and intervenes [1] |
| **Choose–Get–Keep–Leave** | Extension of the above | Helps the person **leave a job** well, so it builds future success [1] |
| **Augmented SE** | SE + extra techniques | Adds social skills training, cognitive remediation, motivational interventions [1, 2] |
| **SE within other services** | ACT teams, residential programmes, peer-delivered services | Employment support delivered by assertive community treatment teams, in residential settings, or by peer employees [1] |

**Older "train–place" models** (for contrast): sheltered workshops, transitional employment (the **Clubhouse** model, Fountain House 1948), work crews, social firms, day-centre vocational training. They give structure but lead less often to competitive work [2].

### IPS principles (fidelity scale)
1. **Zero exclusion:** anyone who wants to work is eligible, regardless of diagnosis, symptoms or substance use.
2. **Competitive employment** is the goal.
3. **Integration** of employment specialists with the mental health team.
4. **Client preferences** guide the job type and disclosure.
5. **Personalised benefits counselling.**
6. **Rapid job search:** first employer contact within about 30 days.
7. **Systematic job development** with employers.
8. **Time-unlimited, individualised follow-along support** [1, 2].

### Assessment
- Vocational profile: work history, education, skills, interests, preferences, support needs [1].
- Clinical: symptoms, cognition (attention, processing speed, executive function), substance use, medication side effects (sedation, EPS).
- Disability: IDEAS score and disability certificate (RPwD Act) for reservation and benefits.
- Workplace: job demands, accommodations, accessibility [1].

### Management steps
Engage and assess preferences → benefits counselling and disclosure decision → rapid job search with employer contact → placement → on-the-job and off-site support (coaching, problem-solving, liaison with employer and family) → integrate with treatment (medication timing, relapse plan) → career progression, or a supported job change [1, 2].

### Key evidence
- **EQOLISE** (Burns 2007, Lancet, 6 European sites, n=312): IPS vs vocational services. **55% vs 28%** worked at least 1 day; IPS also reduced re-hospitalisation [2].
- **Meta-analyses** (Modini 2016; Cochrane 2013/2017): IPS is about **2–2.5 times** as likely to achieve competitive employment as traditional vocational rehabilitation, with longer job tenure and higher earnings. Augmenting IPS with cognitive remediation or skills training improves outcomes further [2].

**Conclusion.** Supported employment, especially **IPS**, is the best-evidenced vocational intervention for SMI and should be routine in community psychiatry. In India, legal mandates (RPwD Act 2016: 4% reservation, reasonable accommodation, non-discrimination) exist, but there are almost no employment specialists, and most work is informal. The challenge is to adapt IPS principles to self-employment, family enterprises and the informal sector, rather than relying on sheltered workshops.

## In depth

### Background
- Supported employment was first developed in the 1970s–80s for people with **intellectual and developmental disabilities**, as an alternative to sheltered workshops. It was later adapted for psychiatric disability [1].
- The US Rehabilitation Act Amendments of 1986 formally recognised supported employment.
- In the early 1990s Becker and Drake (New Hampshire–Dartmouth) defined IPS and its fidelity scale, by converting day treatment programmes into SE programmes. Employment rose without adverse effects.
- Anthony and colleagues (Boston University Center for Psychiatric Rehabilitation) developed the Choose–Get–Keep model within the psychiatric rehabilitation framework [1].
- Supported employment represents **inclusion** of people with disabilities "into the fabric of community settings" (DiLeo) [1].

### Epidemiology: employment and disability in India
- **Census 2011:** 2.68 crore persons with disabilities (2.21% of the population) [1].
- **Central Statistics Organisation:** 87.3% of persons with mental illness were outside the labour force (Chakrabarti 2010) [1].
- **WHO:** 87% of persons with disabilities in India work in the informal sector [1].
- **World Bank (2007), *People with Disabilities in India*:** the employment rate of disabled people fell from 42.7% (1991) to 37.6% (2002) [1].
- Average employment rate of people with disabilities: **0.28%** in the private sector and **0.54%** in the public sector [1].
- **NCPEDP (2009), central ministries:**
  - 4,527,293 posts, of which 460,396 were identified for disabled people.
  - Only 20,053 were filled: 4.46% of identified posts, **0.44% of all posts**.
  - Public sector undertakings: only 0.37% of all posts were filled by people with disabilities [1].
- Internationally, employment rates in schizophrenia are typically 10–20%, while most patients say they want to work [2].

### Mechanism in detail: barriers that SE addresses

| Barrier | How it causes job loss | SE response |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cognitive deficits (attention, speed, executive function) | Slow learning, errors | Job matching, on-site coaching, cognitive remediation (augmented SE) |
| Negative symptoms, low motivation | Absenteeism | Preference-based jobs, motivational support |
| Relapse and hospitalisation | Breaks in employment | Integrated clinical team, relapse plans, job held open |
| Stigma, disclosure dilemmas | Discrimination | Disclosure counselling, employer liaison, legal protections |
| Benefits trap (fear of losing pension) | Disincentive to work | Benefits counselling |
| Social skills deficits | Conflict with co-workers | Social skills training |
| Medication side effects | Sedation, tremor | Dose timing, switching |

### Clinical features in detail: who does well
- **Predictors of employment:** previous work history (strongest), lower negative symptoms, better cognition, motivation, and programme fidelity. Diagnosis, symptom severity and substance use predict outcome poorly, which supports **zero exclusion** [2].
- People with first-episode psychosis do especially well with IPS (early intervention services, education as well as work).

### Assessment in detail
- **Vocational assessment within IPS is brief and continuous**, carried out through real work experience, not lengthy testing [1].
- **Tools:** Work Behaviour Inventory, Vocational Fidelity Scale (IPS-25), cognitive tests (MATRICS), Indian Disability Evaluation and Assessment Scale (**IDEAS**), WHO-DAS 2.0.
- **Under the RPwD Act,** a **benchmark disability (≥40%)** certified by a medical authority is needed for reservation and many benefits.

### Differential: vocational models compared

| Feature | Sheltered workshop | Transitional employment (Clubhouse) | Pre-vocational training | Supported employment (IPS) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Approach | Train-place (often no place) | Time-limited placements owned by clubhouse | Train-place | Place-train |
| Setting | Segregated | Community jobs, but temporary | Clinic | Competitive community jobs |
| Wage | Token | Minimum wage | None | Market wage |
| Support duration | Indefinite | Time-limited | Time-limited | Time-unlimited |
| Evidence for competitive work | Weak | Moderate | Weak | Strong |

### Management details
- **Employment specialist caseload:** about 20 clients. The specialist attends clinical team meetings, which is what "integration" means in practice [2].
- **Job development:** build relationships with employers; job carving; use family networks (very important in India).
- **Follow-along support:** workplace visits (with consent), phone check-ins, problem-solving and employer education. Support is time-unlimited but fades as the person stabilises [1, 2].
- **Disclosure:** the person decides. Disclosure enables accommodations but risks stigma.
- **Special groups:**
  - Young people with first-episode psychosis: supported education plus IPS.
  - People with co-occurring substance use: not excluded.
  - Women: flexible, part-time and home-based work.
- **Peer-delivered services:** peer-run services, peer partnerships and peer employees. They are built on respect, shared responsibility and mutual agreement about what helps [1].
- **ACT teams:** provide case management and help consumers get and keep regular jobs. With no fixed length of stay, they avoid the stigma of attending a centre [1].

### Evidence table

| Study | Design | n | Result |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Drake et al. 1996, 1999 | RCTs, IPS vs group skills training / enhanced vocational rehab | 143 / 152 | Competitive employment much higher with IPS [2] |
| Burns et al. 2007 (EQOLISE), Lancet | RCT, 6 European countries | 312 | 55% vs 28% worked ≥1 day; fewer readmissions [2] |
| Kinoshita et al. 2013, Cochrane | Systematic review | 14 RCTs | SE increases competitive employment, days worked and job tenure [2] |
| Modini et al. 2016, Br J Psychiatry | Meta-analysis | 17 RCTs | IPS about 2.4 times more likely to gain competitive employment, across economic conditions [2] |
| Suijkerbuijk et al. 2017, Cochrane | Network meta-analysis | 48 RCTs | Augmented SE > SE alone > pre-vocational training > TAU [2] |
| McGurk et al. 2007 | RCT, SE + cognitive remediation | 44 | Higher employment and earnings than SE alone [2] |

### Critical appraisal
- Most evidence comes from **high-income countries** with formal labour markets and welfare benefits. Transferability to India's **informal economy** (about 87% of disabled workers) is uncertain [1].
- Many jobs obtained through IPS are **part-time, entry-level and short-lived**, and long-term tenure data are limited [2].
- **Fidelity drift** reduces effectiveness; IPS needs dedicated staff and funding, which few Indian services have.
- **Choose–Get–Keep and skills models** have weaker trial evidence but may suit people who want career planning.
- Residential programmes can recreate **"mini-institutions"** when services are delivered in-house [1].
- **Outcome definitions vary** (any work vs sustained work vs income), making comparisons hard.

### Indian context
- **RPwD Act 2016:**
  - **Section 34:** reservation in government establishments raised from 3% to **4%** of vacancies for benchmark disabilities. Mental illness falls within the 1% shared with autism, intellectual disability, SLD and multiple disabilities [1].
  - **Section 20:** non-discrimination in government employment and **reasonable accommodation**. No promotion may be denied, and no employee who acquires a disability may be dispensed with or reduced in rank. If they are unsuitable for their post, they are shifted to another post with the same pay, or kept on a **supernumerary post** until a suitable post is available or superannuation [1].
  - **Section 35:** incentives to private employers so that at least 5% of their workforce are persons with benchmark disability.
  - Every establishment must have an **equal opportunity policy** [1].
- **Emerging issues:** identification of jobs for mental illness under the reservation clause; repeated medical examinations of disabled employees; lack of accessibility; disability discrimination [1].
- **Government skill initiatives:** National Skill Development Corporation (NSDC) programmes; short-term **Modular Employable Skills (MES)** courses for school leavers, dropouts and informal workers [1].
- **Government job groups:** Group D/C (7th CPC pay levels 1–5) through Group A (levels 10–14) [1].
- **Service examples:** NIMHANS psychiatric rehabilitation services (vocational training units, day care); NGOs such as The Banyan (Chennai), SCARF (Chennai), Richmond Fellowship Society (Bengaluru), and the Medico-Pastoral Association (Bengaluru). Many use family-based, self-employment and social-enterprise models.
- **MHCA 2017, Section 18:** the government must provide rehabilitation services, including supported employment and community-based rehabilitation.
- **Disability benefits for psychiatric disorders** depend on IDEAS certification (≥40% benchmark disability).

### Recent advances
- **Established:** IPS fidelity-based implementation in the UK, US and Europe; IPS in early intervention in psychosis; augmented SE with cognitive remediation [2].
- **Emerging:**
  - IPS for common mental disorders and in primary care.
  - Digital job-search support.
  - IPS adaptations for low- and middle-income countries (for example, in China and Hong Kong), using self-employment and family business models.
  - Peer employment specialists [2].
