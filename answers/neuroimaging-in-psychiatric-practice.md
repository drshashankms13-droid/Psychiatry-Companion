## Exam answer

### Principles [1]
- Investigations should give the **most information with the least inconvenience and risk** to the patient.
- **Non-invasive tests (EEG, CT)** may be done even when suspicion of an organic cause is low. Invasive procedures are kept for specific, important questions.
- **Few tests have well-defined sensitivity and specificity** for neuropsychiatric disorders, so judgement is needed.
- An investigation is useful only if it has been **discussed with the person performing it**.

### Structural imaging

**1. Computed tomography (CT)** [1]
- **About 100 times more sensitive** than conventional radiography, with no greater radiation than a skull series. Non-invasive; no anaesthesia.
- **Shows:**
  - ventricles, basal cisterns, sulci, thalami, caudate heads, internal capsule;
  - **space-occupying lesions** (tumour, abscess, haematoma), oedema;
  - **infarction** as low density; **haemorrhage vs infarction** (important before anticoagulation);
  - **acute head-injury haematomas**, before clinical deterioration;
  - **CT angiography** (helical CT with bolus contrast): 3-D views of aneurysms and malformations;
  - white matter lucencies (MS; **leucoaraiosis**), though **MRI is the method of choice** for white matter.
- **Contrast** (iodinated) helps separate tumour from surrounding brain.
- **Preferred over MRI** for obese patients and for those who cannot lie still.

**2. Interpreting atrophy (CT and MRI)** [1]
- **Ventricles enlarge with age** even in healthy people, sharply from age 70. The normal range widens in the elderly.
- **Dementia:**
  - CT correctly predicted dementia in **83%** of elderly subjects (Jacoby & Levy); cortical atrophy discriminated better than ventricular size;
  - at autopsy, ventricles were **normal for age in about 40%** of senile dementia, so a normal scan does not exclude dementia;
  - **cognitive severity tracks atrophy**, ventricular measures more often than cortical; further enlargement parallels decline;
  - **hippocampal, amygdala and entorhinal volumes**: positive predictive value **about 70–80%** for AD, even against MCI.
- **Schizophrenia:** ventricular enlargement first shown on CT by **Johnstone et al. (1976)**; affective disorder studies followed.
- **Reversible or misleading atrophy:** **alcohol**, **steroids** (largely reversible on stopping), **anorexia nervosa** (minor, reversible).

**3. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)** [1]
- **No ionising radiation**; uses hydrogen nuclei in a magnetic field (0.5–4.0 T) excited by radiofrequency pulses.
- **Sequences:**
  - **T1** (inversion recovery): CSF dark; excellent grey–white contrast and anatomy;
  - **T2** (spin echo): CSF and grey matter light, white matter dark;
  - proton density;
  - **FLAIR**: CSF signal suppressed, so periventricular lesions (MS plaques), **hippocampal sclerosis** and **limbic encephalitis** stand out. A healthy hippocampus may also look mildly bright;
  - **DWI**: **acute infarction** (water diffusion halves early, giving hyperintensity);
  - **DTI and tractography**: white-matter tract integrity and orientation; promising in neurodevelopmental disorders;
  - **magnetisation transfer / MRA**: vessels, usually with gadolinium;
  - **gadolinium** contrast: blood–brain barrier breakdown; tumour vs oedema;
  - **T2\***: **microbleeds** (hypertension, amyloid angiopathy, CADASIL).
- **Advantages over CT:**
  - small demyelinating lesions; **posterior fossa and pituitary** (no bone artefact); any plane without moving the patient;
  - tumours, **small infarcts**, epileptogenic lesions, **residua of head injury**, **infections including HIV**;
  - safe for **repeat scans**.
- **Psychiatrically important signs:**
  - **variant CJD**: **pulvinar sign** (T2) and **"hockey-stick" sign** (FLAIR: pulvinar plus dorsomedial thalamus);
  - **frontotemporal dementia**: frontal and temporal atrophy; **left perisylvian** in progressive non-fluent aphasia; **left anterior temporal** in semantic dementia;
  - **Huntington's disease**: caudate and putamen thinning, even before symptoms;
  - **Alzheimer's disease**: medial temporal atrophy predicted decline over 6 years with **91% specificity, 89% sensitivity** (Rusinek). Atrophy runs at **2–3% a year** in AD vs **0.2–0.5%** in healthy middle age. Serial registered scans (boundary shift integral) measure change precisely.
- **Safety and contraindications:**
  - **intracranial metal** (aneurysm clips, skull prostheses: heating risk);
  - **cardiac pacemakers, cochlear implants**;
  - **claustrophobia** and **noise** (ear protection needed).

**4. White matter hyperintensities (WMH)** [1]
- Best seen on T2 or FLAIR. Common in the elderly; when **multiple and confluent** they are pathological.
- **Pathology:** myelin rarefaction, gliosis, microvascular change, probably ischaemia of **penetrating arterioles**.
- **Associations:** vascular events, **cognitive decline** and **depression** (especially **frontal** WMH).
- **Grading (Kapeller):**
  - deep: **punctate (1)**, early confluent (2), confluent (3);
  - periventricular: caps (1), bands (2), extension into deep white matter (3).
- **Rotterdam study:** "silent" infarcts gave a **more than 2-fold dementia risk** and a **5-fold stroke risk** over 3–4 years.
- **Age norms are essential:** over 60, only **5% had no WMH** at all; at 80–90, none were free of subcortical lesions.

### Functional and molecular imaging

**5. Magnetic resonance spectroscopy (MRS)** [1]
- Uses an MRI scanner (1.5 T or more) to measure tissue chemistry.
- **¹H MRS:** NAA, choline, creatine, lactate, glutamate/aspartate.
  - **NAA is neuronal**: the NAA:creatine ratio falls with neuronal damage, sometimes before structural change.
  - Choline and creatine rise with astrocytosis.
  - **Schizophrenia:** reduced hippocampal (left more than right) and frontal NAA; reduced DLPFC glutamate/aspartate.
- **³¹P MRS:** ATP, phosphocreatine, inorganic phosphate (energy metabolism) and membrane phospholipids. DLPFC hypoactivity and membrane changes in schizophrenia.
- **Other nuclei:** **⁷Li** (lithium pharmacokinetics), **¹⁹F** (fluorinated antipsychotics), ¹³C, ²³Na.

**6. Functional MRI (fMRI)** [1]
- **BOLD** contrast: local activity raises blood flow and changes the oxy- to deoxyhaemoglobin ratio, brightening active regions. **Non-invasive; no radiation.**
- **Strengths over PET:** finer spatial resolution, sub-second temporal resolution, repeatable.
- **Research uses:** cognition, emotion, **auditory hallucinations**, compulsions, fears.
- **Limits:**
  - scanner space limits task design;
  - **signal drop-out** near air-filled bone (temporal and inferior frontal lobes);
  - poor test–retest reliability; heavy post-processing; group averaging.
  - **Clinical use so far is limited.**
- **Clinical application:** **language lateralisation before epilepsy surgery** (less invasive than the Wada test); mapping eloquent cortex.

**7. Positron emission tomography (PET)** [1]
- Short-lived isotopes (**¹⁵O, ¹³N, ¹¹C, ¹⁸F**; half-lives about 2, 10, 20 and 110 minutes) need a **nearby cyclotron**, so PET is restricted to special centres.
- **Two uses:**
  1. **Blood flow (¹⁵O-water) and metabolism (¹⁸F-FDG).** Declining in research but with a clear **clinical role in dementia and movement disorders**.
  2. **Receptor and transporter mapping** with radioligands. Expanding.
- **FDG findings:**
  - **AD**: **temporoparietal hypometabolism** (up to 50% in severe cases);
  - FTD: frontal and temporal hypometabolism;
  - **Huntington's disease**: caudate hypometabolism;
  - **epileptic focus**: hypermetabolic ictally (up to 70%), hypometabolic interictally (up to 80%);
  - stroke: "luxury" and "misery" perfusion, explaining large deficits from small lesions.
  - Because of normal ageing variation, **PET is not yet a diagnostic test** for dementia.
- **Ligands:**
  - **¹¹C-raclopride** (D2); **¹⁸F-dopa** (presynaptic stores); ¹¹C-flumazenil; ¹¹C-carfentanil; 5-HT2A ligands.
  - **Antipsychotics:** D2 occupancy is rapid on starting and reverses on stopping. It is dose-related to serum levels, and **fast D2 dissociation** may explain the low EPS of atypicals.
- **Parkinson's disease:**
  - reduced, asymmetric **caudal putamen ¹⁸F-dopa** uptake;
  - **normal in essential tremor and vascular parkinsonism**;
  - VMAT2 (¹¹C-DTBZ) loss even in early disease.

**8. Single photon emission CT (SPECT)** [1]
- Gamma-emitting tracers with **longer half-lives**, so **no cyclotron is needed** and it is available wherever there is nuclear medicine.
- **Cheaper** than PET but **less precise**: resolution 8–10 mm vs 5–7 mm for PET. **Wide normal variation.**
- **Perfusion tracers:**
  - ¹³³Xe; ¹²³I-IMP;
  - **⁹⁹ᵐTc-HMPAO** (now standard): a "frozen" image, scanned 20–30 minutes after injection;
  - **⁹⁹ᵐTc-ECD**.
- **Clinical uses:**
  - **AD** (temporoparietal hypoperfusion); **frontal dementias**; multi-infarct (scattered defects); Huntington's disease (caudate);
  - stroke, migraine, epilepsy, alcoholism;
  - schizophrenia (frontal hypoperfusion); depression (reduced flow, rising with recovery); hallucinations (research).
- **Neurotransmitter SPECT:**
  - **¹²³I-IBZM** (D2); ¹²³I-QNB (muscarinic; reduced in AD);
  - **dopamine transporter: ¹²³I-β-CIT** (about 50% reduction at presentation of PD) and **¹²³I-FP-CIT** (imaging at 3 hours instead of 24); ⁹⁹ᵐTc-TRODAT-1.

**9. Older or angiographic techniques** [1]
- **Catheter angiography**: largely replaced by CT angiography and MRA, but still needed for precise delineation of vascular anomalies.
- **Air encephalography**, isotope scanning and isotope cisternography: obsolete, replaced by CT and MRI.

### Practical use in psychiatry (as drawn from the source) [1]

| Clinical question | Imaging suggested in the source |
| --- | --- |
| Space-occupying lesion, haemorrhage, acute head injury | **CT** (fast, available) |
| Dementia subtype, MCI progression | **MRI** (medial temporal or regional atrophy); **FDG-PET or HMPAO-SPECT** (temporoparietal vs frontal) |
| Vascular cognitive impairment, late-life depression | **MRI T2/FLAIR** (WMH grade), **T2\*** (microbleeds) |
| Variant CJD | **MRI**: pulvinar and hockey-stick signs |
| Limbic encephalitis, hippocampal sclerosis | **MRI FLAIR** |
| Parkinsonism vs essential tremor | **¹⁸F-dopa PET / DAT-SPECT** |
| Pre-surgical epilepsy work-up | MRI, **fMRI language lateralisation**, FDG-PET |
| Alcohol, steroids, anorexia nervosa | Atrophy may be **reversible**; interpret with caution |

**Conclusion.** Neuroimaging in psychiatry has two roles: clinical exclusion and diagnosis of organic disorder, and research into pathophysiology.
- **CT** is the quick screen for mass lesions, haemorrhage and trauma.
- **MRI** is superior for dementia (medial temporal atrophy), white matter disease, small infarcts, epilepsy, infection and signs such as the vCJD pulvinar sign. It has no radiation but has contraindications (metal, pacemakers, claustrophobia).
- **Functional imaging** characterises dementia subtypes and parkinsonism and maps receptor occupancy by antipsychotics: **FDG-PET** and **HMPAO-SPECT** for metabolism and perfusion; **dopaminergic PET or DAT-SPECT** for dopamine function.
- **MRS and fMRI** remain mainly research tools, apart from fMRI language lateralisation.

Interpretation requires **age norms** (ventricular enlargement and WMH are common in healthy elderly people). Few tests have established sensitivity and specificity, so imaging must be guided by clinical questions.
