## Exam answer

**Introduction.** **WHO definition:** "a single or repeated act, or lack of appropriate action, occurring within any relationship where there is an **expectation of trust**, which causes harm or distress to an older person" [1].
- India defines a **senior citizen** as aged **60 or over** (Maintenance and Welfare of Parents and Senior Citizens Act, MWPSC 2007).
- India had about **104 million** elderly people at Census 2011 (53 million women) [1].

The theme of prevention is that **elder abuse is a social and cultural phenomenon**, so it has to be prevented at societal, family, carer and clinical levels.

### Types [1]
1. **Physical:** beating, manhandling, inappropriate restraint, over- or under-medication.
2. **Emotional/verbal:**
   - insults, intimidation, ageist discrimination, false accusations, isolation;
   - in the Indian setting also nagging, mocking a person with dementia, and **excluding elders from family decisions and ceremonies**.
3. **Financial:**
   - control or theft of pension and property;
   - forcing changes to a will;
   - exploiting elders as childcare.
4. **Sexual.**
5. **Neglect:**
   - withholding food, care, medical treatment or affection;
   - forced eviction or relocation.
6. **Accusations of witchcraft** (stigmatisation and ostracism, especially of widows).
7. **Systemic/institutional abuse:** dehumanising treatment in health facilities and pension offices; marginalisation.
8. **Abandonment** (sometimes listed separately; MWPSC makes it an offence).

### Magnitude
- **HelpAge India 2018** (5,014 elders, 23 cities) [1]:
  - **25%** reported abuse;
  - abusers were mainly **sons (52%)** and **daughters-in-law (34%)**;
  - **82% did not report it**, citing **family honour (52%)** and **lack of awareness (34%)**.
- **WHO global estimate:** about **1 in 6** older people in community settings experience abuse each year (Yon 2017) [general knowledge].

### Risk factors (ecological model) [1]
- **Victim:**
  - female sex; low income; dependency;
  - **cognitive impairment / dementia** (major risk);
  - physical disability;
  - **depression**;
  - **disruptive behaviour** (BPSD);
  - social isolation.
- **Perpetrator / carer:**
  - **substance use**;
  - **carer depression and burden**;
  - long duration of caregiving;
  - financial dependence on the older person;
  - psychiatric illness;
  - history of violence.
- **Relationship:** poor prior relationship; shared living with conflict; **caregiver abused by the patient**, who then retaliates. In Coyne's dementia study, 12% of carers admitted physical abuse and 33% had been abused by the patient [1].
- **Societal:**
  - **erosion of intergenerational bonds** (rural–urban migration, education);
  - loss of elders' traditional roles;
  - ageism; poverty; weak services [1].

### Recognition (indicators) [1]
- **Physical:** unexplained falls, injuries, bruises or burns in unusual sites, restraint marks, malnutrition or dehydration, poor hygiene, "doctor-shopping", repeat or unused prescriptions.
- **Emotional:** fear, withdrawal, resignation, depression, avoiding eye contact with the carer, reluctance to talk.
- **Sexual:** genital injuries or infections; unexplained behaviour change.
- **Financial:**
  - unusual bank withdrawals;
  - changed wills or property titles;
  - missing belongings;
  - **care not commensurate with income**.
- **Carer indicators:**
  - stressed, defensive or hostile;
  - **refuses to let the elder be interviewed alone**;
  - blames the elder (for incontinence);
  - infantilises the elder;
  - substance misuse.

### Prevention strategies
**Primary prevention (stop abuse before it starts):**
1. **Public awareness and anti-ageism campaigns.** World Elder Abuse Awareness Day (15 June). Intergenerational programmes.
2. **Strengthen family and community support:**
   - day care centres; respite care;
   - home-based care (NPHCE);
   - senior citizen associations;
   - social pensions (IGNOAPS).
3. **Caregiver support (the most important for dementia):**
   - **psychoeducation** about BPSD;
   - **skills training** (managing aggression, wandering, incontinence);
   - **respite**;
   - **screening and treating carer depression and burden** (Zarit Burden Interview);
   - support groups (ARDSI) [1].
4. **Financial safeguards:**
   - **money management programmes** [1];
   - joint accounts with safeguards;
   - **advance planning:** wills, power of attorney, **advance directives (MHCA 2017)**.
5. **Training professionals** (doctors, nurses, police, bank staff) to recognise abuse [1].
6. **Legal literacy:** awareness of the **MWPSC Act**, helplines, and maintenance tribunals.

**Secondary prevention (early detection):**
- **Routine enquiry** in geriatric clinics, emergency departments and home visits. Interview the older person **alone**.
- **Screening tools** [1]:
  - **EASI (Elder Abuse Suspicion Index)**;
  - **H-S/EAST (Hwalek–Sengstock)**;
  - **VASS**;
  - **BASE**;
  - Conflict Tactics Scale (modified);
  - OAFEM (financial);
  - OAPAM;
  - E-IOA.
- **Helplines:**
  - the most widely used intervention internationally [1];
  - India: **Elderline 14567** (national, MoSJE) [general knowledge]; HelpAge India helpline; police senior-citizen cells (e.g., Delhi and Mumbai police).

**Tertiary prevention (stop recurrence, limit harm):**
1. **Safety planning; emergency shelters** (old age homes or short-stay homes as safe havens) [1].
2. **Multidisciplinary teams:** health, social work, police, legal aid, financial services, long-term care, proxy decision-making [1].
   - Example: the **Israeli multisystem model** (SUPTEA unit with a social-worker coordinator, paraprofessional home visits, an advisory team, and a court welfare officer) [1].
3. **Interventions for abusive carers:** counselling, substance-use treatment, respite. There is suggestive evidence that this reduces revictimisation [1].
4. **Legal remedies:**
   - **MWPSC Act 2007:**
     - maintenance tribunals (claims up to a ceiling; the 2019 amendment bill proposed removing the cap) [general knowledge; check];
     - **Section 23:** property transfers can be voided if the condition of care is broken;
     - **Section 24:** **abandonment** is punishable (up to 3 months and/or a fine);
     - state old-age homes;
   - **CrPC Section 125** (BNSS Section 144 from 2024): maintenance for parents [1, general knowledge];
   - IPC/BNS for physical and sexual offences;
   - **Protection of Women from Domestic Violence Act 2005** (for older women).
5. **Treat the victim's psychiatric sequelae:** **depression, anxiety, PTSD**. Verbal and emotional abuse are the strongest predictors of distress (Dong) [1].
6. **Build resilience:** **mastery** and **social support** are protective (Chokkanathan) [1].

### Role of the psychiatrist
- **Detect:** ask routinely, especially in dementia, depression and delirium.
- **Assess capacity:** a person **with capacity may refuse intervention**, and that choice must be respected [1]. Without capacity, act in best interests (nominated representative; MHCA).
- **Treat:** BPSD (to reduce carer strain), depression, carer burden.
- **Avoid iatrogenic or "systemic" abuse:** overuse of sedation or restraint; dignity on wards.
- **Document carefully; refer** to social work, police or legal services.
- **Recognise the dilemmas.** Some care decisions feel abusive but may be necessary, such as washing a resistant person with dementia, or locking the door against wandering. **Abuse vs care is a judgement made in context** [1].

### Indian barriers to help-seeking (Chokkanathan, Chennai) [1]
- **Service:** low awareness; distrust of services.
- **Spiritual:** **karma** ("written on my head"); concern for **moksha** (the son must perform funeral rites).
- **Family:** fear of eviction; fear of the son's arrest; protecting family "happiness".
- **Individual:** economic dependency; self-blame.

**Conclusion.** Elder abuse is common (about 1 in 4 urban Indian elders), mostly **intra-familial** and **hidden** (82% unreported). Prevention must be **multi-level**:
- societal attitude change and social security;
- **caregiver support** (especially in dementia);
- routine screening and helplines;
- legal protection under the MWPSC Act;
- coordinated multidisciplinary responses.

Throughout, it must respect the older person's **capacity and autonomy** and the cultural forces that silence victims.

## In depth

### Background
- Elder abuse was first described as "granny battering" (Baker 1975; Burston 1977) [general knowledge].
- **Toronto Declaration on the Global Prevention of Elder Abuse** (WHO/INPEA, 2002).
- **International Plan of Action on Ageing** (Madrid, 2002).
- **India:**
  - National Policy on Older Persons (1999);
  - MWPSC Act (2007);
  - **National Policy on Senior Citizens** (2011 draft);
  - **NPHCE** (2010–11).

### Epidemiology
- **Global (Yon 2017, *Lancet Glob Health*):**
  - **15.7%** overall;
  - psychological 11.6%;
  - financial 6.8%;
  - neglect 4.2%;
  - physical 2.6%;
  - sexual 0.9%.

  Rates are higher in institutions (about 64% of staff admit abuse; WHO 2022) [general knowledge].
- **India:**
  - LASI found about 5% self-reported abuse in the previous year (probably an underestimate) [general knowledge; check];
  - HelpAge 2018: 25% [1];
  - Shivamogga community study [1].

### Classification
- By type (above); by setting (domestic, institutional, self-neglect); intentional vs unintentional (carer stress).
- **Self-neglect** is counted in some systems (US Adult Protective Services).

### Mechanism: theoretical models
- **Caregiver stress model:** burden leads to frustration, which leads to abuse. Supported by the dementia data [1].
- **Social learning / intergenerational transmission** of violence.
- **Dependency theory:** perpetrator dependent on victim (financial, housing).
- **Social exchange theory:** imbalance of power and resources.
- **Ecological model:** individual, relationship, community and societal factors (WHO).

### Clinical features: psychiatric consequences [1]
- **Depression** (abuse is a strong predictor), anxiety, PTSD, psychological distress.
- Increased mortality: about 3× in abused elders (Lachs 1998, *JAMA*) [general knowledge].
- Hospitalisation; nursing home placement.

### Assessment
- Interview **alone**; use open, non-judgemental questions.
- Physical examination with documentation (body maps, photographs with consent).
- Cognitive assessment and **capacity** for specific decisions.
- **Carer assessment:** burden, depression, substance use, knowledge of the illness.
- **Risk assessment:** immediacy, severity, wishes of the victim.

### Differential: abuse vs other explanations
- Falls from frailty, osteoporosis, anticoagulant bruising.
- Dementia-related behaviours.
- Delusional accusations: paranoid delusions in dementia or late-onset psychosis can include false allegations. This is illustrated by the user's Scenario 3, where a delusion of infidelity sat alongside a genuinely exhausted 90-year-old carer [1].
- Self-neglect from depression.
- Appropriate care that the older person resents (e.g., placement after falls; Scenario 1 shows the family's perspective) [1].

### Management: evidence for interventions
| Intervention | Evidence [1, general knowledge] |
| --- | --- |
| Caregiver interventions (psychoeducation, respite, skills) | Suggestive; may prevent revictimisation |
| Money management programmes | Case-study evidence |
| Helplines | Widely used; case-study evidence of early intervention |
| Emergency shelters | Model from domestic violence services |
| Multidisciplinary teams | Consensus best practice |
| Screening | No RCT evidence that screening improves outcomes (US Preventive Services Task Force: "insufficient evidence") |

### Evidence table
| Study | Finding |
| --- | --- |
| HelpAge India 2018 | 25% abused; son 52%, daughter-in-law 34%; 82% unreported [1] |
| Coyne 1993, *Am J Psychiatry* | Dementia carers: 12% physically abusive; 33% abused by patient [1] |
| Dong systematic review | Depression, dementia and substance use increase vulnerability; verbal abuse most distressing [1] |
| Chokkanathan (Chennai) | Service, spiritual, family and individual barriers [1] |
| Yon 2017, *Lancet Glob Health* (general knowledge) | Global prevalence 15.7% |

### Critical appraisal
- **Definitions vary**, especially for emotional abuse and neglect, which are culture-dependent. Comparisons across studies are unreliable.
- **Under-reporting** is severe, so all prevalence figures are minimums.
- **Few RCTs** of prevention exist; most evidence comes from case studies [1].
- **Tension between autonomy and protection:** paternalistic interventions can themselves be experienced as abuse.
- **Source note:** the user's slides are a NIMHANS seminar. Quantitative claims trace to HelpAge India and published studies.

### Indian context
- **Programmes and laws:**
  - **MWPSC Act 2007** (and the 2019 amendment bill);
  - **National Action Plan for Senior Citizens (NAPSrC)**;
  - **Elderline 14567**;
  - **NPHCE**;
  - **Ayushman Bharat PM-JAY** (extended to everyone over 70 in 2024) [general knowledge; check];
  - **MHCA 2017** (rights of persons with mental illness, advance directives, nominated representatives).
- **Institutional care:**
  - old age homes are increasingly common;
  - quality regulation is weak;
  - rights violations exist in some institutions [Likely].
- **Cultural factors:**
  - filial piety (*seva*) is protective, but its erosion and joint-family conflict create risk;
  - widows face particular vulnerability (e.g., Vrindavan).

### Recent advances
- **WHO Decade of Healthy Ageing (2021–2030)**, which prioritises combating ageism.
- **Financial technology safeguards:** bank alerts for unusual transactions on senior accounts.
- **Telemedicine and community health worker home visits** for detection.
