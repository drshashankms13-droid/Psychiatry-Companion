## Exam answer

**Introduction.**
- **What they are:** prion diseases (transmissible spongiform encephalopathies, TSEs) are rapidly progressive neurodegenerative disorders. Pathology shows **spongiform vacuolation, astrocytosis, neuronal loss and accumulation of an abnormal, protease-resistant, self-propagating prion protein (PrPSc)** [1].
- **Human forms:**
  - **Creutzfeldt–Jakob disease (CJD)**: sporadic, variant, familial, iatrogenic;
  - **Gerstmann–Sträussler–Scheinker disease (GSS)**;
  - **fatal familial insomnia (FFI)**;
  - **kuru** (ritual cannibalism, Papua New Guinea; Gajdusek).

### Prion biology [1]
- **Prion-only hypothesis (Prusiner):** disease transmitted by **protein alone**.
- **Normal PrPc:**
  - neuronal; α-helical;
  - roles in copper metabolism, oxidative balance, neuroprotection.
- **PrPSc:**
  - over 40% **β-pleated sheet**; insoluble; **protease-resistant**;
  - **converts PrPc to PrPSc** (template-directed: infectivity).
- **Triggers:**
  - exogenous PrPSc (iatrogenic, vCJD);
  - a random event (sporadic);
  - a **PRNP mutation** increasing conversion (familial CJD, GSS, FFI).
- **Predictions confirmed:**
  - species barrier;
  - **PrP-knockout mice are resistant**;
  - transgenic host genes alter incubation.
- **Neurotoxicity mechanism unclear:** not PrPc loss, nor PrPSc accumulation alone; possibly abnormal intracellular localisation.

**PRNP gene:**
- **Familial disease:** over 30 mutations (insertions, point mutations, stop codons); autosomal dominant.
- **Codon 129 polymorphism (M/V):**
  - **homozygosity** increases risk of sporadic and iatrogenic CJD;
  - **all clinical vCJD cases have been 129MM**;
  - MM homozygotes show the classical sporadic phenotype; other genotypes show slower course, no myoclonus, atypical EEG.
- **D178N mutation:** FFI with 129M; CJD with 129V.

### Types of CJD [1]

| Type | Epidemiology | Key features |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Sporadic (sCJD)** | Under 1 per million per year (6% familial in a French series). Onset usually 5th–6th decade | See clinical features below; most die **within 2 years** |
| **Variant (vCJD)** | First described in **1996** (UK). Linked to **BSE** (cattle epidemic peaked 1992) by strain typing. 165 UK cases by March 2007. **All 129MM** | **Young adults**; course "more rapid" than sCJD per Lishman (⚠️ reviewer: this conflicts with my general knowledge; no uploaded source resolves it). **Psychiatric onset**: depression, anxiety, behavioural change. Then **cerebellar ataxia and myoclonus**, then cognitive decline, then prolonged **akinetic mutism**. **MRI pulvinar sign** (bilateral). Pathology: **florid plaques** (amyloid surrounded by vacuoles). PrP in the **lymphoreticular system** (appendix studies), hence a transfusion risk |
| **Familial** | PRNP mutations (autosomal dominant). E200K: rapidly progressive dementia with myoclonus in the late 50s. Octapeptide-repeat insertions: highly variable (onset 21–82) and early neuropsychiatric or personality change | — |
| **Iatrogenic** | **Pituitary-derived growth hormone and gonadotrophins**, **dural and corneal grafts**, neurosurgical instruments; possibly **blood transfusion** (vCJD) | Slightly different presentation |

### Clinical features of sporadic CJD [1]
1. **Prodrome** (weeks to months):
   - **"neurasthenic"**: fatigue, insomnia, anxiety, depression;
   - mental slowing; unpredictable behaviour;
   - occasionally elevation;
   - early memory and concentration problems; vague weakness, unsteady gait.
   - **Often diagnosed as a functional psychiatric disorder**; early remissions add to this.
   - **Psychiatric symptoms in 80% in the first 3–4 months** (sleep disturbance, anxiety, psychosis) (Wall 2005).
2. **Rapid dementia with neurological signs:**
   - **myoclonus** (almost invariable);
   - **cerebellar ataxia**; pyramidal spasticity; **extrapyramidal** rigidity, tremor, chorea or athetosis;
   - **anterior horn cell** involvement (ALS-like wasting);
   - dysphasia, dysarthria; **parietal signs** (right–left disorientation, acalculia, finger agnosia);
   - rapidly progressive **cortical blindness**;
   - nystagmus, pseudobulbar laughing and crying; seizures;
   - delirium; auditory hallucinations and delusions; confabulation.
3. **Terminal:**
   - profound dementia; **akinetic mutism**;
   - decorticate or decerebrate posture; persistent myoclonus;
   - emaciation, coma; **death usually within 2 years**.

Lishman's case: a 38-year-old with alcohol use and "depression", labelled hysterical pseudodementia (approximate answers). He went on to develop apraxia, myoclonus and choreoathetosis, and died within about 15 months.

### Investigations [1]
- **EEG:**
  - almost always abnormal;
  - early diffuse slowing;
  - later **periodic synchronous triphasic sharp-wave complexes at 1–2 Hz** on a suppressed background, often with myoclonus;
  - usually **late** in sCJD; atypical in some genotypes and in vCJD.
- **MRI:**
  - **diffusion-weighted imaging**: high sensitivity and specificity; cortical/grey matter signal (predicts shorter survival);
  - **pulvinar sign** in **vCJD**.
- **CT:** often normal or mild atrophy, even when advanced.
- **CSF:** **14-3-3 protein** and **tau** raised (modest sensitivity and specificity).
- **Lymphoreticular tissue** (vCJD): PrP accumulation (appendix and transfusion studies).
- **Genetics:** PRNP sequencing and codon 129 status.
- **Neuropathology:**
  - **spongiform change (status spongiosus)**, astrocytosis, neuronal loss;
  - **no inflammation**;
  - **PrP immunocytochemistry**;
  - kuru-type or florid plaques in some.

### Other human TSEs [1]
- **GSS:**
  - PRNP mutations (e.g., codon 102); 1–10 per 10 million;
  - onset in the 4th–5th decade;
  - **progressive ataxia**, extrapyramidal signs, dysarthria, then dementia;
  - **duration about 7 years**;
  - PrP plaques plus tangles.
- **FFI:**
  - D178N with 129M;
  - **intractable insomnia**, loss of circadian rhythms, **dysautonomia** (hypertension, loss of nocturnal BP dip), motor signs;
  - hypercortisolism, raised catecholamines;
  - **thalamic degeneration**.

### Management [1]
- **The source describes no specific treatment for CJD.** The general dementia management principles in Chapter 9 apply: diagnosis and information, physical care, carer support, residential or nursing care.
- **Prevention of transmission** (from the epidemiology section):
  - **iatrogenic transmission** via pituitary hormones, dural and corneal grafts and neurosurgical procedures;
  - concern about **blood and other non-neural tissue** because of lymphoreticular PrP (vCJD).
- **Public health:**
  - **national surveillance** (UK National CJD Surveillance Unit);
  - **bans on ruminant protein in animal feed**, which nearly eliminated BSE.

**Conclusion.** CJD is the commonest human prion disease: a **rapidly progressive dementia with myoclonus, ataxia and visual or pyramidal signs**, often with a **psychiatric prodrome**, causing death within months to 2 years.
- **Variant CJD** (BSE-linked; young, psychiatric onset, ataxia, pulvinar sign, 129MM) is distinct.
- **Familial** (PRNP) and **iatrogenic** forms exist.
- **Diagnosis:** periodic EEG complexes, DWI-MRI, CSF 14-3-3/tau, and neuropathology.
- **Management:** supportive dementia care; prevention of iatrogenic transmission; surveillance.
