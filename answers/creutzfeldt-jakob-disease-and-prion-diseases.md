## Exam answer

**Introduction.**
- **What they are:** prion diseases (transmissible spongiform encephalopathies, TSEs) are rapidly progressive neurodegenerative disorders. Pathology shows **spongiform vacuolation, astrocytosis, neuronal loss and accumulation of an abnormal, protease-resistant, self-propagating prion protein (PrPSc)** [1].
- **Human forms:**
  - **Creutzfeldt–Jakob disease (CJD)**: sporadic, variant, familial, iatrogenic;
  - **Gerstmann–Sträussler–Scheinker disease (GSS)**;
  - **fatal familial insomnia (FFI)**;
  - **kuru** (ritual cannibalism, Papua New Guinea; Gajdusek).

### Prion biology [1]
- **Prion-only hypothesis (Prusiner):** disease transmitted by **protein alone**.
- **Normal PrPc:**
  - neuronal; α-helical;
  - roles in copper metabolism, oxidative balance, neuroprotection.
- **PrPSc:**
  - over 40% **β-pleated sheet**; insoluble; **protease-resistant**;
  - **converts PrPc to PrPSc** (template-directed: infectivity).
- **Triggers:**
  - exogenous PrPSc (iatrogenic, vCJD);
  - a random event (sporadic);
  - a **PRNP mutation** increasing conversion (familial CJD, GSS, FFI).
- **Predictions confirmed:**
  - species barrier;
  - **PrP-knockout mice are resistant**;
  - transgenic host genes alter incubation.
- **Neurotoxicity mechanism unclear:** not PrPc loss, nor PrPSc accumulation alone; possibly abnormal intracellular localisation.

**PRNP gene:**
- **Familial disease:** over 30 mutations (insertions, point mutations, stop codons); autosomal dominant.
- **Codon 129 polymorphism (M/V):**
  - **homozygosity** increases risk of sporadic and iatrogenic CJD;
  - **all clinical vCJD cases have been 129MM**;
  - MM homozygotes show the classical sporadic phenotype; other genotypes show slower course, no myoclonus, atypical EEG.
- **D178N mutation:** FFI with 129M; CJD with 129V.

### Types of CJD [1]

| Type | Epidemiology | Key features |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Sporadic (sCJD)** | Under 1 per million per year (6% familial in a French series). Onset usually 5th–6th decade | See clinical features below; most die **within 2 years** |
| **Variant (vCJD)** | First described in **1996** (UK). Linked to **BSE** (cattle epidemic peaked 1992) by strain typing. 165 UK cases by March 2007. **All 129MM** | **Young adults**; Lishman says the course is "more rapid", probably relative to other dementias. Its **median duration (about 14 months) is longer than sCJD's (about 4–6 months)** [general knowledge; see In depth]. **Psychiatric onset**: depression, anxiety, behavioural change. Then **cerebellar ataxia and myoclonus**, then cognitive decline, then prolonged **akinetic mutism**. **MRI pulvinar sign** (bilateral). Pathology: **florid plaques** (amyloid surrounded by vacuoles). PrP in the **lymphoreticular system** (appendix studies), hence a transfusion risk |
| **Familial** | PRNP mutations (autosomal dominant). E200K: rapidly progressive dementia with myoclonus in the late 50s. Octapeptide-repeat insertions: highly variable (onset 21–82) and early neuropsychiatric or personality change | — |
| **Iatrogenic** | **Pituitary-derived growth hormone and gonadotrophins**, **dural and corneal grafts**, neurosurgical instruments; possibly **blood transfusion** (vCJD) | Slightly different presentation |

### Clinical features of sporadic CJD [1]
1. **Prodrome** (weeks to months):
   - **"neurasthenic"**: fatigue, insomnia, anxiety, depression;
   - mental slowing; unpredictable behaviour;
   - occasionally elevation;
   - early memory and concentration problems; vague weakness, unsteady gait.
   - **Often diagnosed as a functional psychiatric disorder**; early remissions add to this.
   - **Psychiatric symptoms in 80% in the first 3–4 months** (sleep disturbance, anxiety, psychosis) (Wall 2005).
2. **Rapid dementia with neurological signs:**
   - **myoclonus** (almost invariable);
   - **cerebellar ataxia**; pyramidal spasticity; **extrapyramidal** rigidity, tremor, chorea or athetosis;
   - **anterior horn cell** involvement (ALS-like wasting);
   - dysphasia, dysarthria; **parietal signs** (right–left disorientation, acalculia, finger agnosia);
   - rapidly progressive **cortical blindness**;
   - nystagmus, pseudobulbar laughing and crying; seizures;
   - delirium; auditory hallucinations and delusions; confabulation.
3. **Terminal:**
   - profound dementia; **akinetic mutism**;
   - decorticate or decerebrate posture; persistent myoclonus;
   - emaciation, coma; **death usually within 2 years**.

Lishman's case: a 38-year-old with alcohol use and "depression", labelled hysterical pseudodementia (approximate answers). He went on to develop apraxia, myoclonus and choreoathetosis, and died within about 15 months.

### Investigations [1]
- **EEG:**
  - almost always abnormal;
  - early diffuse slowing;
  - later **periodic synchronous triphasic sharp-wave complexes at 1–2 Hz** on a suppressed background, often with myoclonus;
  - usually **late** in sCJD; atypical in some genotypes and in vCJD.
- **MRI:**
  - **diffusion-weighted imaging**: high sensitivity and specificity; cortical/grey matter signal (predicts shorter survival);
  - **pulvinar sign** in **vCJD**.
- **CT:** often normal or mild atrophy, even when advanced.
- **CSF:** **14-3-3 protein** and **tau** raised (modest sensitivity and specificity).
- **Lymphoreticular tissue** (vCJD): PrP accumulation (appendix and transfusion studies).
- **Genetics:** PRNP sequencing and codon 129 status.
- **Neuropathology:**
  - **spongiform change (status spongiosus)**, astrocytosis, neuronal loss;
  - **no inflammation**;
  - **PrP immunocytochemistry**;
  - kuru-type or florid plaques in some.

### Other human TSEs [1]
- **GSS:**
  - PRNP mutations (e.g., codon 102); 1–10 per 10 million;
  - onset in the 4th–5th decade;
  - **progressive ataxia**, extrapyramidal signs, dysarthria, then dementia;
  - **duration about 7 years**;
  - PrP plaques plus tangles.
- **FFI:**
  - D178N with 129M;
  - **intractable insomnia**, loss of circadian rhythms, **dysautonomia** (hypertension, loss of nocturnal BP dip), motor signs;
  - hypercortisolism, raised catecholamines;
  - **thalamic degeneration**.

### Management [1]
- **The source describes no specific treatment for CJD.** The general dementia management principles in Chapter 9 apply: diagnosis and information, physical care, carer support, residential or nursing care.
- **Prevention of transmission** (from the epidemiology section):
  - **iatrogenic transmission** via pituitary hormones, dural and corneal grafts and neurosurgical procedures;
  - concern about **blood and other non-neural tissue** because of lymphoreticular PrP (vCJD).
- **Public health:**
  - **national surveillance** (UK National CJD Surveillance Unit);
  - **bans on ruminant protein in animal feed**, which nearly eliminated BSE.

**Conclusion.** CJD is the commonest human prion disease: a **rapidly progressive dementia with myoclonus, ataxia and visual or pyramidal signs**, often with a **psychiatric prodrome**, causing death within months to 2 years.
- **Variant CJD** (BSE-linked; young, psychiatric onset, ataxia, pulvinar sign, 129MM) is distinct.
- **Familial** (PRNP) and **iatrogenic** forms exist.
- **Diagnosis:** periodic EEG complexes, DWI-MRI, CSF 14-3-3/tau, and neuropathology.
- **Management:** supportive dementia care; prevention of iatrogenic transmission; surveillance.

## In depth

### Background
- **Creutzfeldt (1920)** and **Jakob (1921)** described the eponymous cases. Some of the original cases would not meet modern criteria [general knowledge].
- **Scrapie** (sheep) was described in the 19th century; its transmissibility was shown in the 1930s [1].
- **Kuru** (Fore people, Papua New Guinea): **Gajdusek** showed that kuru, and then CJD, were **transmissible to primates** (Nobel Prize 1976) [1; general knowledge for the Nobel].
- **Prusiner (1982)** proposed the **"prion" (proteinaceous infectious particle)**, a protein-only challenge to the central dogma (Nobel Prize 1997) [1; general knowledge for the Nobel].
- **BSE epidemic (UK; peak 1992; more than 180,000 cattle)**, then **variant CJD (1996)**. BSE was linked by strain typing in mice and by biochemistry [1].

### Epidemiology [1; general knowledge for updated figures]
- **All human prion disease:** about **1–2 per million per year** worldwide [general knowledge]. In France to 1982, under 1 per million, of which 6% were familial [1].
- **Proportions** [general knowledge]:
  - **sporadic about 85%**;
  - **genetic 10–15%**;
  - **acquired** (iatrogenic, vCJD, kuru) under 1%.
- **vCJD:** 165 UK cases by March 2007 [1]. **178 UK and about 232 worldwide** by 2020 [general knowledge]. Very few new cases since 2016. **All clinical cases were 129MM until 2016**, when a **129MV** case was reported [1; general knowledge for 2016].
- **Iatrogenic CJD** [1; general knowledge for numbers]:
  - pituitary-derived **growth hormone** (about 230 cases worldwide);
  - **dura mater grafts** (about 230 cases, especially in **Japan**);
  - corneal grafts;
  - neurosurgical instruments;
  - EEG depth electrodes;
  - **blood transfusion** (vCJD: 3–4 cases) [1].
  - Recent evidence suggests **iatrogenic Aβ transmission** from cadaveric growth hormone (2024) [general knowledge].
- **Clusters** reflect chance, familial disease or unexplained environmental exposure [1].
- **Chronic wasting disease** in deer (up to 10% of farmed animals) has not been shown to cross into humans [1].
- **Carrier concern:** abnormal PrP was found in the appendices of asymptomatic **129VV** individuals. A **129MV** transfusion recipient had PrP accumulation. This raises a possible "**second wave**" [1].

### Classification
**Human prion diseases [1; general knowledge for VPSPr and sFI]:**
| Category | Disease | Genetics / route |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Sporadic** | sCJD; **sporadic fatal insomnia (sFI)**; **variably protease-sensitive prionopathy (VPSPr)** | Spontaneous misfolding; codon 129 modulates |
| **Genetic** | **Familial CJD** (E200K, V210I, octapeptide-repeat insertions); **GSS** (P102L); **FFI** (D178N-129M) | *PRNP* on **chromosome 20p** [general knowledge]; autosomal dominant [1] |
| **Acquired** | **Kuru**; **iatrogenic CJD**; **vCJD** (BSE) | Exogenous PrPSc [1] |

**sCJD molecular subtypes (Parchi 1999)** [general knowledge]:
- defined by **codon 129 genotype** (MM, MV, VV) and **PrPSc type** (1 or 2, by protease-fragment size);
| Subtype | Share | Phenotype | Duration |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **MM1 / MV1** | about 70% | **Classic**: rapid dementia, myoclonus, **periodic EEG**, visual signs (Heidenhain) | About 4 months |
| **VV2** | about 15% | **Ataxic onset**; dementia late; EEG often non-periodic | About 6 months |
| **MV2** | about 9% | **Kuru plaques**; ataxia, psychiatric features, longer course | About 17 months |
| **MM2-cortical** | about 2% | Dementia, aphasia; long course | About 15 months |
| **MM2-thalamic (sFI)** | about 2% | **Insomnia, dysautonomia**, ataxia | About 16 months |
| **VV1** | about 1% | Young onset; slow dementia | About 15 months |

**Clinical variants of sCJD** [general knowledge; 1 for cortical blindness and cerebellar onset]:
- **Heidenhain**: early visual disturbance, then **cortical blindness** [1];
- **Brownell–Oppenheimer**: cerebellar onset [1];
- amyotrophic (anterior horn cell) [1];
- thalamic;
- **psychiatric-onset**.

### Mechanism in detail [1; general knowledge]
- **PrPC** is a GPI-anchored membrane glycoprotein, α-helical, neuronal. Functions: **copper metabolism, oxidative balance, neuroprotection**, synaptic function [1].
- **PrPSc** is more than 40% **β-sheet**, **protease-resistant**, insoluble, and **recruits PrPC** by templated conversion [1].
- **"Seeding–nucleation" model:** aggregated PrPSc seeds fragment and multiply, giving **exponential spread** [general knowledge].
- **Strains** are distinct PrPSc conformations that encode the phenotype (incubation, lesion profile). This explains MM1 vs VV2 and BSE-to-vCJD identity [1; general knowledge].
- **Species barrier:** depends on sequence homology. **Codon 129** heterozygosity protects by impeding homologous conversion. This is why MM homozygotes are over-represented in sCJD, iatrogenic CJD and vCJD, and why **kuru survivors were enriched for 129MV** [1; general knowledge for kuru].
- **Neurotoxicity:** not loss of PrPC (knockout mice are healthy). Not simply PrPSc accumulation. Candidates are **toxic oligomers** and abnormal localisation; PrPC is **required** for toxicity (knockout mice resist infection) [1].
- **Peripheral pathogenesis (vCJD):** after oral exposure, prions replicate in **gut-associated and lymphoreticular tissue** (tonsil, appendix, spleen), then spread **neuroinvasively** via autonomic nerves. Hence **tonsil biopsy** for diagnosis and **blood-borne transmission** [1; general knowledge for tonsil].

### Clinical features in detail
**sCJD vs vCJD** [1; general knowledge for quantitative data]:
| Feature | sCJD | vCJD |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Median age at onset | **About 65** | **About 26** (range 12–74) |
| Median duration | **About 4–6 months** (90% die within 1 year) | **About 14 months** |
| Presentation | Rapid dementia, myoclonus; psychiatric prodrome in 80% in the first 3–4 months [1] | **Psychiatric** (depression, anxiety, withdrawal, behavioural change) for about 6 months [1] |
| Sensory | Rare | **Persistent painful dysaesthesia** (limbs, face) |
| Neurology | Myoclonus, ataxia, visual and pyramidal or extrapyramidal signs [1] | **Ataxia**, involuntary movements (myoclonus, chorea, dystonia) [1], then dementia, then **akinetic mutism** [1] |
| EEG | **Periodic sharp-wave complexes** in about 65% | Non-specific slowing; PSWCs absent [1] |
| MRI | Cortical ribboning, caudate and putamen DWI hyperintensity | **Pulvinar sign** (posterior thalamus brighter than anterior putamen; about 90%) and **"hockey stick" sign** [1; general knowledge for hockey stick] |
| CSF 14-3-3 | Positive in about 85–90% | About 50% |
| Pathology | Spongiform change; synaptic PrP | **Florid plaques** (amyloid core with surrounding vacuoles) [1]; lymphoreticular PrP |
| **Tonsil biopsy** | Negative | **Positive** |
| Codon 129 | All genotypes (MM over-represented) | MM (one MV case) [1] |

- *Resolution of a flag in the exam answer:* Lishman states vCJD's "course is more rapid" [1]. Surveillance data show vCJD has a **longer** median course (**about 14 months**) than sCJD (**about 4–6 months**) [general knowledge]. Lishman's sentence probably compares vCJD with **other dementias**, not with sCJD.

**Psychiatric features (sCJD)** [1; general knowledge]:
- **80% have psychiatric symptoms in the first 3–4 months** (Wall 2005): sleep disturbance, anxiety, psychosis, depression [1];
- early fluctuation and **remission** lead to "functional" diagnoses [1];
- **Lishman's case**: labelled "hysterical pseudodementia" because of **approximate answers**; CJD was confirmed at autopsy [1];
- visual hallucinations and distortions (Heidenhain); catatonia (reported).

**Genetic prion diseases [1; general knowledge]:**
- **E200K** (the commonest familial CJD worldwide): Libyan Jews, Slovakia, Chile; **sCJD-like phenotype** with rapid dementia and myoclonus in the late 50s [1].
- **Octapeptide repeat insertions**: onset from 21 to 82; early **neuropsychiatric and personality change**; long course; often misdiagnosed as AD or HD [1].
- **GSS (P102L):** onset in the 4th–5th decade; **ataxia** first, then dementia; **average duration about 7 years**; PrP plaques plus tangles [1].
- **FFI (D178N-129M):**
  - intractable **insomnia** and loss of sleep spindles;
  - oneiric stupor and **dream enactment**;
  - **dysautonomia** (hypertension, hyperhidrosis, tachycardia);
  - hypercortisolism;
  - **anterior and medial thalamic degeneration**;
  - **PET thalamic hypometabolism** [1; general knowledge for PET].
  - The same **D178N mutation with 129V produces CJD** [1].

### Assessment
**CDC 2018 / ECDC criteria for sCJD** [general knowledge]:
- **Definite:** neuropathology, immunohistochemistry or Western blot; or scrapie-associated fibrils.
- **Probable:**
  - **(a)** neuropsychiatric disorder **plus** a **positive RT-QuIC** in CSF or other tissue; **or**
  - **(b)** rapidly progressive cognitive impairment **plus at least 2 of**: myoclonus; visual or cerebellar problems; pyramidal or extrapyramidal signs; akinetic mutism. **Plus** a typical EEG, or **positive 14-3-3 with duration under 2 years**, or **typical MRI** (caudate and putamen, or at least 2 cortical regions: temporal, parietal, occipital). **And** routine investigations do not suggest an alternative.
- **Possible:** clinical features with duration under 2 years, without supportive tests.

**vCJD (WHO) criteria** [general knowledge]:
- progressive neuropsychiatric disorder over 6 months;
- no alternative diagnosis or iatrogenic exposure;
- at least 4 of 5: early psychiatric symptoms, persistent painful sensory symptoms, ataxia, involuntary movements, dementia;
- EEG not typical of sCJD;
- **bilateral pulvinar high signal**;
- **positive tonsil biopsy** supports the diagnosis.

**Test performance** [1; general knowledge]:
| Test | Sensitivity / specificity (sCJD) |
| --- | --- |
| **CSF RT-QuIC** | About 92–97% / **about 99–100%** |
| MRI DWI | About 90% / about 95% |
| CSF 14-3-3 | About 85% / about 70–80% (raised in stroke, encephalitis, seizures) [1] |
| CSF total tau (>1,150 pg/mL) | About 85% / about 85% [1] |
| EEG PSWCs | About 65% / about 85% (late) [1] |

### Differential diagnosis [1; see `rapidly-progressive-dementia`]
- **Autoimmune encephalitis** (LGI1, NMDAR, IgLON5), **Hashimoto encephalopathy**.
- **Rapid AD; DLB** (myoclonus and fluctuations); FTD-MND; CBD.
- **Non-convulsive status epilepticus**; **Wernicke**; **lithium or bismuth toxicity** (myoclonus, periodic EEG).
- **CNS lymphoma**; paraneoplastic encephalitis.
- **Neurosyphilis**; HIV; PML; **SSPE** (periodic EEG; young; measles history).
- **vCJD in the young:** **depression, conduct disorder, drug misuse, Wilson's disease, HD, metachromatic leucodystrophy** [general knowledge].
- **Functional disorders:** the CJD prodrome is often misdiagnosed as depression or "hysterical pseudodementia" [1].

### Management details [1; general knowledge]
- **No disease-modifying treatment.** Trials have failed [general knowledge]:
  - **quinacrine** (PRION-1, 2009; CJD-QUIN);
  - **doxycycline** (2014 RCT);
  - **pentosan polysulphate** (intraventricular; no clear benefit);
  - **PRN100** (anti-PrP antibody): early compassionate use, 2022;
  - **ION717** (PRNP antisense): in trials.
- **Symptomatic and palliative care:**
  - **myoclonus**: clonazepam, levetiracetam, valproate;
  - agitation and psychosis: low-dose antipsychotics (quetiapine) or benzodiazepines;
  - depression: SSRIs;
  - insomnia;
  - **nutrition** (PEG decisions in advance);
  - pressure care; **hospice**.
- **Psychiatric liaison:** support the family through a **rapid, terminal course**; discuss **genetic implications** (sequence PRNP in all cases); predictive testing for relatives (HD-type protocol).
- **Infection control** [1; general knowledge]:
  - **standard precautions** suffice for routine care (not transmitted by casual contact);
  - **instrument quarantine** and single-use instruments for high-risk tissue (brain, spinal cord, eye, and lymphoreticular tissue in vCJD);
  - prions **resist standard sterilisation**: use **NaOH or sodium hypochlorite** with **autoclaving at 134 °C** (extended cycles).
- **Public health** [1]:
  - **notification** to the national surveillance unit;
  - **blood donation exclusion** for at-risk individuals (transfusion recipients after 1980 in the UK);
  - leucodepletion of blood;
  - **feed bans**, which nearly eliminated BSE [1].

### Critical appraisal
- **14-3-3** is a marker of **rapid neuronal destruction**, not of prions [1].
- **vCJD duration discrepancy:** see above. Lishman's statement is best read as relative to other dementias.
- **Under-ascertainment** in countries without surveillance (including India) [general knowledge].
- **Lishman (2009)** predates RT-QuIC, the 2018 criteria and the first 129MV clinical vCJD case.
- **Codon 129 and susceptibility:** a possible **second wave** in MV or VV individuals with longer incubation remains unconfirmed but concerning [1].

### Indian context [general knowledge]
- **Case reports and series** come mainly from **NIMHANS** (sCJD; mostly MM1-like phenotype) and AIIMS. Incidence appears lower, but this probably reflects **under-diagnosis** (limited MRI DWI interpretation, no routine RT-QuIC, low autopsy rates).
- **No national CJD surveillance programme.** Notification and instrument-decontamination protocols vary.
- **vCJD** has not been reported from India.
- **Differential considerations specific to India:** **SSPE** (periodic EEG and myoclonus in adolescents), **neurosyphilis**, TB meningitis, autoimmune encephalitis.

### Recent advances [general knowledge]
- **RT-QuIC on olfactory mucosa brushings** and **skin**, which avoids lumbar puncture.
- **PRNP-lowering antisense oligonucleotides** (ION717, PrProfile trial) and gene-silencing approaches for genetic prion disease.
- **"Prion-like" spread** concepts now extend to tau, α-synuclein and Aβ. **Iatrogenic AD** (2024) has implications for decontamination.
- **Biomarker-based staging** (plasma NfL, GFAP) for pre-symptomatic genetic carriers (E200K, P102L, D178N).
