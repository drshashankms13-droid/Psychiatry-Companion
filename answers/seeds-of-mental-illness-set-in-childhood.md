## Exam answer

**Introduction and definition.** The statement claims that **the origins of most adult mental illness lie in early life**: in genetic and prenatal vulnerability, early brain development, caregiving and childhood adversity. Two related framings appear in examinations:
- "Early adverse childhood experiences have long-term consequences."
- "How does early life stress impact vulnerability to mental illness in later life?"

This essay argues that the statement is **largely true but incomplete**. Childhood plants the seeds of risk, but whether they germinate depends on later "soil": adolescent and adult stress, substances, illness, social conditions and resilience [1].

### Theoretical framework
- **Developmental psychopathology** (Cicchetti, Rogosch): disorders emerge over time from interactions between biological vulnerability and environment.
  - **Multifinality:** one adversity can lead to many outcomes.
  - **Equifinality:** many paths lead to one disorder [1].
- **Diathesis–stress and "two-hit" models:** an early "hit" (genes, prenatal or early adversity) is followed by a later "hit" (adolescent cannabis use, adult stress).
- **Developmental origins of health and disease** (Barker): early environment programmes later physiology.
- **Social determinants** (Marmot): early life is one of the ten "Solid Facts". The social gradient in health starts in childhood and accumulates [2].

### Arguments FOR the statement

| Line of evidence | Key findings |
| --- | --- |
| **Age of onset** | Half of lifetime disorders start by about age 14 and three-quarters by about 24 (Kessler 2005). In the Dunedin cohort, about 74% of adults with a disorder had a diagnosis before 18 and about 50% before 15 (Kim-Cohen 2003) [3, 4] |
| **Genetic and prenatal vulnerability** | High heritability: schizophrenia ~80%, bipolar ~70%, ADHD ~76%. Maternal depression or stress in pregnancy alters the fetal HPA axis. Prenatal famine (Dutch Hunger Winter), infection and obstetric complications raise schizophrenia risk [1] |
| **Neurodevelopmental continuity** | Schizophrenia: premorbid motor, cognitive and social deficits; dysregulated synaptic pruning (neurodevelopmental hypothesis). ADHD → SUD, bipolar, depression; conduct disorder → antisocial PD; ASD → social anxiety, OCD; SLD → depression, low self-esteem [1] |
| **Adverse childhood experiences** | ACE study: dose–response. ≥4 ACEs give about 4× the risk of depression and about 12× the risk of suicide attempts, plus PTSD, SUD and personality disorders [1] |
| **Early life stress biology** | Chronic cortisol → hippocampal and amygdala change; FKBP5/NR3C1 methylation; raised CRP, IL-6 → depression, anxiety, impulsivity. Animal work (Meaney's licking–grooming) shows causal epigenetic programming [1] |
| **Attachment** | Insecure or disorganised attachment → BPD, PTSD, chronic anxiety (Bowlby; Fonagy's mentalisation) [1] |
| **Deprivation experiments** | Bucharest Early Intervention Project: institutional care → brain structural changes and later disorder; earlier foster placement helps [1] |
| **Prospective cohorts** | Dunedin: poor childhood self-control predicts adult anxiety, antisocial behaviour, substance use and poor health. ALSPAC: emotional dysregulation at 7 → depression at 18 [1] |
| **Social environment** | Poverty, urban violence, malnutrition and poor schooling predict conduct disorder, depression, PTSD and somatisation. Health inequalities track early social determinants [1, 2] |

### Arguments AGAINST, or qualifying, the statement
1. **Not all disorders start in childhood.** About half begin after adolescence. Examples: late-onset depression (including **vascular depression**), late-onset psychosis, dementia, post-stroke and other organic disorders, PTSD after adult trauma (war, sexual violence), bereavement, medical illness, substance-induced disorders [1].
2. **Resilience is the rule, not the exception.** Most children exposed to adversity do not develop disorders. In Werner's Kauai cohort, about a third of high-risk children became competent adults. Protective factors include high IQ, social skills, one consistent caregiver or mentor, and education and community support [1].
3. **Methodological problems:**
   - Retrospective recall of childhood is biased by current mood.
   - Prospective and retrospective measures pick out different people.
   - Associations are confounded by shared genes and poverty.
4. **Adult neuroplasticity:** the brain remains modifiable. Treatment, recovery and adult environments change trajectories [1].
5. **Determinism and blame:** the claim can slide into parent-blaming, fatalism and neglect of adult social causes (unemployment, discrimination, violence).
6. **Genes are not "seeds set in childhood".** Inheritance is fixed at conception and often expressed only later. Heritability also varies with age (it rises for cognition and depression in adulthood).

### Clinical and public-health implications
- **Developmental history** in every adult assessment, including ACEs and early milestones [1].
- **Prevention:** parenting education; maternal and perinatal mental health; early childhood development programmes; school mental health; child protection; trauma-informed care [1].
- **Early intervention:** child and adolescent services; first-episode psychosis services.
- **Life-course approach:** address adult risk factors too, such as substance use, vascular risk and social determinants [2].

**Conclusion.** The evidence from age of onset, neurodevelopment, early-adversity biology, attachment and cohort studies strongly supports the claim that **many** of the roots of mental illness lie in childhood. The word "set" overstates it, though. Childhood seeds risk; it does not fix destiny. Germination depends on later experiences, context and resilience, and some disorders arise de novo in adulthood. **Prevention and early intervention in childhood** are the highest-yield investments in population mental health, but adult services and social policy remain essential.

## In depth

### Background
- **Psychoanalysis** (Freud) placed the roots of neurosis in early psychosexual development. Later object-relations theorists (Klein, Winnicott) and **Bowlby's attachment theory** emphasised early relationships.
- **Rutter's** Isle of Wight studies (1960s–70s) and work on maternal deprivation refined Bowlby: it is the quality of care, not separation alone, that matters. Rutter also studied **English and Romanian Adoptees (ERA)**, which showed lasting effects of institutional deprivation beyond 6 months of age.
- **Neurodevelopmental hypothesis of schizophrenia** (Weinberger 1987; Murray & Lewis 1987): an early lesion is expressed after adolescent maturation.
- **ACE study** (Felitti & Anda 1998) and **Dunedin, ALSPAC and E-Risk cohorts** provided population-level and prospective data [1].
- **WHO Commission on Social Determinants of Health** (launched 2005, report 2008): early child development is a priority action area [2].

### Epidemiology

| Data | Finding |
| --- | --- |
| Kessler 2005 (NCS-R) | Median onset: anxiety 11, impulse-control 11, substance 20, mood 30 years; 50% of lifetime cases by 14, 75% by 24 [3] |
| Kim-Cohen 2003 (Dunedin, age 26) | ~74% of adult cases had a diagnosis before 18; ~50% before 15 [4] |
| Solmi 2022 (meta-analysis, 192 studies) | Onset before 14 in ~35%, before 18 in ~48%, before 25 in ~63%; peak age of onset about 14.5 years [5] |
| Marmot 2005 | Life expectancy spread of 48 years between countries and 20 or more years within countries; a social gradient in child and adult mortality [2] |

So **around half of disorders begin after 18**. This is the strongest quantitative counter-argument to "set in childhood."

### Mechanism in detail: biological embedding of early stress
1. **Critical and sensitive periods.** Limbic and prefrontal circuits mature through childhood and adolescence. Stress during these windows changes their developmental trajectory: altered synaptic pruning, delayed myelination, hippocampal growth reduction [1].
2. **HPA axis programming.** Early stress → long-lasting changes in GR expression (NR3C1 methylation) and FKBP5 → impaired cortisol negative feedback → exaggerated stress responses later [1].
3. **Immune programming.** Low-grade inflammation (CRP, IL-6) persists into adulthood and is associated with depression and cardiometabolic disease [1].
4. **Stress sensitisation and kindling.** Each later stressor provokes a larger response, and a smaller stress is needed to trigger depression.
5. **Cognitive and attachment pathways.** Internal working models of self and others; threat-biased attention; reward hyporesponsivity ("latent vulnerability" theory, McCrory & Viding).
6. **Behavioural and social cascades.** School failure → poor employment → poverty → adult stress (the cumulative disadvantage that Marmot describes) [2].

### Clinical features in detail: childhood-to-adult continuities

| Childhood condition | Associated adult outcome |
| --- | --- |
| ADHD | Substance use disorder, bipolar disorder, depression, antisocial behaviour |
| Conduct disorder | Antisocial personality disorder, SUD |
| ASD | Social anxiety, OCD, depression (and psychosis in some) |
| SLD | Depression, low self-esteem, social anxiety |
| Childhood anxiety | Adult anxiety and depression (homotypic and heterotypic continuity) |
| Language delay | Communication and interpersonal difficulties |

Source: [1].

### Assessment in detail
- Developmental history: pregnancy and birth complications, milestones, temperament, school performance, peer relationships.
- **ACE / CTQ / ACE-IQ** for adversity; protective factors (a caring adult, PCEs).
- Use Rutter's multiaxial thinking: syndrome, development, intelligence, medical, psychosocial situation.

### Differential: competing explanations for the childhood–adult link

| Explanation | Implication |
| --- | --- |
| Causal effect of adversity | Prevention of adversity reduces illness |
| Genetic confounding (parents pass on genes and an adverse environment) | Adversity is partly a marker of familial risk |
| Reverse causation (an early-onset disorder elicits harsh parenting) | Child effects shape the environment (evocative rGE) |
| Reporting bias | Depressed adults recall more adversity |
| Cumulative life-course disadvantage | Adult poverty continues the effect |

### Management details: what should follow from the essay
- **Universal prevention:**
  - Nurse home-visiting programmes.
  - Parenting programmes (Incredible Years, Triple P).
  - Early childhood education.
  - Anti-bullying and school mental health programmes.
  - Cash transfers.
- **Selective prevention:** children of parents with mental illness; children in care; children exposed to violence.
- **Indicated prevention:** clinical high-risk-for-psychosis services; early treatment of child anxiety and ADHD.
- **Adult services:** trauma-informed care; treat adult-onset risks (alcohol, cannabis, vascular risk, social isolation).

### Critical appraisal
- **The word "set" is too strong.** Probabilistic risk is not destiny [1].
- **Measurement:** retrospective ACE reports are biased; prospective cohorts are few, mostly from high-income countries, and suffer attrition.
- **Causality:** gene–environment correlation is hard to remove. Genetically informed designs (twins, adoption, polygenic scores) show both genetic confounding and **independent environmental effects**, for example of maltreatment on depression.
- **Specificity:** adversity raises risk for almost all disorders. It is a general vulnerability factor, not a disorder-specific seed.
- **Policy risk:** over-emphasis on childhood can divert attention from adult structural causes (debt, unemployment, caste and gender discrimination) and from treatment of adult-onset disorders [2].
- **Balanced position:** childhood is formative but not deterministic. Many adults recover even after severe early adversity [1].

### Indian context
- **Early childhood:** Integrated Child Development Services (ICDS, anganwadis) and Poshan Abhiyan address malnutrition. Stunting in India remains high (NFHS-5, about 35% of under-5s), and stunting is linked to cognitive outcomes.
- **Child and adolescent burden:** the Bengaluru epidemiological study (Srinath 2005) found 12.5% prevalence of psychiatric disorders in children aged 0–16. NMHS 2015–16 found about 7.3% prevalence in 13–17-year-olds.
- **Programmes and law:** RBSK (screening for developmental delay, the "4 Ds"); RKSK and school health and wellness ambassadors; POCSO 2012; JJ Act 2015; Mission Vatsalya; Tele-MANAS for adolescents.
- **Cultural factors:** joint families can buffer adversity, but corporal punishment, academic pressure, gender discrimination, child marriage and parental alcohol misuse are common adversities.

### Recent advances
- **Established:** life-course and social-determinants frameworks in WHO and national policy [2]; ICD-11 complex PTSD.
- **Emerging:**
  - Polygenic scores × environment.
  - Epigenetic clocks.
  - Normative modelling of brain development to identify deviation early.
  - **Positive childhood experiences** research.
  - Digital phenotyping of adolescent risk.
- **Debate:** precision prevention vs universal (population) approaches. Rose's prevention paradox argues for universal early-years investment.
