## Exam answer

**Definition.** **ECT in children (under 13) and adolescents (13–17)** means modified electroconvulsive therapy given under general anaesthesia and muscle relaxation to minors with severe psychiatric illness. It is a **rarely used, last-resort or life-saving treatment**, given only after careful multidisciplinary assessment and with legal safeguards [1, 2].

**MHCA 2017, Section 95(1)(b) and 95(2):** ECT is **prohibited for minors**, except where, in the opinion of the attending psychiatrist, it is **required**. It may then be performed only after obtaining the **informed consent of the guardian** and **prior permission of the concerned Mental Health Review Board (MHRB)** [1, 2]. **Unmodified ECT is banned for everyone** (Section 95(1)(a)).

### Mechanism
As in adults: electrical stimulus → generalised seizure → neurotransmitter changes (↑ GABA, monoamines), neurotrophic effects (BDNF, hippocampal neurogenesis), normalisation of HPA function, and an anticonvulsant effect with raised seizure threshold. **Children have lower seizure thresholds and longer seizures**, so dosing must be adjusted [1].

### Indications in minors
Evidence comes from **case series and reviews only; there are no RCTs** [1]:
- **Catatonia** (any cause, including in autism spectrum disorder): the strongest indication; often life-saving when lorazepam fails.
- **Severe depression** with psychosis, refusal of food or fluids, or high suicide risk, after medication failure.
- **Mania** (severe, refractory or dangerous).
- **Schizophrenia** (refractory, including clozapine-resistant; catatonic features).
- **Neuroleptic malignant syndrome; severe self-injurious behaviour** in autism or intellectual disability (case series).
- **AACAP (2004) criteria:**
  1. a severe, persistent diagnosis;
  2. severe, disabling or life-threatening symptoms;
  3. lack of response to at least 2 adequate medication trials, unless urgency precludes waiting.

### Pre-ECT assessment
- Two independent psychiatrists' opinions, ideally including a child psychiatrist (AACAP).
- Medical and anaesthetic assessment; neurological examination; **EEG** if there is a seizure history; consider neuroimaging.
- Baseline **cognitive and school-performance assessment**.
- Review drugs that alter seizure threshold (e.g., benzodiazepines, anticonvulsants, theophylline).
- **Consent:** guardian's informed consent plus the child's or adolescent's **assent**. **MHRB permission** in India [1].

### Administration: modifications for children
| Aspect | Adjustment |
| --- | --- |
| Setting | Hospital with paediatric anaesthesia and resuscitation |
| Anaesthesia | Propofol or thiopentone (or ketamine); succinylcholine relaxant; pre-oxygenation |
| Electrode placement | **Bifrontal or right unilateral** preferred initially, for cognition. Bitemporal if urgent |
| Dose | **Lower stimulus doses**: dose titration from low charge (seizure thresholds are lower). Age-based methods have been used |
| Seizure monitoring | EEG + motor. **Prolonged seizures (>120–180 s) are more common**; terminate with IV anaesthetic or benzodiazepine |
| Frequency and number | 2–3 per week; typical course 6–12; review after each session |
| After the course | Monitor for agitation or delirium; cognitive follow-up; relapse prevention (medication ± continuation ECT) |

### Adverse effects
- **Prolonged or tardive seizures** (more likely than in adults) [1].
- Headache, nausea, myalgia, post-ictal agitation, transient confusion.
- Cognitive effects are usually transient. **Long-term cognitive effects have not been studied systematically** [1].
- Anaesthesia risks.

### Key evidence
- **Systematic reviews** (Rey & Walter 1997; Lima 2013; Døssetor 2014; Castaneda-Ramirez 2022) report **high response rates in mood disorders and catatonia**, with lower rates in schizophrenia [4]. Adverse effects are similar to adults, with **no evidence of lasting cognitive harm** in follow-up studies, which are small.
- **Catatonia in autism:** case series show dramatic benefit (Wachtel; Dhossche).

**Conclusion.** ECT in minors is a rarely needed but sometimes **life-saving** treatment, especially for catatonia and severe, psychotic or suicidal depression. It is effective and reasonably safe when technique is adapted (lower doses, bifrontal or RUL placement, close seizure monitoring). In India, MHCA 2017 allows it only with **guardian consent and prior MHRB permission**. This protects children from misuse, but it can dangerously delay urgent treatment. Expedited MHRB review procedures are needed.

## In depth

### Background
- **1938:** Cerletti and Bini introduced ECT [1].
- **1940s:** Heuyer, Bell and Lang treated children with ECT in France. Bender (New York) gave ECT to hundreds of children with "childhood schizophrenia" (1947), with later criticism of the poor outcome data and ethics.
- **Regulation:** several US states (e.g., California, Colorado, Texas) restrict ECT under 12–16 years.
- **AACAP Practice Parameter (2004):** indications and safeguards.
- **India:** MHA 1987 had no specific restriction. MHCA 2017 Section 95 introduced the prohibition with exception [1, 2].

### Epidemiology
- ECT in minors is rare: under 1–2% of all ECT courses in most countries.
- India: small Indian case series (e.g., from NIMHANS and PGIMER) report its use mainly for catatonia and mood disorders. Use has fallen further since 2018 because of the MHRB requirement.

### Classification and criteria
- **AACAP 2004:** the three criteria in the exam answer, plus two independent concurring opinions, and **contraindicated in under-12s only in exceptional circumstances** with special safeguards.
- **Royal College of Psychiatrists:** the same principles, with legal safeguards under the Mental Health Act. Under-12s are rarely treated.
- **MHCA 2017, Section 95:** see the exam answer [1].

### Mechanism in detail: developmental issues
- Seizure threshold rises with age, so children need less charge. Pre-pubertal brains may be more seizure-prone and more plastic.
- **Concern about the developing brain:** animal data on repeated seizures and development are mixed. Human follow-up (e.g., Cohen 2000; Taieb 2002) shows no lasting cognitive or academic decline compared with matched controls, but samples are small.

### Clinical features in detail: catatonia in youth
- Underdiagnosed, especially in autism (12–17% of adolescents and young adults with autism show catatonic features in some series).
- Associated with mood disorders, schizophrenia, autoimmune encephalitis (anti-NMDAR), and medical causes. **Exclude organic causes before ECT.**
- **Lorazepam challenge** (1–2 mg) and scaling (BFCRS, PCRS). ECT if there is no response or a malignant course.

### Assessment in detail
- Standardised severity measures: CDRS-R, YMRS, PANSS, BFCRS.
- Cognitive battery: memory, attention, executive function.
- Family psychoeducation; written information.
- Documentation for the MHRB: diagnosis, severity, failed treatments, urgency, risks and benefits, guardian consent, the minor's assent, and the second opinion.

### Differential: alternatives to consider
| Alternative | Comment |
| --- | --- |
| High-dose lorazepam (catatonia) | First line; ECT if it fails |
| Clozapine (refractory schizophrenia) | Licensed age varies; monitoring |
| Lithium or antipsychotics (mania) | Standard |
| Fluoxetine + CBT (depression) | TADS evidence |
| rTMS (adolescent depression) | Investigational; mixed RCTs |
| Ketamine | Investigational in adolescents |

### Management details
- **Anaesthetic:** paediatric anaesthetist; fasting; IV access; succinylcholine dose by weight; bite block.
- **Stimulus:** start at a low charge (e.g., 5–10% of device maximum or a titration schedule). Adolescents may need near-adult doses.
- **Seizure:** adequate EEG seizure 20–25 s or more. **Terminate seizures lasting over 180 s** (some recommend over 120 s) with propofol, midazolam or diazepam.
- **Medications during the course:** reduce benzodiazepines (they raise threshold), except in catatonia where lorazepam may continue cautiously. Avoid lithium co-administration (delirium).
- **After the course:** maintenance pharmacotherapy; consider continuation ECT if relapse is likely (rarely needed).

### Evidence table
| Study | Design | Result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Rey & Walter 1997 (Am J Psychiatry) | Review, 60 reports, 396 patients | Response: depression 63%, mania 80%, catatonia 80%, schizophrenia 42%; side effects similar to adults |
| Cohen et al. 2000 | Follow-up of adolescents after ECT | No lasting cognitive or academic deficits vs controls |
| Taieb et al. 2002 | Long-term follow-up | Positive views of ECT by patients and parents |
| Døssetor et al. 2014 | Retrospective audit | Effective and safe; prolonged seizures more common |

### Critical appraisal
- **The evidence is weak:** no RCTs; retrospective series; selection and publication bias.
- **Ethics:** children cannot consent. Stigma and history (Bender) make strong safeguards reasonable.
- **MHCA critique:** an outright ban with MHRB exception may be **"sentiment more than science"**. Waiting for board approval in life-threatening catatonia or suicidal depression may amount to withholding treatment. Some suggest **expert-panel or emergency approval routes** [1, 2].
- **Counterpoint:** the MHRB requirement ensures second opinions, documentation and accountability.

### Indian context
- **MHCA 2017, Section 95:** prohibition of ECT in minors, except with guardian consent and **prior MHRB permission**. No ECT as emergency treatment (Section 94) [1].
- **Practice:** MHRB availability varies widely by state, so delays are common. Clinicians should document urgency and seek expedited hearings.
- **IPS guidance on ECT** (clinical practice guidelines) recommends safeguards consistent with AACAP.

### Recent advances
- **Established:** brief-pulse, bifrontal or RUL techniques for youth; recognition of catatonia in autism as an ECT indication.
- **Emerging:** ultrabrief RUL in adolescents; **magnetic seizure therapy** (cognitively sparing; adolescent pilot data); ketamine anaesthesia; EEG-guided dosing.
- **Research:** prospective cognitive follow-up studies; Indian multicentre registry.
