## Exam answer

**Introduction.**
- "Common mental disorders" are mainly **depression and anxiety disorders** (GAD, panic, social anxiety, OCD, PTSD).
- The claim is that SSRIs treat all of them, for everyone. SSRIs are indeed **universally recommended as first-line pharmacological treatment for depression** and are first-line drugs across the anxiety disorders [1]. Whether that makes them a panacea needs testing.

### Arguments FOR (SSRIs are broadly useful)
1. **First-line across the common disorders [1]:**
   - **Depression:** "universally recommended as first-line pharmacological treatment". NICE recommends a **generic SSRI** when an antidepressant is prescribed.
   - **GAD:** SSRIs are first line (fluoxetine and sertraline preferred). Effective treatment of GAD **may prevent major depression**.
   - **Panic disorder:** SSRIs are first line (Cochrane and 2022 meta-analysis).
   - **Social anxiety disorder:** SSRIs are first line.
   - **OCD:** **any SSRI** is first line. Continuing for 2 years halves relapse (25–40% vs 80%).
   - **PTSD:** paroxetine, sertraline or fluoxetine are first-line drugs.
2. **Tolerability and safety:** much **better tolerated than TCAs and MAOIs**, and **less toxic in overdose** than TCAs.
3. **Broad efficacy:**
   - PANDA (2019) supports SSRIs even for **mild–moderate symptoms** below diagnostic thresholds;
   - antidepressants beat placebo in depression with **chronic medical illness**;
   - SSRIs and venlafaxine are relatively better for **depressed mood, suicidality and psychic anxiety**.
4. **Earlier onset than believed:** improvement is fastest in **weeks 1–2**; an effect is usually visible by 2 weeks.
5. **Suicide prevention:** "the most effective way to treat or prevent suicidal thoughts and acts is to treat depression"; for the most part, suicidality is **greatly reduced** by antidepressants.
6. **Other uses:** serotonergic antidepressants treat **premature ejaculation**.

### Arguments AGAINST (not a panacea)
**1. Not first-line for everyone or every severity [1]:**
- **Less severe depression:** antidepressants are **not recommended first line**. Counselling, guided self-help, **CBT**, behavioural activation, mindfulness, IPT, short-term psychodynamic therapy or **exercise** are preferred (NICE).
- **Children and adolescents with mild depression:** antidepressants **should not be prescribed**. Psychological therapy is offered first, alone or combined, in moderate–severe cases. A 2021 Cochrane review called antidepressant effects in young people "**small and unimportant**".
- **PTSD:** "psychological approaches should be used before drug treatments". SSRI support is good but the **effect size is small**.

**2. Limited efficacy in many patients [1]:**
- **30–50%** fail to respond to first- or second-line antidepressants. Treatment-resistant depression has poor outcomes and needs lithium, quetiapine, other augmentation, ECT or ketamine.
- Antidepressants only **modestly improve quality of life and social functioning**.
- **Mirtazapine** ranked second most efficacious in a network meta-analysis, through sleep and appetite effects. Other drugs (**bupropion, agomelatine**) may be the most effective in GAD.
- **OCD** response is slow (10–12 weeks) and needs higher doses. **Clomipramine** or antipsychotic augmentation is often needed.

**3. Ineffective or risky in some conditions [1]:**
- **Bipolar depression:** risk of **cycle acceleration and switching**. Mood stabilisers alone are as effective as mood stabiliser plus antidepressant. There is no consensus on long-term use.
- **Borderline personality disorder:** evidence is **weak**; benefits modest at best. Fluoxetine added to DBT was linked with **more suicide attempts** in one RCT.
- **Anorexia nervosa:** citalopram and sertraline **do not restore weight** and are not recommended.

**4. Adverse effects [1]:**
| Effect | Detail |
|---|---|
| **GI and headache** | Common class effects; diarrhoea more with sertraline |
| **Sexual dysfunction** | Reported with all antidepressants; more with paroxetine |
| **Weight gain** | More with paroxetine |
| **Hyponatraemia** | Highest risk in the first 4 weeks; risk factors include **older age, female sex**, diuretics, NSAIDs, low baseline sodium, renal impairment, hypothyroidism |
| **Bleeding** | Relative risk about 1.4; highest in the first 30 days; worse with NSAIDs or anticoagulants; peptic ulcer risk |
| **Initial anxiety worsening** | In GAD and panic; start at half dose |
| **Suicidality** | Raised risk of suicidal thoughts and acts in **adolescents and young adults** early in treatment and around stopping; the absolute risk is small |
| **Withdrawal** | Weighted average incidence **56%** (range 27–86%), e.g. dizziness, "brain zaps", nausea; worst with short half-life drugs (paroxetine 42–100%) |
| **Drug interactions** | Fluvoxamine (CYP1A2: theophylline); fluoxetine and paroxetine (CYP2D6: clozapine seizures; **tamoxifen failure**) |

**5. Inter-individual variation:** marked, and "not easily predicted". A flexible approach to choosing a drug is needed [1].

**6. They do not replace psychotherapy:** the chapter's focus on drugs "does not imply any lack of confidence" in non-drug treatments, which are first-line alternatives in milder and moderate depression [1].

### Synthesis
- SSRIs are the **most versatile first-line drug class** for the common mental disorders: well tolerated, safe in overdose, effective across depression and anxiety disorders.
- But they are **not a panacea**:
  - they are not first line for **mild depression**, youth depression or PTSD, where psychological treatment comes first;
  - **a third to a half** of patients do not respond;
  - they carry real adverse effects (hyponatraemia, bleeding, sexual dysfunction, withdrawal, early suicidality in the young);
  - they can **harm** in bipolar depression;
  - they are of little use in BPD and anorexia.
- The best care is **matched to diagnosis, severity, age and patient preference**, with psychological therapies and other drugs as needed.

**Conclusion.** "SSRIs are the panacea" is an **overstatement**. SSRIs are a **useful first-line drug class**, not a universal remedy. Their benefits are modest for many patients, they carry risks, and they must sit within a stepped, personalised plan that gives psychological treatment its proper place.

## In depth

### Basic principles of antidepressant prescribing [1]
- Discuss the choice of drug and the availability of non-drug treatments.
- Use an effective dose; assess at **2–4 weeks**.
- Monitor adherence and adverse effects; warn about withdrawal.
- Continue for **at least 6 months** after remission of a single episode; longer for recurrent illness (NICE: at least 2 years if high risk).
- **Withdraw very gradually** (e.g. hyperbolic or exponential tapering).
