## Exam answer

**Introduction.** Selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) — fluoxetine (1987), sertraline, paroxetine, citalopram, escitalopram, fluvoxamine — transformed the treatment of depression and anxiety because they are safer and better tolerated than tricyclics and MAOIs. They are first-line drugs for depression, most anxiety disorders, OCD and PTSD. But a "panacea" is a remedy for all ills; the evidence shows SSRIs are **broadly useful but not universally effective, not always first line, and not free of harm** [1].

### Arguments for — why SSRIs look like a panacea

1. **Broad spectrum of licensed and evidence-based uses** [1]:

| Disorder | SSRI role |
| --- | --- |
| Major depression | **First-line drug** universally recommended; NICE recommends a generic SSRI |
| Generalised anxiety disorder | First line; start at half dose, effect over 6 weeks, continue at least 1 year |
| Panic disorder | First line (benzodiazepines only in crisis); continue at least 8 months |
| Social anxiety disorder | First-line drug (SSRIs, then venlafaxine) — though CBT is first-line treatment |
| OCD | First line at higher doses; response over 10–12 weeks; relapse 25–40% if continued vs 80% if stopped |
| PTSD | First-line drug; higher doses, response by 8–12 weeks |
| Body dysmorphic disorder | Add SSRI to CBT if moderate–severe |
| Others | Bulimia, PMDD, premature ejaculation, emotionalism after stroke (author's knowledge) |

2. **Efficacy:** all antidepressants beat placebo; NNT about **3 vs no treatment** and **5 vs placebo**; with "depressed mood" as the outcome, 91% of SSRI comparisons favour the drug [1].
3. **Safety and tolerability:** far safer in overdose than TCAs; lowest drop-out rates in network meta-analyses (with agomelatine) [1].
4. **Simplicity:** once-daily dosing, little titration, usable in primary care; generics are cheap — important in India.
5. **Wider benefit:** the PANDA trial (2019) supported SSRIs even in people with mild–moderate symptoms below diagnostic thresholds [1].

### Arguments against — why they are not a panacea

**a. Efficacy limits**
- Of moderate–severe depression, about **20% recover untreated, 30% respond to placebo and 50% to antidepressant** — half do not respond [1].
- **30–50%** fail first- or second-line antidepressants (treatment-resistant depression) [1].
- Dual-action drugs (SNRIs, TCAs, mirtazapine) may be more effective; amitriptyline ranked most efficacious in network meta-analysis; SSRIs lead on tolerability, not efficacy [1].
- Only **modest improvement in quality of life and social functioning** [1].
- Little dose–response above the minimum dose for most SSRIs [1].

**b. Not first line everywhere**
- **Mild depression:** psychological therapies, exercise, guided self-help preferred; NICE does not recommend antidepressants first line [1].
- **Social anxiety, OCD, PTSD, panic, insomnia:** CBT or exposure-based therapy is first line or equal [1].
- **Bipolar depression:** SSRI monotherapy is not recommended; switching and rapid cycling risk; only fluoxetine with olanzapine is well supported [1].
- **Psychotic depression:** requires an antipsychotic plus antidepressant, or ECT [1].
- **Children and adolescents:** fluoxetine only modestly effective; suicidality warning.
- **Not effective** for substance use, personality disorders, eating restriction (anorexia), dementia-related agitation (citalopram has some data but QT risk), or primary psychosis.

**c. Adverse effects** [1]
- GI upset, headache, **sexual dysfunction** (high with paroxetine), weight gain (paroxetine), insomnia, **akathisia**, emotional blunting.
- **Hyponatraemia** (elderly), **GI and other bleeding** (with NSAIDs, anticoagulants), QTc (citalopram/escitalopram), falls.
- **Suicidality** and agitation early in treatment in people under 30 [1].
- **Withdrawal (discontinuation) symptoms** can be severe and prolonged — slow, hyperbolic tapering needed [1].
- **Serotonin syndrome** in combinations; drug interactions (fluoxetine, paroxetine, fluvoxamine inhibit CYP enzymes).

**d. Conceptual and social criticisms**
- The **serotonin-deficiency theory** of depression is not supported; SSRIs act through downstream neuroplastic effects (author's knowledge).
- Over-prescription and long-term use without review; medicalisation of normal distress.
- Publication bias and industry influence inflated early estimates (author's knowledge).

### Balanced position

| Better described as | Not |
| --- | --- |
| A safe, broad-spectrum **first-line drug option** for depression and anxiety-spectrum disorders | A cure for all common mental disorders |
| Most effective as part of **stepped care** with psychological therapy | A substitute for psychotherapy, social support or addressing causes |
| Best for moderate–severe depression, OCD, panic, GAD, PTSD | Appropriate for bipolar depression alone, mild distress or psychosis |

**Conclusion.** SSRIs are among the most useful drugs in psychiatry — safe, cheap and effective across a broad range of depressive and anxiety disorders — but calling them a panacea is wrong. Half of patients do not respond to the first drug, psychological therapies match or exceed them in several conditions, they are inappropriate or harmful in some (bipolar, psychotic depression, mild distress), and their adverse and withdrawal effects are under-recognised. They are a cornerstone, not a cure-all.

## In depth

### Background

- Fluoxetine was approved in the USA in 1987, followed by sertraline, paroxetine, citalopram, escitalopram and fluvoxamine. Their safety in overdose and simplicity led to widespread prescribing, including by non-psychiatrists.
- The 1990s "Prozac nation" era framed SSRIs as lifestyle drugs; later critiques (Kirsch's meta-analyses, publication bias) and the serotonin-theory debate shifted opinion.
- India: generic SSRIs are widely available, inexpensive and are the mainstay in primary care and the District Mental Health Programme.

### Mechanism in detail

- SSRIs block the serotonin transporter (SERT) → acute rise in synaptic 5-HT → 5-HT1A autoreceptor desensitisation over days to weeks → increased serotonergic transmission → downstream effects on BDNF, neurogenesis and emotional processing (early change in negative bias).
- Onset is not delayed: improvement is greatest in weeks 1–2, and early response predicts remission; no improvement by 3–4 weeks predicts non-response [1].

### Assessment in detail — choosing the right drug

| Factor | Consideration |
| --- | --- |
| Severity | Mild → psychological therapy; moderate–severe → antidepressant ± therapy [1] |
| Bipolarity | Screen for past hypomania, family history before prescribing |
| Psychosis | Add antipsychotic or ECT |
| Age | Elderly: sodium, falls, bleeding; young: suicidality monitoring |
| Medical | Bleeding risk, cardiac QTc, hepatic impairment |
| Interactions | Fluoxetine, paroxetine (CYP2D6), fluvoxamine (CYP1A2) |
| Patient preference and past response | Shared decision-making [1] |

### Management details [1]

- Assess efficacy at **2 weeks**; switch if no improvement at 2–3 weeks (BAP suggests 4 weeks).
- Continue for at least **6 months after remission** (single episode); at least **2 years** for recurrent or high-risk depression.
- For non-response: switch (within or across class), combine (SSRI + mirtazapine best supported) or augment (aripiprazole, quetiapine, lithium).
- In anxiety disorders: start low, titrate; anxiety patients are especially sensitive to discontinuation symptoms; reduce over several months.

### Evidence table

| Evidence | Finding |
| --- | --- |
| Cipriani et al., 2018 (network meta-analysis, 522 trials) | All 21 antidepressants beat placebo; amitriptyline most efficacious; agomelatine and SSRIs (e.g. escitalopram, sertraline) among best tolerated [1] |
| Hieronymus et al. (18 SSRI trials, n = 6,669) | Using depressed mood item, 91% of comparisons favoured SSRI vs 44% using total HAM-D [1] |
| PANDA trial, 2019 | Sertraline improved anxiety and quality of life in primary care patients with mild–moderate symptoms [1] |
| OCD maintenance trials | Relapse 25–40% with 2 years' continuation vs 80% after stopping [1] |
| STEP-BD | Adjunctive antidepressants gave no benefit in bipolar depression [1] |

### Critical appraisal

- **Rating scales** dilute effect sizes (insomnia items score as much as suicidality); clinically, the mean drug–placebo difference is small but the proportion who benefit is meaningful [1].
- **Placebo response** has risen over decades, partly due to trial methodology [1].
- **Long-term use:** many patients stay on SSRIs for years without review; withdrawal effects can be mistaken for relapse [1].
- **Equity:** SSRIs are often the only treatment available in low-resource settings, which risks over-reliance in the absence of psychotherapy.

### Indian context

- SSRIs (fluoxetine, sertraline, escitalopram) are on the **National List of Essential Medicines** and are available free in many state programmes (author's knowledge — verify).
- Many Indian patients present with somatic symptoms; SSRIs help, but psychoeducation is needed to avoid premature discontinuation.
- Task-shared psychological treatments (Healthy Activity Programme) show that SSRIs need not be the only option in primary care.
- Fixed-dose combinations (e.g. SSRI with benzodiazepine) are widely sold in India and encourage benzodiazepine dependence — a practice to avoid.

### Recent advances

- Rapid-acting agents (esketamine, zuranolone for postpartum depression), psychedelic-assisted therapy and new mechanisms (vortioxetine, dextromethorphan–bupropion) challenge the SSRI monopoly.
- Pharmacogenomic testing (CYP2C19, CYP2D6) for SSRI dosing — modest evidence.
- Recognition of protracted withdrawal and hyperbolic tapering (Maudsley Deprescribing Guidelines) [1].
