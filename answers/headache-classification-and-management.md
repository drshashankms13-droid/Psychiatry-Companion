## Exam answer

**Introduction.**
- Headache is among the commonest chronic pain conditions. It is the **most common reason for lost productivity** in the US workforce [2].
- About **5%** of the population has **chronic daily headache** [2].
- The boundary between **migraine** and **tension-type headache** is blurred, and the distinction can be hard in some cases [1].

### Classification

**A. Common chronic headache conditions [2]:**
1. Migraine
2. Tension-type headache
3. Cluster headache
4. Headache associated with trauma
5. Chronic daily headache
6. Cervicogenic headache
7. Postdural puncture headache

**B. IHS classification of migraine (Subcommittee IHSC 2004; Lishman Box 8.7) [1]:**
- **1.1 Migraine without aura** (previously "common migraine").
- **1.2 Migraine with aura** (previously "classic migraine"). Includes aura with or without headache, **familial hemiplegic migraine** and **basilar-type migraine**.
- **1.3 Childhood periodic syndromes** that are commonly precursors of migraine: cyclical vomiting, abdominal migraine, benign paroxysmal vertigo of childhood.
- **1.4 Retinal migraine.**
- **1.5 Complications of migraine:** migrainous infarction, migraine-triggered seizure.

**C. Conditions reclassified out of migraine by the IHS [1]:**
- **Cluster headache** (previously "migrainous neuralgia"): now one of the **trigeminal autonomic cephalalgias**.
- **Ophthalmoplegic migraine:** now a **cranial neuralgia**.

**D. Chronic daily headache group [2]:**
- constant (**transformed**) **migraine**;
- **chronic tension-type headache**;
- **medication-overuse headache**;
- **hemicrania continua**.

**E. Other less common orofacial pain conditions [2]:** cluster headache, SUNCT, Tolosa–Hunt syndrome, atypical facial pain.

### Diagnostic criteria for migraine without aura (IHS 2004) [1]
- **A.** At least **5 attacks** fulfilling B–D.
- **B.** Attacks last **4–72 hours** (untreated or unsuccessfully treated).
- **C.** At least **2 of**: unilateral; pulsating; moderate or severe intensity; aggravated by, or causing avoidance of, routine physical activity.
- **D.** At least **1 of**: nausea and/or vomiting; photophobia **and** phonophobia.
- **E.** Not attributed to another disorder.
- **"POUNDing" predictors** (Detsky 2006): **P**ulsating, duration 4–72 h**O**urs, **U**nilateral, **N**ausea, **D**isabling. **4 or 5** of these make migraine very likely.

### Clinical distinctions [1, 2]
| Feature | Migraine | Tension headache | Cluster headache |
|---|---|---|---|
| Site | Mostly unilateral at onset, diffuse later | Bilateral or diffuse (implied by failing migraine criterion C) | Severe unilateral, usually periorbital |
| Quality | Throbbing, worse on coughing or jolting | Not pulsating (source does not define) | Severe |
| Associated | Anorexia, nausea, vomiting, photophobia, phonophobia, autonomic changes | — | Ipsilateral lacrimation, nasal blockage, sometimes Horner's |
| Pattern | Attacks of 8–24 h; aura in about one-third | — | Bouts over weeks, often at the same time each day |
| Mechanism | Trigeminovascular; cortical spreading depression for aura | "Muscle contraction" (Lishman, post-traumatic) | Hypothalamic activation |

### Differential diagnosis of headache [1]
- **Other causes of head and face pain:** chronic sinus disease, glaucoma, hypertension, raised intracranial pressure. History, examination and simple tests usually separate these.
- **Red flags needing a neurological opinion:**
  - deficits that **outlast the headache**, or hemiplegic attacks always on one side: consider an **angioma**;
  - one-sided ophthalmoplegic attacks: consider an **aneurysm** of the internal carotid or posterior communicating artery;
  - prominent brainstem deficits: consider a **brainstem lesion**;
  - classic migraine starting **in adult life**, especially auras without much headache.
- **Subdural haematoma:** vague, gradual headache with fluctuating consciousness, especially in the elderly [1].
- **Post-traumatic headache:** diffuse, vague, unremitting headache without clear precipitants and resistant to analgesics suggests a **psychogenic** basis. It fluctuates with tension or depression. Occipital neuralgia, vasospastic scalp arteries and **tension headache due to muscle contraction** must be considered [1].

### Management

**1. General principles [1, 2]:**
- **Identify and control comorbidities and risk factors** [2].
- **Regularise sleep and meal times**; avoid stress where possible; consider provocative dietary substances [1].
- **Detect and treat depression or anxiety** that aggravate headache [1].
- Look for **conflicts at work or in relationships** [1].
- **Watch for analgesic overuse**, which leads to **rebound headache** [2].

**2. Migraine: acute attack [1, 2]:**
- **Triptans** (selective 5HT1B/1D agonists): work even several hours after onset; also relieve nausea and photophobia. Use **nasal spray or suppository** when vomiting is present.
  - **Contraindications:** ischaemic heart disease, uncontrolled hypertension, pregnancy.
- **NSAIDs** [2].
- **Alternatives:** aspirin with metoclopramide; ergotamine by nasal spray or rectally [1].
- **CGRP antagonists:** a new class, effective in one trial [1].

**3. Migraine: prophylaxis [1, 2]:**
- **Indication:** 2 or more attacks a month, regularly [1].
- **Evidence-based, well tolerated:** valproate, amitriptyline, **propranolol (first choice)**, timolol [1].
- **Effective but problematic side effects:** methysergide, pizotifen [1].
- **K&S's best prophylactic agents:** β-blockers, flunarizine, valproate, topiramate, and **CBT** [2].
- **Buspirone** may reduce headache in anxious migraineurs [1].

**4. Psychological treatment [1]:**
- **Relaxation training** gives enduring improvement.
- **Hypnosis** aimed at reducing tension or apprehension.
- **Behavioural techniques** reduce frequency and severity.
- Understand what the attacks may express (a halt after activity, an outlet for repressed anger, a self-punitive role in depression; Sacks).

**5. Tension headache and chronic daily headache [1, 2]:**
- **Combined medication plus CBT** is better than either alone for chronic daily headache [2].
- **Mirtazapine** reduces the **duration and intensity** of chronic tension-type headache [2].
- **Treat comorbid depression and anxiety.** Patients with chronic daily headache have more depression, anxiety and functional disability [2].
- **Stop analgesic overuse** to prevent rebound headache [2].
- **Relaxation and tension reduction** (by analogy with the migraine evidence) [1].

**6. Chronic or transformed migraine [1]:**
- Defined as headache on **at least 15 days a month** (for on average more than 4 hours a day if untreated).
- Usually comes with **severe depression**, malaise, anorexia, insomnia, weight loss.
- Over-medication may be partly responsible.
- Treat the depression: migraine and depression **reinforce each other**.
- **Occipital nerve stimulation** may help.

**Conclusion.** Headache is classified clinically into migraine (with its IHS subtypes), tension-type, cluster and other headaches, plus chronic daily headache. Management combines exclusion of secondary causes, acute and prophylactic drugs, treatment of comorbid depression and anxiety, avoidance of analgesic overuse, and psychological methods such as relaxation and CBT.

## In depth

### Psychiatric comorbidity in headache [1]
- Depression or anxiety risk is **2–3 times** higher in migraine. Anxiety risk is higher than depression risk.
- Studies:
  - **Breslau 1991:** MDD 26.5% vs 9% (OR about 3);
  - **Lipton 2000:** depression 47% vs 17%.
- **Non-migraine headache carries a similar risk** (Breslau 2000: MDD 40.7% in migraine, 35.8% in severe non-migraine headache, 16% in controls). There is a dose–response with headache frequency (Zwart 2003).
- **Neuroticism** scores are equally raised in migraine and tension-type headache. In one study only tension-type headache was associated with high EPI neuroticism (Rasmussen 1992).
- **Suicide attempt** risk is raised in migraine, not fully explained by depression.

### Psychiatric features during migraine attacks (Klee 1968) [1]
- Memory impairment 10%.
- Clouding or delirium 8%.
- Pronounced anxiety 8%.
- Complex hallucinations 6%.
- Body-image change 6%.
- Severe depression 4%.
