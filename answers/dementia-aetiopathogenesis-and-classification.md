## Exam answer

### Definition [1, 2]
- **DSM-IV (as used by Lishman):**
  - memory impairment **plus** at least one of aphasia, apraxia, agnosia or executive dysfunction;
  - impairing social or occupational function;
  - a decline from a previous level;
  - distinguished from delirium [1].
- **DSM-5-TR major neurocognitive disorder (NCD)** [2]:
  - A. **Significant decline in one or more domains**:
    - complex attention, executive function, learning and memory, language, perceptual-motor, social cognition;
    - based on concern (of the individual, an informant or the clinician) **and** substantial impairment on standardised testing.
  - B. **Interferes with independence** (at least needs help with complex IADLs).
  - C. Not exclusively during delirium.
  - D. Not better explained by another mental disorder.
  - **Specifiers:** with or without behavioural disturbance; severity (mild = IADL, moderate = basic ADL, severe = fully dependent).
- **Mild NCD:** modest decline; independence preserved with effort [2].

### Epidemiology [1, 3]
- **Prevalence:**
  - about **5% over 65**, doubling every 5 years, to **20–30% over 85** [1];
  - 20% in the over-80s [3].
- **Global:** over 24 million (2005), forecast over 80 million by 2040.
- **Under-recognition:** only half to two-thirds are known to GPs.
- **Pathology vs clinical diagnosis:** AD pathology is twice as common as a clinical AD diagnosis.

### Classification

**A. Clinical / aetiological (DSM-5-TR subtypes)** [1, 2]:

| Group | Conditions |
| --- | --- |
| **Primary neurodegenerative** | **Alzheimer's disease** (about 60%; up to two-thirds); **dementia with Lewy bodies** (10–20%; third commonest); **frontotemporal dementias** (Pick's disease and others; important at age 45–65); **Parkinson's disease dementia**; **Huntington's disease**; PSP, CBD |
| **Vascular** | Multi-infarct; small-vessel (Binswanger's); post-stroke; strategic infarct; CADASIL; amyloid angiopathy (second commonest; frequently **mixed with AD**) |
| **Prion** | Sporadic, variant, familial and iatrogenic CJD; GSS; fatal familial insomnia |
| **Infective / inflammatory** | HIV, **general paresis (neurosyphilis)**, encephalitis, cerebral abscess, vasculitis, sarcoid, Whipple's disease |
| **Structural** | Tumour (e.g., frontal meningioma), **subdural haematoma**, **normal-pressure hydrocephalus** |
| **Traumatic** | Traumatic brain injury; **dementia pugilistica** |
| **Metabolic / endocrine / nutritional** | **Hypothyroidism**, hypoglycaemia, renal or hepatic failure, parathyroid disease, **B12 and folate deficiency**, thiamine deficiency (Korsakoff's) |
| **Toxic** | **Alcohol**, drugs, heavy metals (lead, manganese), carbon monoxide |
| **Anoxic** | Cardiac arrest, anaemia, chronic pulmonary disease |
| **Others** | Wilson's disease, MS, neuroacanthocytosis, metachromatic leucodystrophy, Kufs disease, mitochondrial disorders |
| **Pseudodementia** | Depression, Ganser state, hysterical or simulated "dementia" |

**B. Cortical vs subcortical** [1]:
- **Cortical** (e.g., AD): amnesia (encoding), aphasia, apraxia, agnosia.
- **Subcortical** (PSP, HD, PD, Wilson's, NPH, lacunar states): slowing (bradyphrenia), retrieval deficit, executive dysfunction, apathy and depression; no aphasia, apraxia or agnosia.
- In reality this is a **spectrum**.

**C. Age at onset:**
- **early onset** (before 65; "presenile") vs **late onset**;
- biologically more meaningful: **very early (before 50–55, often autosomal dominant)**, typical, and very late (after 80–85) [1].

**D. Reversibility:**
- "Reversible" causes are **rare**: in a meta-analysis of about 6,000 patients, **9% potentially reversible, 0.6% actually reversed** (Clarfield) [1].
- More secondary causes in **early-onset** cases (30% vs 8%) [1].
- **India:** a higher proportion of neuroinfections and B12 deficiency, often reversible (Srikanth & Nagaraja 2005) [1].

**E. Molecular / neuropathological classification ("proteinopathies")** [1]:

| Group | Aggregated protein | Disorders |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Tauopathies** | Tau (3R/4R) | AD (with amyloid), PSP, CBD, many FTDs (Pick's 3R; PSP and CBD 4R), FTDP-17 |
| **Synucleinopathies** | α-synuclein | PD, DLB, MSA |
| **Amyloidopathies** | Aβ; PrP | AD, prion diseases, familial British/Danish dementia, cerebral amyloid angiopathy |
| **TDP-43 proteinopathy** | TDP-43 | FTD (ubiquitin-positive, tau-negative), FTD-MND [2] |
| **Polyglutamine** | Huntingtin | HD |

**Caveats:**
- **Overlap**: AD is both a tauopathy and an amyloidopathy, and may have Lewy bodies.
- The scheme points to **shared mechanisms and therapies**, but **symptom-based classification remains more clinically useful** [1].

### Aetiopathogenesis: general mechanisms [1, 2]
1. **Protein misfolding and aggregation:**
   - Aβ (amyloid cascade);
   - hyperphosphorylated tau disrupting **microtubules and axonal transport**;
   - α-synuclein;
   - prion PrPSc template conversion;
   - huntingtin nuclear aggregates.
   - Failure of the **ubiquitin–proteasome and autophagy** clearance systems.
2. **Neurotransmitter loss:**
   - cholinergic (nucleus basalis) in AD and DLB;
   - noradrenergic and serotonergic loss also;
   - GABA and acetylcholine loss with dopamine excess in HD.
3. **Vascular injury:**
   - infarction, small-vessel disease, white-matter ischaemia, amyloid angiopathy;
   - synergistic with AD pathology.
4. **Genetics:**
   - **autosomal dominant**: APP, PSEN1, PSEN2 (AD); MAPT, GRN, C9orf72 (FTD) [2]; HTT (HD); PRNP;
   - **susceptibility**: APOE ε4 (3–4× heterozygous, about 10× homozygous; "when, not if").
5. **Inflammation, oxidative stress, excitotoxicity, insulin resistance.**
6. **Environmental and vascular risk factors:**
   - age (dominant); head injury;
   - hypertension, diabetes, homocysteine; smoking;
   - low education/"reserve"; depression (prodrome);
   - **protective**: NSAIDs (epidemiological only), activity.
7. **Cognitive reserve:** one-third of non-demented elderly people have similar pathology to demented ones (MRC-CFAS). Reserve and synaptic dysfunction may matter more than lesion counts [1].

**Conclusion.** Dementia (major NCD) is an acquired, progressive decline in cognition causing loss of independence. It is classified:
- **clinically/aetiologically**: AD > vascular > DLB > FTD, plus secondary causes;
- **anatomically**: cortical vs subcortical;
- **by age at onset**;
- **molecularly**: tau, synuclein, amyloid, TDP-43, prion.

Mechanisms converge on protein aggregation, neurotransmitter loss, vascular injury and genetic susceptibility. Mixed pathology is the rule in the elderly. Truly reversible dementias are rare but must be sought, especially in early-onset cases and in India.
