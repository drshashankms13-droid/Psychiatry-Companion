## Exam answer

**Case summary.** A 35-year-old factory worker, ill for 2 months with irritability, suspiciousness and social withdrawal, **stopped his medication 2 weeks ago**. For 3 days he has been **mute, immobile, not eating**, maintaining fixed postures. Examination shows **waxy flexibility, negativism and posturing**, with **fever (38.7 °C), tachycardia (116/min) and raised CPK**.

### 1. Most likely diagnosis, subtypes and key features

**Most likely diagnosis: catatonia (retarded/stuporous) with autonomic instability — malignant catatonia — associated with a relapse of a psychotic disorder (probably schizophrenia), precipitated by stopping antipsychotic treatment.**

Justification:
- **Catatonia:** DSM-5 requires at least 3 of 12 signs; he shows **stupor, mutism, posturing, waxy flexibility and negativism** (5 signs) [1].
- **Malignant features:** fever, tachycardia and raised CK, plus 3 days without food or fluid [2].
- **Underlying disorder:** a 2-month prodrome of suspiciousness and withdrawal, and medication stopped 2 weeks earlier, suggest **relapse of schizophrenia**; a mood disorder with catatonia must also be considered because catatonia is commoner in mood disorders [1].
- **Withdrawal catatonia:** if the medication was **clozapine** or a **benzodiazepine**, abrupt withdrawal itself can cause catatonia [2].

**Subtypes of catatonia [2]:**

| Subtype | Features |
| --- | --- |
| Retarded / stuporous | Mutism, immobility, posturing, negativism, waxy flexibility, catalepsy, staring, withdrawal |
| Excited | Purposeless overactivity, agitation, combativeness, impulsivity, stereotypies |
| Malignant | Either of the above with autonomic instability — fever, labile or high BP, tachycardia, tachypnoea, sweating; can be fatal |

DSM-5 categories: catatonia associated with another mental disorder; catatonic disorder due to another medical condition; unspecified catatonia [1].

**Key clinical features:** stupor, catalepsy, waxy flexibility, mutism, negativism, posturing, mannerism, stereotypy, agitation, grimacing, echolalia, echopraxia; also withdrawal (food refusal), staring, rigidity, ambitendency, automatic obedience, gegenhalten, grasp reflex [1]. Complications: dehydration, renal failure, rhabdomyolysis, **DVT/pulmonary embolism**, aspiration pneumonia, pressure sores and death [1, 2].

### 2. Initial emergency management

**Immediate (first hour)**
- Manage in an area with **medical monitoring**; involve physicians early; transfer to ICU if temperature keeps rising, BP is unstable, airway is at risk or CK is very high.
- ABC; IV access; **IV fluids** (rehydrate and protect kidneys against myoglobinuria); cooling for fever; nil by mouth until swallowing is safe.
- **Stop all antipsychotics** for now (NMS not yet excluded) [2].
- **Lorazepam challenge:** 2 mg IV/IM/sublingual; repeat 2 mg after about 3 hours if no effect [2].
- **DVT prophylaxis** (low-molecular-weight heparin unless contraindicated), pressure-area care, eye and mouth care, urinary catheter only if retention.

**Investigations**
- **Blood:** CBC (leucocytosis), electrolytes, urea, creatinine, glucose, calcium, magnesium, phosphate, **CK (serial)**, LFT, **serum iron (low in NMS/malignant catatonia)**, TSH, coagulation, **blood cultures**, D-dimer, urine myoglobin.
- **Toxicology:** urine drug screen; lithium level if relevant.
- **Infection screen:** chest X-ray, urine culture — fever must not be blamed on catatonia until infection is excluded.
- **ECG** (tachycardia, QT before drugs).
- **Neurology (if atypical, first episode or poor response):** **EEG** (non-convulsive status), **MRI brain**, **lumbar puncture** with CSF anti-NMDAR antibodies (autoimmune encephalitis).
- Collateral history: exact medication (name, dose, depot/LAI, clozapine, benzodiazepines), last dose, substance use, past episodes and response.
- **Bush–Francis Catatonia Rating Scale** at baseline and after each lorazepam dose.

**First-line treatment** [2]
Lorazepam challenge → **lorazepam up to 4 mg/day**, increasing to **8–24 mg/day** in divided doses if needed → **if no clear response in 1–2 days, or if life is in danger at any point → ECT** (do not wait in malignant catatonia).

### 3. Differentiating from neuroleptic malignant syndrome

| Feature | Malignant catatonia | NMS |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Trigger | Psychiatric or medical illness; antipsychotic withdrawal | **Recent dopamine-antagonist exposure** or dose increase [2] |
| Sequence | Catatonic signs (often days) **before** fever and autonomic change | Rigidity and fever develop together, usually within days of a drug change |
| Motor | Posturing, waxy flexibility, negativism, stereotypies | **Generalised lead-pipe rigidity**, tremor [1] |
| Consciousness | Usually alert but unresponsive | Fluctuating, confusion |
| CK | Raised | Raised, **often >1,000 units/L** [2] |
| Autonomic | Present | Usually more severe [1] |
| Course | May respond within hours to lorazepam | Resolves over 1–2 weeks after stopping the drug |

**In this patient:** he stopped medication 2 weeks ago, so NMS is less likely but **not excluded** — check whether he received a **depot/LAI** (which remains active for weeks) or any antipsychotic in the emergency department. Malignant catatonia and NMS **may be indistinguishable clinically and in laboratory tests**, and NMS may be a drug-induced variant of malignant catatonia [2].

**Management implications (same core plan for both):**
- **Withhold all antipsychotics**, including depot, until both are resolved [2].
- **Benzodiazepines** treat both; **ECT** is effective for both, even after drug treatment fails [2].
- If NMS is favoured: add **bromocriptine** and/or **dantrolene** in the medical unit; artificial ventilation if needed [2].
- Later rechallenge: wait **at least 5 days** after full resolution, start a **low dose of a low-potency, structurally different drug** (quetiapine or clozapine), titrate slowly with monitoring of temperature, pulse and BP; **avoid depots and high-potency FGAs** [2].

### 4. ECT — indications, procedure and legal aspects in India

**Indications in catatonia** [2]
- **Malignant catatonia** or any catatonia with **danger to life** (refusal of food and fluids, autonomic instability) — ECT should not be delayed.
- **No response to benzodiazepines** after 1–2 days.
- Catatonia in **schizophrenia** (benzodiazepine response only 40–50%).
- NMS not responding to drug treatment; catatonia with delirious mania, autism or limbic encephalitis.

**Procedure**
- Pre-ECT work-up: physical examination, CBC, electrolytes, ECG, chest X-ray, anaesthesia review; correct dehydration, potassium and CK-related renal risk (succinylcholine raises potassium in rhabdomyolysis/immobilised patients — anaesthetist may choose a non-depolarising relaxant).
- **Modified ECT only** (general anaesthesia + muscle relaxant).
- **Bilateral** electrode placement, **liberal stimulus dosing** to obtain well-generalised seizures [2]; often daily for the first few sessions in malignant catatonia, then three times a week; usual course 6–12 sessions.
- Benzodiazepines raise the seizure threshold — reduce or omit the pre-session dose, or use flumazenil at induction.
- Monitor vital signs, seizure duration (EEG), orientation and memory.

**Legal aspects — Mental Healthcare Act 2017**
- **Capacity assessment** (Section 4): a mute, stuporous patient usually lacks capacity.
- Admission under **supported admission (Section 89)** with the **nominated representative's** consent if he lacks capacity, has a severe illness and risk of harm (here: inability to care for himself — not eating or drinking).
- **Informed consent for ECT** is required — from the patient if he regains capacity, otherwise from the **nominated representative**; document capacity, consent, indication and risks.
- **Section 94 emergency treatment (72 hours)** does **not** permit ECT.
- **Section 95:** ECT **without muscle relaxants and anaesthesia is prohibited**; ECT for minors only with guardian consent and prior Mental Health Review Board permission.
- Check for an **advance directive** (Section 5) refusing ECT; to act against it, the treating psychiatrist must apply to the Mental Health Review Board (Section 11).

### 5. Psychosocial and rehabilitative strategies after recovery

- **Explain the episode** to patient and family: catatonia is a treatable complication, often linked to stopping medication.
- **Relapse prevention:** shared decision on maintenance antipsychotic; consider a **long-acting injectable** once NMS is excluded and he is stable; avoid abrupt stopping; identify early warning signs (irritability, suspiciousness, withdrawal, poor sleep).
- **Explore why he stopped medication:** adverse effects (sexual dysfunction, sedation, EPS), cost, lack of insight, stigma, belief that he was cured.
- **Family psychoeducation** to reduce expressed emotion and improve supervision of medication.
- **Physical recovery:** nutrition, mobilisation, physiotherapy for deconditioning, follow-up of renal function and CK.
- **Occupational rehabilitation:** graded return to factory work; liaise with employer about absence; supported employment if needed; disability certification under the RPwD Act 2016 if impairment persists.
- **Case management and follow-up:** regular outpatient or community follow-up (DMHP, Tele-MANAS for remote support); crisis plan; record the episode and response to lorazepam and ECT so future episodes are treated quickly.
- **Advance directive and nominated representative** under MHCA 2017 for future episodes.

**Conclusion.** This man has malignant catatonia in a relapse of psychosis after stopping medication. Treat it as a medical emergency: stop antipsychotics, exclude NMS, infection and organic causes, rehydrate, give lorazepam, and proceed to modified bilateral ECT early if there is no rapid response or life is threatened — with informed consent from his nominated representative under MHCA 2017. After recovery, reintroduce antipsychotics cautiously and build relapse prevention around adherence, family work and occupational rehabilitation.

## In depth

### Background

- Kahlbaum (1874) described catatonia; Kraepelin made it a subtype of dementia praecox. **Stauder (1934)** described "lethal catatonia" with hyperthermia and death before antipsychotics existed — evidence that malignant catatonia is not only drug-induced. DSM-5 (2013) separated catatonia from schizophrenia [1].
- Many authorities view NMS as a drug-induced form of malignant catatonia because the clinical and laboratory pictures overlap and both respond to benzodiazepines and ECT [2].

### Epidemiology

- Pooled prevalence of catatonia about **9%** across clinical samples [2]; 7–17% of acute psychosis admissions and 13–31% of mood-disorder admissions [1].
- NMS incidence about 0.02–0.16% of antipsychotic-treated patients; mortality about **5.6%** [2]. Risk factors: high-potency FGAs, rapid dose increase or reduction, IM administration, dehydration, agitation, young adult males; NMS is more lethal in older people [2].
- **Catatonic schizophrenia carries a much higher risk of NMS** than non-catatonic schizophrenia [2].

### Classification and criteria

- **DSM-5:** catatonia requires 3 or more of 12 signs; "catatonic disorder due to another medical condition" needs evidence of a causal medical condition and is not diagnosed if symptoms occur only during delirium [1].
- **ICD-11:** catatonia has its own grouping — catatonia associated with another mental disorder, catatonia induced by substances or medications, and secondary catatonia syndrome (medical causes).
- **NMS diagnostic criteria:** an international Delphi consensus (Gurrera 2011) — recent dopamine antagonist exposure, hyperthermia, rigidity, altered consciousness, CK elevation, autonomic lability, tachycardia, tachypnoea, negative work-up for other causes [2].

### Mechanism in detail

- **Catatonia:** reduced GABA-A function in orbitofrontal and motor cortex; glutamate/NMDA dysregulation (anti-NMDAR encephalitis often presents with catatonia); dopamine hypofunction in basal ganglia, which explains why D2 blockade can worsen catatonia or tip it into NMS [1, 2].
- **NMS:** sudden D2 blockade in hypothalamus (thermoregulation) and nigrostriatal pathway (rigidity), with sympathoadrenal hyperactivity [2].
- **Antipsychotic withdrawal:** cholinergic and dopaminergic rebound after abrupt stopping, especially of clozapine, can produce catatonia; benzodiazepine withdrawal can too [2].

### Clinical features in detail

- Patients may later recall being aware but unable to move or speak, or have patchy, dream-like memories [1].
- Catatonic stupor can alternate quickly with excitement, making diagnosis difficult [1].
- Fever in a catatonic patient has three main explanations to exclude in order: **infection** (aspiration pneumonia, UTI), **NMS**, and **malignant catatonia**.
- Examination for catatonia must be active: test for catalepsy, waxy flexibility, negativism, gegenhalten, echo-phenomena, automatic obedience and grasp reflex.

### Assessment in detail

| Tool / test | Purpose |
| --- | --- |
| Bush–Francis Catatonia Rating Scale (23 items; first 14 = screen) | Diagnosis and monitoring |
| Lorazepam challenge | Diagnostic and therapeutic; improvement within minutes to hours supports catatonia [2] |
| Serial CK, creatinine, potassium | Rhabdomyolysis, renal failure |
| Serum iron | Low iron is reported in NMS and malignant catatonia |
| EEG | Non-convulsive status; diffuse slowing in encephalitis/delirium |
| MRI, CSF (cells, protein, anti-NMDAR) | Encephalitis, structural lesions |

### Differential diagnosis

| Condition | Points for / against in this case |
| --- | --- |
| Malignant catatonia in schizophrenia relapse | Prodrome, catatonic signs, autonomic instability — **most likely** |
| NMS | Raised CK, fever, tachycardia — but medication stopped 2 weeks ago; check for depot |
| Catatonia in bipolar/depressive episode | Irritability could be mood-related; ask about past mood episodes and family history |
| Clozapine or benzodiazepine withdrawal catatonia | Depends on which drug was stopped [2] |
| Sepsis / CNS infection (meningitis, encephalitis) | Fever, tachycardia; needs cultures, LP |
| Anti-NMDAR encephalitis | Prodromal psychiatric symptoms, catatonia, autonomic instability, dyskinesias, seizures |
| Non-convulsive status epilepticus | EEG needed |
| Serotonin syndrome | Only if on serotonergic drugs; clonus, hyperreflexia |
| Heat stroke, thyrotoxicosis | Environment; thyroid tests |
| Malignant hyperthermia | Anaesthetic exposure |

### Management details

- **Why ECT in malignant catatonia:** it is the most successful treatment; mortality in untreated malignant catatonia is high [2]. Use liberal stimulus dosing [2].
- **Bromocriptine** 2.5 mg 8-hourly (up to about 40 mg/day) and **dantrolene** 1–2.5 mg/kg IV are used for NMS in medical settings (doses from author's knowledge); evidence is limited and guidelines vary [2].
- **Lorazepam route:** sublingual tablets are tasteless — useful in a patient who will not swallow [2].
- **Restarting antipsychotics:** in catatonic schizophrenia, many clinicians reintroduce antipsychotics only after catatonia resolves, or when benzodiazepines/ECT have failed and psychosis is clearly present [2]. If clozapine withdrawal caused catatonia, re-establish clozapine early with re-titration [2].
- **Antipsychotic choice after NMS:** quetiapine or clozapine (low D2 affinity); aripiprazole possible but long half-life and some NMS risk; avoid depots and high-potency FGAs [2].
- **Maintenance ECT** may be considered if catatonia recurs on stopping ECT.

### Evidence table

| Study | Design | Main finding |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Solmi et al., 2018 | Meta-analysis, n = 107,304 | Catatonia prevalence about 9% [2] |
| Ungvari et al. | Crossover RCT | Lorazepam ineffective in chronic catatonic schizophrenia [2] |
| Lin et al. | Observational, n = 21 | Lorazepam–diazepam protocol effective in medical/substance catatonia [2] |
| Rogers et al., 2023 (BAP) | Consensus guideline | Benzodiazepines first line; ECT if failure or life-threatening [2] |
| Guinart et al., 2021 | Pooled patient-level analysis | Mortality in NMS predicted by respiratory problems, severe hyperthermia, failure to stop antipsychotic [2] |
| Funayama et al. | Cohort | Catatonic stupor in schizophrenia associated with medical complications and mortality [2] |

### Critical appraisal

- The evidence for every step comes from case series, observational studies and consensus, not RCTs.
- The NMS–malignant catatonia boundary is uncertain; the practical overlap of treatment makes the distinction less important acutely, but it matters for later antipsychotic rechallenge.
- Stopping antipsychotics treats NMS but may worsen the underlying psychosis — timing of reintroduction is a balance with little evidence.
- ECT is underused in catatonia in some settings because of stigma and consent barriers, despite being the most effective treatment.

### Indian context

- Patients often reach emergency services late, after days of food refusal, so dehydration, renal injury and thromboembolism are common.
- Families may initially attribute stupor to possession or "black magic" and consult faith healers; non-adherence is often linked to cost, stigma or the belief that the illness is cured.
- ECT is widely available in Indian teaching hospitals; MHCA 2017 requires informed consent and prohibits unmodified ECT — documentation of capacity and nominated representative consent is essential.
- Lorazepam injections are cheap and available; ICU beds may be limited, so early liaison with physicians is important.
- After recovery, the **District Mental Health Programme**, community follow-up and **Tele-MANAS (14416)** support continuity of care.

### Recent advances

- **BAP catatonia guidelines (2023)** standardise the benzodiazepine-then-ECT pathway [2].
- Greater recognition of **autoimmune encephalitis** presenting as catatonia, and of **withdrawal catatonia** after clozapine [2].
- **Zolpidem** test dose and treatment, and **rTMS/tDCS** as alternatives, are under study [2].
