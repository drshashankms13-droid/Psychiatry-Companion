## Exam answer

### Introduction

Culture-bound syndromes is a historical term used for patterns of distress, illness experience or behaviour that appear to be specific to, or strongly shaped by, particular cultural contexts. DSM-5 replaced this terminology with the broader concept of **cultural concepts of distress**.

### DSM-5 cultural concepts of distress

1.  **Cultural syndromes:** clusters of symptoms or troubling
    experiences recognised within a cultural group.
2.  **Cultural idioms of distress:** culturally shared ways of
    expressing suffering.
3.  **Cultural explanations or perceived causes:** explanatory models
    used to understand symptoms.

### Examples

-   **Dhat syndrome:** distress attributed to semen loss, reported
    particularly in South Asian settings.
-   **Koro:** fear that the genitalia are retracting into the body,
    sometimes associated with fear of death.
-   **Amok:** historical description of episodes of disturbed behaviour,
    often discussed in cultural psychiatry.
-   **Ataque de nervios:** episodes of intense emotional distress
    described in some Latin American communities.
-   **Susto:** illness attributed to fright or loss of soul in some
    Latin American contexts.
-   **Neurasthenia:** fatigue and weakness syndrome with historical and
    cultural relevance, including in some Asian settings.

These examples should not be used as stereotypes; presentations vary within cultures and require individual assessment.

### Indian context

Dhat-related distress, possession experiences, spirit-related explanatory models and culturally patterned somatic presentations may be encountered. Clinicians should explore the patient's own explanatory model and distinguish culturally accepted beliefs from psychopathology.

### Assessment

The DSM-5 Cultural Formulation Interview (CFI) explores:
- The person's definition of the problem.
- Perceived causes and explanatory models.
- Cultural identity and context.
- Stressors and supports.
- Coping and help-seeking.
- Preferences and barriers to care.

### Management

Use culturally sensitive communication, avoid premature pathologisation, explain diagnoses collaboratively and integrate family or community resources when appropriate. Evidence-based treatment should be adapted without compromising safety or efficacy.

### Conclusion

Culture shapes the expression, interpretation and management of distress. The cultural concepts of distress framework improves diagnostic accuracy and therapeutic alliance by incorporating patients' explanatory models.
