## Exam answer

### The clinical dilemma [1, 2]
- Stopping mood-stabilising medication in pregnancy gives a **high relapse risk**. After delivery the risk is **hugely increased**:
  - DSM-5-TR: the postpartum risk is consistently raised in bipolar I disorder. Pregnancy itself carries no excess risk **except in women who stop medication** [2];
  - **postpartum relapse up to 70%** in women who stopped lithium before conception [1].
- **Untreated mood disorder** harms the mother (self-care, obstetric care, admission) and the child (neglect to infanticide).
- Data on anticonvulsants come mostly from **epilepsy** cohorts and may not transfer exactly to psychiatric use.

### Drug by drug [1]

**Lithium**
- **Crosses the placenta completely.** Increased risk of congenital anomalies, **highest in the first trimester**, possibly dose-related. The overall risk was probably **overestimated in the past**.
- **Ebstein's anomaly:**
  - the effect is **much smaller** than earlier thought;
  - a surveillance study of 5.6 million births linked Ebstein's to **maternal mental illness generally** rather than to lithium specifically;
  - **maximum risk 2–6 weeks after conception**, often before the pregnancy is known;
  - **atrial and ventricular septal defects** may also increase.
- **Third trimester:** clearance rises with total body water, so **higher doses** are needed. Requirements **fall abruptly after delivery**, giving a toxicity risk.
- **Neonatal and obstetric associations:**
  - goitre, **hypotonia, lethargy**, arrhythmia, respiratory problems, low Apgar scores;
  - preterm birth and large for gestational age (not confirmed in a large cohort);
  - possibly neonatal readmission.
- **Neonatal brain development is probably unaffected.**
- **Policy:** avoid if possible. But if lithium is the drug most likely to keep her well, **inform her and support her to stay on it**. If stopping, **taper slowly before conception** (abrupt withdrawal raises relapse).

**Valproate: avoid**
- **About 10% major malformations**, higher than carbamazepine:
  - **neural tube defects** (spina bifida);
  - atrial septal defect, cleft palate, hypospadias, polydactyly, craniosynostosis;
  - reduced head circumference; possibly pre-eclampsia.
- **Neurodevelopment:**
  - **up to 40% of exposed pre-schoolers have developmental delay** (EMA review);
  - lower IQ, more **autism**, poorer language, memory and executive function; dose-related deficits; poorer school performance.
- **Must be stopped immediately** if pregnancy occurs while it is prescribed as a mood stabiliser.
- **UK:** not to be started in patients **under 55** or continued in women of child-bearing potential unless **two specialists** document that there is no alternative.
- **COPE:** taper over 2–4 weeks once conception is planned, adding **folic acid 5 mg/day** through the first trimester.
- If it is truly the only option: a full risk briefing and **written consent**. Maudsley says it is hard to imagine benefits outweighing the risks.

**Carbamazepine: avoid**
- Causal link with malformations, especially **neural tube defects**. If essential, use **low-dose monotherapy** (the risk is probably dose-related).
- Third-trimester use: **maternal vitamin K**; NICE adds **prophylactic vitamin K to mother and neonate** after delivery.
- NICE: do not offer it for mental illness in women planning pregnancy, pregnant or breastfeeding.

**Lamotrigine: the safest anticonvulsant option**
- Major malformation risk is **within the background range**; safer than valproate or carbamazepine across outcomes.
- **Clearance rises sharply in pregnancy** and falls postpartum, so **check levels frequently** (NICE).
- Parent-reported behaviour problems and a possible autism association, but **no significant neurodevelopmental effect** overall.

**Others**
- **Topiramate:** orofacial clefts, neurodevelopmental disorders, small for gestational age. **Do not use** in pregnancy; contraception is needed.
- **Oxcarbazepine** and **pregabalin:** unclear data. Pregabalin carries a small malformation signal (MHRA: advise contraception).
- **Folate:** does not prevent anticonvulsant neural tube defects once pregnant (the tube is formed by **8 weeks**). It may help if taken **before conception** and may benefit neurodevelopment, so **always offer it**.

### Recommendations (Box 7.1, Box 7.4) [1]
1. **All women of child-bearing potential:**
   - discuss pregnancy;
   - avoid valproate, topiramate and carbamazepine. If they are unavoidable, explain teratogenicity and **confirm stable long-term contraception**.
2. **Planning pregnancy:**
   - after long remission, consider switching to a **safer drug (antipsychotic)** or withdrawing before conception and for at least the first trimester;
   - refer for **pre-conception perinatal advice**.
3. **No mood stabiliser is clearly safe.** NICE prefers **mood-stabilising antipsychotics** over continuing a mood stabiliser.
4. **Severe illness or rapid relapse after stopping:** continue after discussion (**never valproate**). ACOG advises **not stopping mood stabilisers other than valproate**.
5. **Lithium options in pregnancy (NICE):**
   - **if well:** taper over **4 weeks**, warning of postnatal relapse;
   - **if unwell or high risk:** switch gradually to an antipsychotic; **or** stop and restart in the second trimester (if not breastfeeding and lithium was best); **or** continue if an antipsychotic is unlikely to work;
   - **levels every 4 weeks, then weekly from 36 weeks**; keep in the therapeutic range; ensure fluid balance;
   - **deliver in hospital**; **stop lithium during labour**; check the level **12 hours after the last dose**;
   - **fetal echocardiography / detailed second-trimester ultrasound** (ACOG); COPE: 13 or 18–20-week scan.
6. **Acute mania in pregnancy:** an **antipsychotic**; if ineffective, **ECT**.
7. **Bipolar depression in pregnancy:** **CBT** if moderate, an **SSRI** if more severe; **lamotrigine** is an option.
8. **Breastfeeding:**
   - **lithium: continue it but advise against breastfeeding** (infant levels up to 57% of maternal);
   - **lamotrigine**: infant levels up to 48%, thrombocytosis and one apnoea case;
   - **valproate**: low transfer (under 2% of maternal levels), but reversible thrombocytopenia and anaemia have been reported;
   - if starting postpartum, prefer **olanzapine or quetiapine**.

### Update from IPS CPG 2022 [3]
**Relapse:**
- bipolar relapse is **as high as 66%** in women who **stop prophylaxis**, vs **about 25%** in those who continue (Wesseloo meta-analysis);
- SMI overall: **60–80% without prophylaxis vs 20–30% with it**.

**Hierarchy:**
1. **SGA monotherapy is first-line** for bipolar disorder in pregnancy.
2. **Mood stabilisers are second-line** (after SGAs). Add one only if the SGA alone fails, or she is **already well on it**.
3. **Valproate and carbamazepine are avoided** (malformations and neurodevelopmental disorders).
4. **Lamotrigine** at a **low dose (under 325 mg/day)** may not raise major malformations. It may be **continued in bipolar depression** after discussing risks and benefits.
5. **Lithium** may be considered in women who **have needed it to stay well**, after discussion with the woman and caregivers.

**Risks (Table 8):**

| Drug | Pregnancy | Lactation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Lithium | **Ebstein's anomaly 1/1000**; "may be used if benefits exceed the risks" | **Avoid breastfeeding**, or if breastfed, **monitor infant blood levels**; **RID 12–30%** |
| Valproate | Major anomalies; **neural tube defects 6–9%**; intellectual disability; **avoided** | RID 1.5%; **compatible** |
| Carbamazepine | Increased malformations; **avoided** | RID 1–7%; compatible |
| Lamotrigine | No rise in major malformations; **therapeutic drug monitoring** | RID 9.2–18.3%; **safe with monitoring** |

⚠️ For lithium in lactation, the source prints "do **foetal** blood level monitoring". The context is breastfeeding, so this presumably means the **infant**. Flagged, not corrected.

**Pharmacokinetics (Table 6):**
- **Lithium:**
  - levels **fall throughout pregnancy** (higher GFR and fluid volume) and **return to pre-pregnancy levels soon after delivery**;
  - check levels **monthly until 34 weeks, then weekly until delivery**;
  - consider levels **before and 24 hours after delivery** if fluid balance in labour was not maintained;
  - check **frequently in the first postpartum weeks**.
- **Lamotrigine:**
  - levels may **fall by about 50%** (sex steroids; UGT induction), and **folic acid may reduce its effect**;
  - monitor levels **from preconception to 1 month postpartum**.

**Other measures:**
- **Fetal echocardiography** with lithium; anomaly scans at 10–12 and 16–18 weeks.
- **Relapse prevention** in bipolar disorder: **avoid sleep deprivation**, manage stress, ensure adherence, avoid substances, and learn early warning signs.
- **Bipolar depression** (Table 9):
  - SGA monotherapy (olanzapine or another SGA);
  - **use antidepressants with great caution** (postpartum relapse);
  - if severe and unresponsive, **SSRI plus SGA (e.g., fluoxetine plus olanzapine)**.

⚠️ **Discrepancies (not resolved):**
- **Bipolar depression:** Maudsley (above) lists an **SSRI or lamotrigine** for more severe bipolar depression in pregnancy. IPS places SGA monotherapy first, with antidepressants "with great caution".
- **Lithium:** IPS puts lithium **after SGA failure**, rather than equal to switching. Maudsley gives NICE's three options for women already on lithium.
- **Valproate:** IPS's **neural tube defect rate of 6–9%** and Maudsley's **about 10% major malformations** measure different things. Both say avoid.

**Conclusion.** Valproate (about 10% malformations, up to 40% developmental delay) and carbamazepine and topiramate should be avoided. Lithium carries a smaller Ebstein's and cardiac risk than once thought; it can be continued with informed consent, fetal echocardiography, monthly-then-weekly levels and a planned hospital delivery (stopping it in labour). Lamotrigine is the safest anticonvulsant but needs level monitoring. NICE prefers mood-stabilising antipsychotics. Abrupt discontinuation is dangerous because the postpartum relapse risk is very high.
