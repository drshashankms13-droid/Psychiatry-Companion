## Exam answer

**Case summary.** A 26-year-old woman presents to the emergency department after **slashing her wrist following a break-up**. Since then she feels **worthless** and cries incessantly. She describes **frequent fights and anger outbursts** over trivial issues with her boyfriend, during which she **"zones out" (dissociation)**; work is affected and she has been reprimanded. She was **sexually abused by an uncle in childhood**, which returns as **nightmares**, with **easy waking (hyperarousal)**.

**Formulation.** The picture suggests a **personality disorder with a borderline pattern (emotionally unstable personality disorder, borderline type)** and/or **complex post-traumatic stress disorder**, with a possible **current depressive episode**, presenting after **self-harm with suicide risk** [1–3].

### 1. Further clarifications needed in the history

**The act of self-harm (first priority)**
- Method, lethality, intent (wanted to die vs relieve pain), planning, precautions against discovery, alcohol or drugs, who found her, current feelings about surviving.
- Previous self-harm and suicide attempts; current suicidal ideation and plans; access to means.

**Borderline features (pervasive since adolescence?)** [1]
- Fear of abandonment and frantic efforts to avoid it; unstable, intense relationships with idealisation and devaluation.
- Identity disturbance; chronic emptiness; impulsivity (spending, sex, substances, binge eating, reckless driving).
- Affective instability (mood shifts over hours); intense anger; stress-related paranoia or dissociation.
- Onset and stability of these patterns across relationships, family and work.

**Trauma and PTSD/complex PTSD features** [2]
- Details of abuse (age, duration, disclosure, response of family — invalidation?).
- **Re-experiencing in the present** (flashbacks, nightmares), **avoidance**, **sense of current threat** (hypervigilance, startle).
- **Disturbances of self-organisation:** affect dysregulation, persistent negative self-concept (worthless, shame), difficulty sustaining relationships.
- Other trauma (domestic violence in the relationship?).

**Dissociation**
- Nature of "zoning out": amnesia for episodes, depersonalisation, derealisation, identity alteration; triggers; duration.
- Rule out seizures (automatisms, incontinence, post-ictal confusion).

**Mood**
- Pervasive low mood, anhedonia, sleep and appetite change, guilt, hopelessness — duration (depressive episode vs reactive distress).
- Past episodes of elevated mood, reduced sleep need, increased energy (bipolar disorder).

**Other**
- Substance use (alcohol, cannabis, sedatives); eating disorder; ADHD symptoms in childhood.
- Medical history; medications; pregnancy.
- Family history of mood disorder, suicide, substance use.
- Current supports, living situation, safety in the relationship, financial and occupational situation.
- Previous treatment and response; attitude to help.

### 2. Differential diagnosis

| Diagnosis | For | Against / check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Personality disorder, borderline pattern (BPD)** | Self-harm after rejection, anger outbursts, unstable relationship, dissociation under stress, worthlessness, childhood abuse | Must be a pervasive, long-standing pattern, not one crisis [1] |
| **Complex PTSD** | Childhood sexual abuse, nightmares, hyperarousal, worthlessness, relationship difficulties, affect dysregulation | Requires re-experiencing, avoidance, threat **plus** disturbances of self-organisation; self-concept is stably negative; relationships avoided rather than intensely unstable [2] |
| **PTSD** | Nightmares, hyperarousal | Without the self-organisation disturbances [2] |
| **Depressive episode** | Worthlessness, crying | Duration, pervasiveness, biological symptoms [1] |
| **Bipolar II / cyclothymia** | Mood instability, irritability | Distinct episodes of hypomania lasting days; BPD mood shifts are reactive and last hours [1] |
| **Adjustment disorder** | Acute reaction to break-up | Long-standing pattern argues against |
| **Dissociative disorder** | Zoning out | Dissociation confined to stress and anger episodes suggests BPD or complex PTSD |
| **Substance use disorder** | May drive impulsivity and outbursts | Use history, urine screen |
| **Temporal lobe epilepsy** | Episodes of "zoning out" | Stereotyped, unprovoked, automatisms, EEG |

Comorbidity is common — BPD and complex PTSD often co-occur with depression [1, 2].

### 3. Approach to management

**Step 1 — Emergency care**
- Surgical care of the wound; check for tendon or nerve injury.
- **Suicide risk assessment** (method, intent, hopelessness, impulsivity, substance use, supports); **observation level** according to risk.
- Under **MHCA 2017**, a person who attempts suicide is presumed to be under severe stress and the government must provide care (Section 115); register as a medico-legal case per hospital protocol (author's knowledge).
- **Safety plan:** warning signs, coping strategies, people to contact, crisis numbers (**Tele-MANAS 14416**), removal of means.
- Admission only if risk is high or there is a severe comorbid illness; keep it **brief and goal-directed** [1].

**Step 2 — Engagement and psychoeducation**
- Validate her distress; explain the links between childhood trauma, emotion dysregulation, dissociation and self-harm (biosocial model) [3].
- Offer a clear diagnosis in non-stigmatising language; emphasise that these conditions are treatable.

**Step 3 — Psychotherapy (core treatment)**
- **Dialectical behaviour therapy:** individual therapy + skills groups (mindfulness, distress tolerance, emotion regulation, interpersonal effectiveness) + telephone coaching; hierarchy: self-harm first, then therapy-interfering, then quality-of-life behaviours [3].
- **Stage II of DBT** or **trauma-focused therapy** (trauma-focused CBT, prolonged exposure, EMDR, cognitive processing therapy) for PTSD/complex PTSD once self-harm is controlled [3].
- Alternatives: **mentalization-based treatment**, schema therapy, transference-focused psychotherapy [4].

**Step 4 — Pharmacotherapy (adjunctive)**
- Treat a **comorbid depressive episode or PTSD** with an SSRI (sertraline, fluoxetine); **prazosin** may help trauma nightmares (author's knowledge).
- Avoid benzodiazepines; prescribe limited quantities; review regularly [1].

**Step 5 — Social and occupational**
- Address work problems; liaison with employer with consent; graded return.
- Relationship safety — screen for intimate partner violence.
- Family work if appropriate; avoid confrontation with the abuser without her consent.

**Step 6 — Follow-up**
- Regular outpatient care with one coordinating clinician; crisis plan; monitor self-harm and dissociation.

**Conclusion.** This woman's self-harm after a break-up, anger outbursts, dissociation and worthlessness against a background of childhood sexual abuse point to a borderline pattern personality disorder and/or complex PTSD, possibly with depression. Immediate management is medical care, suicide risk assessment and a safety plan; long-term management is structured psychotherapy — DBT, with trauma-focused work once self-harm is controlled — supported by careful pharmacotherapy for comorbid conditions.

## In depth

### Background

- Linehan's biosocial theory: emotional vulnerability × invalidating environment → emotion dysregulation; childhood sexual abuse is reported by 40–70% of people with BPD, but the association is moderate (r = 0.28) [3].
- Judith Herman (1992) proposed "complex PTSD" for prolonged, repeated interpersonal trauma; ICD-11 adopted it as a separate diagnosis, replacing **enduring personality change after catastrophic experience** [2] (Herman attribution from author's knowledge).

### Classification

- **ICD-11 complex PTSD:** the three PTSD core features plus persistent disturbances in **affect regulation**, **self-concept** and **relational functioning** [2].
- **ICD-11 personality disorder:** severity + trait domains (here likely negative affectivity and disinhibition) + optional **borderline pattern** [2].
- **DSM-5:** BPD (5 of 9 criteria); PTSD with dissociative subtype; complex PTSD not a separate diagnosis.

### Distinguishing BPD from complex PTSD (author's synthesis of the literature)

| Feature | BPD | Complex PTSD |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Fear of abandonment | Central | Not central |
| Relationships | Intense, unstable, idealise–devalue | Avoided, difficulty feeling close |
| Self-concept | Unstable, shifting | Stably negative (worthless, defeated) |
| Self-harm | Common, often impulsive | Possible |
| Trauma exposure | Not required | Required |

### Mechanism in detail

- Repeated childhood abuse → disrupted attachment and mentalizing, chronic hyperarousal, shame-based self-concept → dissociation as a defence → emotion dysregulation in adult relationships → self-harm to relieve unbearable affect (operant reinforcement through relief) [3, 4].
- Chain analysis in DBT identifies these links: prompting event (break-up) → interpretation (abandonment) → shame and anger → dissociation → self-harm → relief → shame [3].

### Assessment in detail

| Tool | Use |
| --- | --- |
| SCID-5-PD / IPDE | Personality disorder diagnosis |
| International Trauma Questionnaire (ITQ) | ICD-11 PTSD and complex PTSD |
| Dissociative Experiences Scale | Dissociation |
| Columbia Suicide Severity Rating Scale | Suicide risk |
| PHQ-9 / HAM-D | Depression |

### Management details

- **DBT 24-hour rule:** no telephone coaching for 24 hours after self-harm, to avoid reinforcing it [3].
- **DBT stage II** includes empirically supported exposure for PTSD after behavioural control in stage I [3].
- **MBT** is effective for BPD with self-harm; at 8-year follow-up suicidality was 23% vs 74% with treatment as usual [4].
- Medication evidence in BPD is weak; drugs target comorbidity and symptom domains [1].

### Critical appraisal

- Diagnosing a personality disorder during an acute crisis risks over-diagnosis; confirm the long-term pattern with collateral history [1].
- Labelling may lead to stigmatising care ("attention-seeking"), which increases risk; a trauma-informed frame may be more acceptable to patients.
- Overlap between BPD and complex PTSD is substantial; many patients meet both.

### Indian context

- Disclosure of sexual abuse by a family member is especially difficult in Indian families; confidentiality and her control over disclosure are essential.
- POCSO applies only to minors; legal options for adult survivors of childhood abuse are limited, but she should be informed of support services.
- DBT teams are few; DBT-informed skills groups and trauma-focused CBT are increasingly available in tertiary centres.
- MHCA 2017 Section 115 and the BNS 2023 frame attempted suicide as a health matter, not a crime (BNS from author's knowledge).

### Recent advances

- ICD-11 complex PTSD and the ITQ have improved recognition of trauma-related self-disturbance [2].
- Integrated DBT with prolonged exposure (DBT-PE) for suicidal patients with PTSD and BPD (author's knowledge).
