## Exam answer

**Definition.** A *neurodevelopmental disorder* is one in which the brain is not formed normally from the start: abnormal genetic programming or early environmental insult disrupts proliferation, migration, differentiation, synapse formation or pruning [1]. The **neurodevelopmental hypothesis of schizophrenia** (Weinberger 1987; Murray & Lewis 1987) says that the illness is the late expression of a brain lesion acquired in fetal or early life, unmasked by normal maturation in adolescence. It competes with the Kraepelinian **neurodegenerative** view ("dementia praecox"). The current consensus is a **progressive neurodevelopmental** model: developmental vulnerability plus processes after onset that can worsen outcome [1].

### Mechanism
Genes (CNVs, many common variants) + early insult (obstetric complications, maternal infection, famine) → abnormal neuronal migration and connectivity (first hit) → premorbid soft signs, cognitive and social deficits → adolescent **excessive synaptic pruning** and dopamine/glutamate maturation (second hit) → stress, cannabis, urbanicity, migration (third hit) → aberrant salience from striatal dopamine dysregulation → psychosis → possible neuroprogression (glutamate excitotoxicity, oxidative stress) in a subgroup [1].

### Evidence for and against

| Line of evidence | Supports neurodevelopment | Against / limitations |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Obstetric complications | Pooled OR ≈ 2 (Geddes 1999; Cannon 2002): bleeding, pre-eclampsia, hypoxia [1] | Effect sizes small; publication bias; many exposed never develop illness |
| Prenatal infection, famine | Influenza, rubella, Dutch Hunger Winter (Susser 1996), winter–spring birth excess [1] | Influenza findings not replicated (Crow 1992; Susser 1994) [1] |
| Minor physical anomalies | High-arched palate, low-set ears, dermatoglyphic changes (ectodermal origin) [1] | Non-specific |
| Premorbid deficits | Delayed milestones, low IQ, poor social competence in birth and conscript cohorts (British 1946, Dunedin, Swedish and Israeli conscripts, Northern Finland 1966) [1] | Most children with these signs never develop schizophrenia |
| Imaging at first episode | Ventricular enlargement, smaller hippocampus, cavum septum pellucidum already present before treatment [1] | Progressive grey matter loss after onset suggests a degenerative element |
| Neuropathology | Neuronal disarray, heterotopic neurons, reduced neuropil and spines; **no gliosis** (insult before third trimester) [1] | Migration findings inconsistently replicated [1] |
| Genetics | DISC1, NRG1, DTNBP1, 22q11.2 deletion, C4 complement (pruning), synaptic genes in GWAS; overlap with autism and intellectual disability CNVs [1] | Polygenic, small effects, also overlap with bipolar disorder |
| Course and treatment | Stable cognitive deficit after onset (Heaton 2001) [1] | Treatability, relapses, and DUP effects fit poorly with a static lesion [1] |

### Assessment implications
Developmental history (birth, milestones, schooling), neurological soft signs, IQ, family history, minor physical anomalies; consider 22q11.2 deletion or other CNV testing in patients with intellectual disability, dysmorphism or congenital anomalies.

### Management implications
- Prevention: better obstetric care, maternal nutrition and infection control.
- Early detection: clinical high-risk services and shorter duration of untreated psychosis (DUP).
- Cognitive remediation and vocational rehabilitation for the developmental cognitive deficit.
- Treat early and keep relapses low to limit any neuroprogressive component.

### Key evidence
- **Weinberger 1987; Murray & Lewis 1987:** original statements of the hypothesis.
- **Feinberg 1983:** excessive adolescent synaptic pruning [1].
- **Jones 1994 (British 1946 cohort):** delayed milestones and speech problems in children who later developed schizophrenia [1].
- **Sekar 2016:** C4 complement variants drive synaptic pruning and are associated with schizophrenia risk.
- **Gupta & Kulhara (Indian review):** evidence favours neurodevelopment more strongly, but a neurodegenerative component exists in subgroups [1].

**Conclusion.** The statement is **largely true but incomplete**. Strong convergent evidence places the origins of schizophrenia in abnormal brain development, but the developmental model alone cannot explain adolescent onset, relapse-linked deterioration, response to treatment, and full recovery in a minority. Schizophrenia is best described as a **progressive neurodevelopmental disorder** with heterogeneous subgroups. This view is also more therapeutically optimistic than either pure model, because it supports early intervention and relapse prevention [1].

## In depth

### Background
- **Clouston (1891)** described "developmental insanity". **Southard (1915)** reported cortical anomalies [1].
- **Kraepelin (1896–1919)** grouped *dementia praecox* by its deteriorating course; this formed the neurodegenerative tradition. He and **Bleuler** also noted childhood oddities in later patients [1].
- **Bender (1947)** and **Watt (1978)** described childhood neurological and social abnormalities [1].
- **1987:** Weinberger ("implications of normal brain development for the pathogenesis of schizophrenia") and Murray & Lewis ("Is schizophrenia a neurodevelopmental disorder?") formalised the hypothesis.
- **Late 1990s:** Woods (1998) proposed the *progressive neurodevelopmental* synthesis; Keshavan (1999) proposed a unitary "development, disease and degeneration" model [1].
- **2010s:** genomics (PGC GWAS, CNVs), C4 and pruning, and the developmental risk-factor model (Murray, Howes 2017).

### Epidemiology of risk factors

| Risk factor | Approximate effect |
| --- | --- |
| Family history: first-degree relative | Risk ~10% (vs ~1%); MZ concordance ~45% |
| 22q11.2 deletion | ~25–30% develop psychosis |
| Obstetric complications | OR ≈ 2 [1] |
| Prenatal famine (Dutch Hunger Winter) | About a twofold increase [1] |
| Winter–spring birth | ~5–8% excess |
| Paternal age >55 | ~2–3 fold |
| Urban upbringing, migration | ~2 fold |
| Childhood adversity | OR ≈ 2.8 (Varese 2012) |
| Heavy cannabis use | ~2–4 fold |

Male excess (≈1.4:1) and earlier onset in men (peak 20–29) than women (peak about 5 years later) fit a model in which oestrogen buffers developmental vulnerability [2].

### Classification and criteria
- DSM-5-TR places schizophrenia under "Schizophrenia spectrum and other psychotic disorders", **not** under neurodevelopmental disorders (which include intellectual disability, autism, ADHD, specific learning disorder, motor and communication disorders).
- ICD-11 likewise keeps it in "Schizophrenia or other primary psychotic disorders" (6A20), separate from 6A0 neurodevelopmental disorders.
- The classifications acknowledge developmental antecedents in their text but **do not reclassify**. This is the strongest nosological argument that the statement oversimplifies.
- Childhood-onset schizophrenia (before age 13) is the clearest developmental form. It has more premorbid abnormality, more CNVs and more progressive grey matter loss [1].

### Mechanism in detail
**Normal development** has critical windows. Neurogenesis and migration (reelin, astrotactin) occur in the first and second trimesters. Synaptogenesis peaks in infancy. Pruning runs from childhood into the third decade, with prefrontal pruning last [1].

**Two-hit/three-hit model:**
1. *Early hit:* genetic risk + prenatal insult → subtle cortical dysconnectivity, GABA interneuron (parvalbumin) abnormalities, reduced neuropil.
2. *Late hit:* adolescent pruning (C4-mediated, NMDA-dependent) removes excess synapses. In vulnerable brains this goes too far, causing cortical "disconnection", lower prefrontal glutamate drive to midbrain dopamine neurons, low tonic and excess phasic striatal dopamine, and aberrant salience [1].
3. *Environmental hit:* stress (HPA activation), cannabis and social defeat sensitise dopamine release.

**Glutamate as the bridge:** glutamate guides migration and plasticity in development and pruning in adolescence, and excess glutamate causes excitotoxicity later. This is the proposed "three-hit glutamate" link between development and degeneration [1].

**Why no gliosis?** Gliosis develops only after the third trimester. Its absence suggests the lesion is early, or that cell loss is through apoptosis rather than necrosis (bcl-2 changes) [1].

### Evidence for neurodegeneration (the counter-case)
- Kraepelin's original concept of deteriorating course.
- **Longer DUP** predicts poorer response, more relapse and worse outcome (Perkins 2005), suggesting active "toxicity" of untreated psychosis [1].
- Each relapse brings slower and less complete recovery; about 1 in 6 relapses leads to treatment resistance.
- **Longitudinal MRI:** progressive grey matter loss (frontal, temporal) and ventricular enlargement after onset, greatest in childhood-onset cases and with more relapses. Antipsychotic exposure confounds this (Ho 2011; primate studies) [1].
- **Oxidative stress:** reduced SOD, glutathione and catalase; positive symptoms correlate inversely with SOD [1].
- **Counter-evidence:** absent gliosis, stable cognition after onset (Heaton 2001; Hoff 2005), and full recovery in a minority [1].

### Clinical features in detail — the developmental trajectory

| Stage | Clinical | Pathological |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Premorbid | Delayed milestones, clumsiness, lower IQ, solitary play | Neurodevelopmental lesion |
| Prodromal | Social withdrawal, decline in functioning, attenuated symptoms | Maturational events, pruning |
| Onset/deterioration | Psychosis, functional decline | Neurochemical sensitisation |
| Chronic/residual | Negative and cognitive symptoms | Neuroprogression in some |

Adapted from Gupta & Kulhara [1].

### Assessment in detail
- **Neurological soft signs:** Neurological Evaluation Scale (sensory integration, motor coordination, sequencing).
- **Minor physical anomalies:** Waldrop scale.
- **Premorbid adjustment:** Premorbid Adjustment Scale (Cannon-Spoor).
- **Neurocognition:** MATRICS Consensus Cognitive Battery; deficits of about 1 SD are present at first episode [1].
- **Genetics:** chromosomal microarray in syndromic cases; 22q11.2 FISH.
- **Imaging:** structural MRI to exclude organic causes. Findings are not diagnostic.

### Differential — other models of schizophrenia

| Model | Core idea | How it relates |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Neurodevelopmental | Early lesion, late expression | Explains premorbid signs and onset age |
| Neurodegenerative | Progressive neuronal loss | Explains deterioration in a subgroup |
| Progressive neurodevelopmental | Both | Best fit to current data [1] |
| Dopamine hypothesis (v III, Howes & Kapur 2009) | Final common pathway: presynaptic striatal dopamine | Downstream of developmental risk |
| Glutamate/NMDA hypofunction | Ketamine/PCP model | Bridges development and degeneration [1] |
| Immune/inflammatory | Maternal infection, microglia, C4 | Links prenatal and pruning hits |
| Social defeat / developmental risk-factor model | Adversity sensitises dopamine | Environment acting on development |

### Management details — what the model changes
- **Primary prevention:** antenatal care, prevention of maternal infection (rubella, influenza vaccination), folate and nutrition, reduction of childhood adversity, and public health campaigns about adolescent cannabis.
- **Secondary prevention:** clinical high-risk (CHR) services, mainly CBT and monitoring, and reducing DUP.
- **Tertiary prevention:** sustained antipsychotic treatment to avoid relapses (if relapse is toxic), cognitive remediation, and supported employment.
- **Investigational neuroprotective approaches:** omega-3 fatty acids (positive early trial, Amminger 2010; not replicated in NEURAPRO), N-acetylcysteine, minocycline, sarcosine and other glycine-site agonists [1], and oestrogen/raloxifene in women [2].

### Critical appraisal
- **Strengths:** convergent evidence from epidemiology, genetics, neuropathology, imaging and cohort studies; explains the premorbid phase and onset age; links to autism and intellectual disability through shared CNVs.
- **Weaknesses:**
  - Most findings are **non-specific** (also in bipolar disorder, autism, depression) and have small effect sizes.
  - Many replications failed (migration abnormalities, influenza) [1].
  - The model does not explain **late-onset** schizophrenia (after age 40, more common in women) or **very late-onset** schizophrenia-like psychosis.
  - It struggles with relapse–remission course, response to treatment and **full recovery** in up to a fifth of patients [1,3].
  - A purely developmental label risks **therapeutic nihilism**. Gupta and Kulhara note the combined view is more optimistic [1].
- **Overreach:** calling schizophrenia simply "neurodevelopmental" ignores the heterogeneity: subgroups with strong developmental loading (childhood onset, cognitive impairment) and others with better outcomes and affective features.
- **Indian outcome data** (Madras Longitudinal Study: only 5 of 61 patients continuously ill over 20 years) argue against inevitable deterioration and against a fixed lesion [3].

### Indian context
- Indian contributions include Gupta & Kulhara's critical review (PGIMER), studies of minor physical anomalies and soft signs (NIMHANS, PGIMER), the Madras and Chandigarh long-term outcome studies, and 22q11.2 and genomic work from NIMHANS (e.g., the Accelerator programme for Discovery in Brain disorders using Stem cells (ADBS)) [1,3].
- Public health relevance: maternal undernutrition, perinatal complications and infections are still common in India, so developmental prevention is relevant here.
- Cannabis is culturally accepted in some regions (bhang), so adolescent education matters.

### Recent advances
- **Established:** large-scale GWAS (PGC 2022: 287 loci) implicate synaptic and neurodevelopmental genes; CNV burden overlaps with autism and intellectual disability.
- **Emerging:** brain organoids from patient iPSCs show altered neurogenesis and synaptic pruning (microglia–neuron co-cultures); normative modelling of brain development finds individual-level deviations rather than a uniform lesion.
- **Investigational:** developmental "risk calculators" combining polygenic score, premorbid function and environment; early-life interventions trialled in high-risk offspring.
