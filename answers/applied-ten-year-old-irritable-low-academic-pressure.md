## Exam answer

**Case summary.** A 10-year-old boy has had the following for **1 year**:
- teachers note he is **distracted and irritable**;
- he feels **angry** and **unable to concentrate**;
- he is **restless**, with **frequent sleep disturbance**;
- he spends **a lot of time on the phone**;
- he feels **low and pressurised** by studies, tuition and skating classes;
- he is **not allowed to play with friends**;
- he feels he "**should not have existed**".

### Provisional diagnosis
**Depressive disorder in childhood: a single depressive episode, probably moderate, with passive death wishes.** In DSM-5-TR terms, major depressive disorder.

**Reasons:**
- In children, "**irritable mood**" can replace depressed mood [1].
- He has **low mood**, irritability, anger, poor concentration, restlessness (psychomotor agitation) and sleep disturbance.
- He has **thoughts of not wanting to exist**: passive suicidal ideation.
- There is **functional decline at school**.
- The onset is **recent (1 year)**, a **change from his previous state**. This argues against a primary neurodevelopmental disorder.
- **Psychosocial context:** academic over-scheduling, restricted play and peer contact (loss of rewarding activity), possibly high parental expectations.

Note: if symptoms have been continuous for at least a year with milder severity, **persistent depressive disorder (dysthymia)** applies. It needs **1 year in children** (versus 2 in adults) [1].

**Differential diagnosis:**
| Condition | For / against |
| --- | --- |
| **ADHD** | Distractibility and restlessness are present, **but** onset is at 9 (not since early childhood), there is a definite change from premorbid state, and there are depressive cognitions. These favour depression [1, 3]. Check whether lifelong traits existed |
| **Adjustment disorder with depressed mood** | Clear stressor; but duration over 6 months and the full syndrome argue against |
| **Anxiety disorder (GAD, performance anxiety)** | "Pressurised"; worry; may co-exist |
| **DMDD** | Severe recurrent temper outbursts at least 3 times a week, with persistent irritability between them, onset before 10. Ask about outbursts. **Cannot co-exist with ODD**; may co-exist with MDD [general knowledge; 2] |
| **Bipolar disorder** | Irritability, sleep disturbance and restlessness; check for **episodic** elation, grandiosity, decreased **need** for sleep, family history [1, 2] |
| **ODD** | Anger and irritability, but defiance is not described |
| **Problematic smartphone use / gaming disorder** | Possibly a coping behaviour or a consequence; it disrupts sleep |
| **Specific learning disorder** | Academic struggle could be a primary driver of the pressure |
| **Child abuse or bullying** | Must be asked about directly |
| **Medical** | Hypothyroidism, anaemia, **obstructive sleep apnoea**, medications (steroids, montelukast, isotretinoin) [2] |

### (a) Evaluation
**1. History: interview the child alone and the parents separately [1]:**
- Parents more often report **externalising** symptoms (irritability). Children report **internalising** symptoms (low mood) [1].
- **DSM criteria:** mood, anhedonia, sleep, appetite and weight (growth chart), energy, worthlessness or guilt, concentration, psychomotor change, **thoughts of death**.
- **The "DUMPS" mnemonic [1]:**
  - **D**uration, **D**epressed mood, **D**efiance, **D**istant / withdrawn behaviour;
  - **U**ndeniable drop in school performance;
  - **M**orbid behaviour (may indicate suicidality);
  - **P**essimism;
  - **S**omatic complaints (headache, abdominal pain).
- **Suicide assessment [1]:**
  - ask directly: "Do you ever feel things are so bad that you wish you were dead?"; "Do you ever feel like wanting to hurt yourself?";
  - plans, previous self-harm;
  - **concept of death** (children may not see death as irreversible, which may increase risk);
  - **access to means** (medicines, heights);
  - online exposure to self-harm content.
- **Psychosocial:**
  - daily schedule (school, tuition, skating);
  - **parental expectations**; parenting style; marital discord;
  - **parental depression**;
  - sibling comparisons;
  - **bullying and cyberbullying**; **abuse**;
  - peer relationships; restricted play.
- **Screen use:** hours, content, night-time use, gaming.
- **Developmental and academic history:** to exclude prior ADHD or SLD; teacher reports.
- **Family history:** depression, **bipolar disorder**, suicide.

**2. Mental state examination:**
- play techniques and drawings for a 10-year-old [1];
- affect; depressive cognitions; hopelessness;
- psychotic symptoms (rare; would suggest severe depression or bipolarity).

**3. Physical:** growth, nutrition, thyroid, anaemia; signs of self-harm; tonsillar hypertrophy (OSA).

**4. Rating scales [1, 2]:**
- **CDRS-R** (clinician-rated);
- **MFQ** (child and parent);
- **RCADS** (anxiety and depression);
- **CGAS** (functioning);
- **C-SSRS** (paediatric);
- SDQ;
- Vanderbilt/Conners if ADHD is to be excluded.

**5. Investigations as needed:** CBC, TSH, ferritin. **No routine neuroimaging** [1].

**6. School report** (with consent).

### (b) Management [1, 2]
**Setting:**
- **Outpatient care** if there is no active plan and support is adequate.
- **Hospitalise** if: active suicidal intent or plan; severe depression; catatonia; malnutrition; or "need for removal from a hostile social environment" [1].

**Safety:**
- a **safety plan**;
- **parents keep medicines locked** [2];
- reduce access to means;
- regular monitoring of suicidality.

**Psychoeducation [1]:**
- depression is an illness, not laziness or "attention-seeking";
- address parents' mislabelling ("he's lazy", "manipulative");
- involve the whole family.

**Psychosocial interventions: the core of treatment here:**
- **Reduce academic and activity load:**
  - negotiate tuition and classes;
  - restore **free play and peer contact**;
  - **sleep hygiene**;
  - **screen-time plan** (no phone at bedtime).
  This is **parental counselling**.
- **Family intervention:** realistic expectations; communication; praise; handling irritability; treat parental psychopathology.
- **School liaison:** teacher support; reduce pressure; anti-bullying measures.
- **Psychotherapy:**
  - **mild depression:** supportive therapy or psychological intervention **first**. **No antidepressant** (NICE, AACAP) [2]. Supportive psychotherapy is a pragmatic alternative in India, where CBT and IPT expertise is limited [1].
  - **moderate–severe:** **CBT** (behavioural activation, cognitive restructuring, problem-solving), **IPT-A** (role disputes with parents), family therapy [1, 2].
  - CBT evidence in children is weaker than in adolescents [1].

**Pharmacotherapy (moderate–severe, or if psychotherapy fails or is unavailable) [1, 2]:**
- **Fluoxetine is first line:**
  - **10 mg/day**, increased to **20 mg after 1 week** if tolerated [1, 2];
  - higher doses (40–60 mg) in severe illness or heavier children [2];
  - **FDA-approved from 8 years** [1, 2].
  - **TADS:** fluoxetine 61% response vs placebo 35% (NNT 4); fluoxetine plus CBT 71% [1, 2].
- **Alternatives:** **sertraline** (25–50 mg, up to 200 mg) or **escitalopram** (5–10 mg, up to 20 mg; FDA from 12 years) [2].
- **Monitor:**
  - **treatment-emergent suicidality** (FDA **black-box warning**) [1];
  - **behavioural activation** (irritability, agitation, hypomania), especially at dose increases [1];
  - weekly contact initially.
- **Non-response after an adequate trial:**
  - add psychotherapy;
  - **switch to another SSRI** (TORDIA: switching plus CBT gave 55% response) [2];
  - then augmentation or a different class (mirtazapine, venlafaxine, bupropion) [1, 2].
- **Continuation:** at least **6–12 months after remission** [1]. Taper slowly.
- **ECT:** only for severe depression, suicidality or catatonia. Rare at 10 years; requires MHRB permission under MHCA for minors [1; general knowledge].

**Prognosis:**
- An episode lasts about 7–9 months on average.
- **Recurrence is high** (about 40% within 2 years).
- Risk of **bipolar switch** (about 20–40% in some cohorts) [general knowledge].
- Predictors of good response (TADS): younger age, less chronicity, higher functioning, less hopelessness and ideation, fewer comorbidities [1].

**Conclusion.** This is **childhood depression presenting with irritability, poor concentration and passive death wishes**, set against an over-scheduled, play-deprived routine and excessive screen use. Assessment must interview the child alone, assess suicide risk and screen for ADHD, bipolarity, anxiety, abuse and bullying. Management begins with **safety, psychoeducation and restructuring of family and school demands**, plus psychotherapy. **Fluoxetine** is added for moderate–severe illness, with close monitoring and 6–12 months of continuation [1, 2].

## In depth

### Background
- Childhood depression was long doubted ("masked depression"). DSM-III (1980) accepted adult criteria with developmental modifications.

### Epidemiology [1, 2]
- **Children:** 0.4–2.5% in community samples; **adolescents:** 0.4–8.3% [1].
- **Lifetime prevalence through adolescence up to 20%** [1]. About 15% of young people experience depression by age 18 [2].
- **Equal sex ratio before puberty**; female preponderance after puberty [1].
- **Under-diagnosed and under-treated** in India [1].

### Classification
- Developmental modifications: **irritable mood allowed**; **failure to gain expected weight** instead of weight loss; dysthymia lasting **1 year** [1].

### Mechanism [general knowledge]
- **Genetic:** high familial loading; offspring of depressed parents have 3–4 times the risk.
- **Temperament:** negative affectivity; behavioural inhibition.
- **Stress:** academic pressure; loss of positive reinforcement (no play or friends), the behavioural model of depression (Lewinsohn).
- **Sleep and circadian disruption:** night-time screen exposure (blue light; arousal).
- **Cognitive:** negative attributional style; learned helplessness (Seligman).
- **Neurobiology:** HPA hyperactivity; reduced reward responsivity (ventral striatum); inflammation.

### Clinical features: age differences [1, 2]
- Young people show **more irritability, loss of energy, insomnia and weight change**, and **less anhedonia and concentration problems**, than adults [2].
- Younger children may show **somatic complaints**, separation anxiety, behavioural problems and **rejection sensitivity** [1].
- **Bipolarity indicators [1]:** psychotic symptoms, marked retardation, reverse neurovegetative symptoms (hypersomnia, hyperphagia), irritability, family history of bipolar disorder.

### Assessment: suicidality in children [1]
- Risk is high with **multiple past attempts, persistent ideation and high intent**.
- Also consider: psychological and interpersonal factors; comorbidity; chronicity; risk-taking; abuse; **failure (exams, love)**; access to lethal means.

### Differential: depression vs ADHD in this case
| Feature | Depression | ADHD |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Onset | Recent (1 year) | Before 12, usually early childhood |
| Course | Episodic, a change from baseline | Trait-like, persistent |
| Mood | Low, irritable | Normal (may be frustrated) |
| Cognitions | Worthlessness, "should not exist" | Absent |
| Sleep | Insomnia, broken sleep | Sleep-onset difficulty |
| Concentration | Reduced from previous level | Always poor, situationally variable |

### Management details: TORDIA and switching [1, 2]
- **TORDIA:** 334 adolescents aged 12–18 with SSRI-resistant depression were switched to another SSRI or venlafaxine, with or without CBT.
  - Switching plus CBT gave the best results (55% vs 41%).
  - An SSRI switch was as effective as venlafaxine, with fewer side effects [2].
  - **61% remitted** by 72 weeks [1].
- **Switching from fluoxetine:** stop immediately (long half-life) and increase the new drug slowly. From other SSRIs, **cross-taper** [1].

### Evidence table
| Study | Finding |
| --- | --- |
| TADS [1, 2] | Fluoxetine 61%, combination 71%, CBT alone not efficacious vs placebo 35% (12 weeks); combination had the fewest suicidal events |
| ADAPT [2] | No benefit from adding CBT to fluoxetine |
| Network meta-analysis of 14 antidepressants, 34 trials, 5,260 participants [1] (Cipriani 2016, *Lancet*: general knowledge for the citation) | **Only fluoxetine** significantly better than placebo |
| Cochrane 2021 [2] | Antidepressant effects "small and unimportant" |
| Escitalopram RCTs (12–17 years) [1] | Superior to placebo |
| Vortioxetine paediatric RCT [2] | Not better than placebo |
| Agomelatine (7–11 years) [2] | Better than placebo; similar to fluoxetine (unlicensed) |

### Critical appraisal
- Effect sizes of SSRIs in young people are modest. The placebo response is high.
- The **black-box warning** reduced prescribing, and some studies linked that reduction with increased suicides (ecological data; contested) [general knowledge].
- CBT evidence is mixed in children; it is better in adolescents [1].
- Few Indian trials exist.

### Indian context
- **Academic pressure and over-scheduling** (tuition culture; competitive exams) are recognised drivers of child distress [general knowledge].
- **NSPS 2022:** mental-wellbeing curricula in schools; School Health Ambassadors [general knowledge for the link].
- **MHCA 2017:**
  - minors are admitted under **s87** (on application by the nominated representative, with Board review within 72 hours);
  - **ECT is prohibited in minors except with MHRB permission** (s95(2)) [general knowledge; verify].
- **Tele-MANAS (14416).**

### Recent advances
- **Digital CBT** for adolescents (SPARX, a serious game).
- **Physical activity interventions.**
- **Screen-time guidelines:** IAP recommends limited recreational screen time for children aged 5–10 [general knowledge].
- **Esketamine** trials in adolescents (investigational).
