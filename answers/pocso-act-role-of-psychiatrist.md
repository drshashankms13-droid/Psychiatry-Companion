## Exam answer

**Introduction.** **WHO definition of child sexual abuse (CSA):** "the involvement of a child in sexual activity that he or she does not fully comprehend, is unable to give informed consent to, or for which the child is not developmentally prepared, or that violates the laws or social taboos of society" [1].
- **Prevalence:**
  - globally, about **19.7% of females and 7.9% of males** experience sexual abuse before 18;
  - Indian estimates range from **4–41% (girls) and 4–57% (boys)**;
  - **53%** of Indian children report some form of abuse (MWCD 2007 study) [1].
- **Why a new law was needed:** before 2012, CSA was prosecuted under IPC Sections 375, 354 and 377, which **did not protect boys**, did not define "modesty" and did not define "unnatural offences". The only specific law was the **Goa Children's Act 2003** [1].

**The Protection of Children from Sexual Offences (POCSO) Act, 2012** [1, 2]:
- passed 20 June 2012; in force **14 November 2012**; **amended 2019**;
- defines a **child as anyone under 18**;
- **gender-neutral** for victims;
- **child-friendly procedures** at every stage.

### Key provisions (relevant to clinicians) [1, 2]
| Section | Provision |
| --- | --- |
| 3–12 | Offences: **penetrative sexual assault (3–4)**, **aggravated** penetrative sexual assault (5–6), sexual assault (7–8), aggravated sexual assault (9–10), **sexual harassment (11–12)**; **pornography (13–15)**; abetment and attempt (16–18) |
| 5 / 9 | **"Aggravated"** includes:<br>• perpetrator a public servant, relative or person in trust or authority;<br>• gang assault; repeated assault; weapons;<br>• grievous hurt;<br>• **taking advantage of the child's mental or physical disability**;<br>• **child below 12**;<br>• **causing the child to become mentally ill**;<br>• pregnancy;<br>• previous conviction.<br>2019 amendment added: assault causing death; during a natural calamity; administering hormones for early sexual maturity |

**Rules:** POCSO Rules 2012, replaced by the **POCSO Rules 2020** [general knowledge]. They provide for:
- registers of **interpreters, translators, special educators and experts** (Rule 3);
- a **support person** assigned by the **CWC** (Rule 4);
- **emergency medical care** (Rule 5), which includes "a **referral or consultation for mental or psychological health** or other counselling";
- compensation [2].

**JJ Act 2015 link:** a sexually abused child is a "**child in need of care and protection**". The police must inform the **CWC within 24 hours**, and the CWC decides on care, rehabilitation and the support person [1, 3].

### Role of the psychiatrist
**1. Mandatory reporting (legal duty):**
- On **knowledge or reasonable apprehension**, report to police or SJPU. Clinicians are **not exempt** (Section 19), and **confidentiality does not override reporting**.
- Failure to report is punishable (Section 21).
- **Document** the disclosure verbatim, with date, time, and to whom it was reported. The **TOG 2020** says POCSO cases must be seen **in person** at first [4].
- Tell the child and family honestly that you must report, and why.

**2. Emergency medical and psychological care (Section 19(5), Section 27, Rule 5):**
- Ensure medical care (injuries; HIV, STI and pregnancy prophylaxis) is not delayed by the legal process.
- Treat acute distress; ensure safety and suicide risk assessment.

**3. Psychiatric assessment:**
- Use a **developmentally appropriate, non-leading**, trauma-informed interview. Avoid repeated interviews, which cause **secondary victimisation**. Coordinate with police so the child is interviewed as few times as possible.
- Assess psychiatric sequelae:
  - **PTSD**, depression, anxiety, dissociation;
  - **sleep disturbance, enuresis, regression**;
  - school problems;
  - **sexualised behaviour**;
  - self-harm, substance use;
  - eating disorders [1].
- Assess pre-existing vulnerabilities: ID, ASD, disability (an aggravating factor).
- **Do not "validate" or investigate the allegation.** That is a forensic or judicial function. Record findings objectively.

**4. Expert role in the legal process (Section 26(2), Section 39, Rule 3):**
- act as **expert or special educator** to help a child with intellectual disability, communication disorder or severe trauma give a statement;
- provide **reports** to the CWC and Special Court;
- give **testimony** (the child's mental state; capacity to testify; the effect of trauma on memory and narrative);
- assess **competence to testify**;
- **recommend accommodations:** breaks, a screen, video-link, a support person, in-camera hearing.

**5. Treatment and rehabilitation:**
- **Trauma-focused CBT (TF-CBT)** is first-line for PTSD.
- Child–parent psychotherapy and play therapy for young children.
- Treat depression and anxiety; medication when indicated (SSRIs).
- **Family work:**
  - caregiver distress, guilt, blame, stigma;
  - support the non-offending parent;
  - intrafamilial abuse (incest) requires child protection planning and possibly a **shelter home** [2].
- **School reintegration**: confidentiality; prevent stigma.
- Long-term follow-up: risk of revictimisation, sexual health, later psychopathology.

**6. Special situations:**
- **Adolescent consensual relationships** ("romantic cases": about **1 in 4** POCSO cases, nearly half involving girls aged 16–18) [1]. The **age of consent is 18**, so reporting is still mandatory. The psychiatrist's role is supportive and advocacy-oriented: avoid harm to both adolescents.
- **Pregnancy in a minor:** an **MTP Act vs POCSO conflict**. Reporting is mandatory, which deters minors from seeking safe abortion [1]. The Supreme Court (2022) allowed providers to withhold the minor's identity in the police report on request [general knowledge; check].
- **Children with intellectual disability:** the Act uses chronological age. **Adults with a "mental age" below 18 are not covered** (Supreme Court, *Eera v State*, 2017) [1; case name general knowledge].
- **Child perpetrators:** handled under the **JJ Act** (Juvenile Justice Board). For heinous offences at age **16–18**, the JJB conducts a **preliminary assessment** of mental and physical capacity, understanding of consequences, and circumstances, **with help from psychologists or psychiatrists** [3].

**7. Prevention and advocacy:**
- training (teachers, health workers);
- **body-safety / personal-safety education** in schools;
- awareness of the law;
- **advocacy for reform** (mandatory reporting dilemmas; adolescent sexuality).

### Contentions and criticisms [1]
1. **Mandatory reporting** has no timeline or process that considers the child's or family's wishes.
   - It can cause **extreme distress** and **secondary victimisation** without guaranteeing protection.
   - In intrafamilial abuse it can threaten **economic security** (abuser as breadwinner) and isolate the family.
   - It may **deter disclosure and help-seeking**.
2. **Criminalisation of adolescent romantic relationships:**
   - the age of consent is 18 (NFHS-4: 39% of girls had sex before 18);
   - families file complaints, and boys are punished;
   - the law is gender-neutral for victims but not, in practice, for perpetrators.
3. **Conflict with the MTP Act** (confidentiality).
4. **Definition of child ignores intellectual disability** (mental age).
5. **Implementation gaps:**
   - few Special Courts; low conviction rates; delays beyond 1 year [general knowledge];
   - shortage of trained experts and support persons.

**Conclusion.** Under POCSO, the psychiatrist is a **mandatory reporter, clinician, expert and advocate**. Every contact must balance **legal duty** with the **best interest of the child**: minimise secondary trauma, provide evidence-based trauma care and family support, assist the court as an expert (Section 39), and work with the CWC (JJ Act). Psychiatrists should also contribute to reform of mandatory reporting and of adolescent consensual-relationship provisions.

## In depth

### Background
- **UN Convention on the Rights of the Child** (ratified by India in 1992).
- **Constitution** Articles 15(3), 39(e), 39(f), 45, 47 [3].
- **Commission for Protection of Child Rights Act 2005** (NCPCR/SCPCR monitor POCSO implementation).
- **MWCD 2007 study** on child abuse: 53% reported some sexual abuse [1].
- **Criminal Law (Amendment) Acts 2013 and 2018.**
- **POCSO amendment 2019** (death penalty; higher minimum sentences).
- **Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita 2023** replaced the IPC; POCSO continues [general knowledge].

### Epidemiology
- **Stoltenborgh 2011 global meta-analysis:** 18–20% of females and about 8% of males [1].
- NCRB reports tens of thousands of POCSO cases annually; numbers have risen with reporting [general knowledge; check current NCRB].
- **Perpetrators** are mostly **known to the child** (family, neighbours, acquaintances) [general knowledge].

### Classification of CSA
- **Contact** (penetrative, non-penetrative) vs **non-contact** (exposure, pornography, online grooming).
- **Intrafamilial** (incest) vs **extrafamilial**.
- **Online CSA:** grooming, sextortion, CSAM.

### Mechanism: why CSA harms
- **Traumagenic dynamics model (Finkelhor & Browne):**
  - traumatic sexualisation;
  - betrayal;
  - powerlessness;
  - stigmatisation.
- **Neurobiology:**
  - HPA dysregulation;
  - amygdala hyperreactivity;
  - hippocampal changes;
  - altered reward and threat processing.
- **Moderators:**
  - severity, duration, relationship to perpetrator;
  - **parental support after disclosure** (the strongest protective factor).

### Clinical features [1]
- **Physical indicators:**
  - unexplained genital injury;
  - recurrent vulvovaginitis, UTI, STI;
  - **pregnancy**;
  - dysuria;
  - anal complaints.
- **Behavioural indicators:**
  - **regression** (enuresis, milestones);
  - **clinginess**;
  - sleep disturbance;
  - eating disorders;
  - school problems;
  - **age-inappropriate sexual behaviour**.
- **Long-term outcomes:**
  - PTSD, depression, anxiety;
  - BPD traits, dissociation;
  - substance use;
  - revictimisation;
  - **incest** carries the most severe long-term trauma [1].

### Assessment
- Interview principles:
  - **open-ended, non-leading questions**;
  - child-friendly room;
  - rapport first;
  - allow free recall;
  - avoid "why" questions;
  - use developmentally appropriate language;
  - **anatomical dolls with caution**.
- **NICHD investigative interview protocol** (forensic interviewers) [general knowledge].
- **Tools:** Trauma Symptom Checklist for Children (TSCC), Child PTSD Symptom Scale (CPSS), Kiddie-SADS.
- **Documentation:**
  - verbatim quotes;
  - demeanour;
  - who was present;
  - consent and assent;
  - reporting details.

### Differential: things that mimic abuse
- Lichen sclerosus.
- Accidental straddle injuries.
- Normal sexual development and exploration.
- Coached or false allegations (rare; custody disputes).
- Psychiatric illness causing sexual preoccupation.

### Management: the "7-step process" for clinicians (NIMHANS seminar framework [1])
1. Recognise.
2. Respond (listen, believe, reassure).
3. Medical care.
4. Report (police/SJPU).
5. Document.
6. Coordinate with CWC, support person, DCPU.
7. Treat and follow up.

### Critical appraisal
- **Mandatory reporting evidence:** international data show increased reports, but also many unsubstantiated reports and possible deterrence of help-seeking. There is no Indian outcome evaluation.
- **Age of consent:** the **Law Commission (22nd, 2023)** advised against lowering it, but suggested **guided judicial discretion** for 16–18-year-olds in tacitly approved relationships [general knowledge; check].
- **System capacity:** a shortage of child psychiatrists and psychologists to serve as Section 39 experts.

### Indian context
- **Institutions:**
  - **CWC** (JJ Act);
  - **DCPU**;
  - **Childline 1098**, now integrated with **ERSS 112** [general knowledge];
  - **One Stop Centres**;
  - NCPCR **e-box** complaints.
- **Telepsychiatry:** the TOG requires initial in-person assessment for POCSO survivors and perpetrators [4].
- **MHCA 2017:** minors' treatment requires the nominated representative (guardian). Conflicts arise when the guardian is the alleged abuser; the CWC or court decides.

### Recent advances
- **Child-friendly courts** and vulnerable witness deposition centres.
- **Online safety:** POCSO Section 15 (storage of CSAM). The Supreme Court (2024) held that viewing or storing CSAM is an offence [general knowledge; check].
- **Trauma-informed care** training for police and judiciary.
