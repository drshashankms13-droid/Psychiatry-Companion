## Exam answer

**Definition (scope as given by the source).** The textbook frames psychiatric care in oncology as covering six areas [1]:
- psychological factors in cancer risk and progression;
- psychiatric disorders in adults with cancer;
- psychiatric issues in specific cancers;
- psychiatric aspects of cancer treatments;
- psychiatric (psychosocial and drug) interventions;
- survivor issues.

### Why it matters [1]
- About **1.7 million** new US cancer cases and nearly **600,000** deaths were expected in 2017. Death rates have fallen **25% since 1991**.
- The number of people living with cancer is expected to **double, from 1.3 to 2.6 million, between 2000 and 2050**. More survivors means more long-term psychosocial need.

### Does the mind cause cancer? (Applications 1: risk and progression) [1]
- Studies of life events, depression and personality have produced **conflicting results**. Associations are found, not causation.
- **Confounders:**
  - unhealthy behaviours (smoking, alcohol, inactivity, poor diet, delayed screening);
  - differences in study design and in how distress is defined;
  - publication bias.
- **Proposed mechanisms:**
  - distress reduces **natural killer cell** function;
  - depression dysregulates the **HPA axis**, raising cortisol and inflammation.
- **Key data:**
  - **Batty 2017** (16 cohorts, more than 163,000 people, GHQ-12): distress was associated with colorectal, prostate, pancreatic and oesophageal cancer and leukaemia.
  - **Bergelt 2006** (Danish cohort): accumulated life events were linked to unhealthy lifestyle, **not** to cancer incidence.
- The evidence linking depression to **progression is stronger than to incidence**, though both are inconsistent.
- Depression may worsen outcome through poorer pain control, poorer adherence and **less desire for life-sustaining therapy**.
- **Bottom line:** "well-designed studies have not confirmed that psychological processes cause cancer". We intervene for **quality of life and adherence**, not survival.

### Psychiatric morbidity in cancer (Applications 2: assessment) [1]
**What shapes adjustment:**
- disease site, symptoms, course, prognosis and treatments;
- prior adjustment;
- threat to developmental tasks;
- cultural and spiritual attitudes;
- available support;
- personality and coping;
- past losses.

| Condition | Key points from the source |
| --- | --- |
| **Depression** | At least **3 times** general-population rates. Meta-analysis (Mitchell 2011; 70 studies, n = 10,071): depression **16.3%**, MDD **14.9%**, minor depression **19.2%**, dysthymia **2.7%**, **adjustment disorder 19.4%**, any mood disorder including anxiety **38.2%**. Risk factors: **younger age**, poor support, **insecure attachment**, **advanced disease**. The **female excess is not consistently seen** in cancer. |
| **Demoralization** | Not a DSM-5 entity. Hopelessness and helplessness from loss of meaning and purpose. Clinically significant in **13–18%**. Risk: single, isolated, jobless, poorly controlled symptoms, untreated anxiety or depression. |
| **Suicide** | **Twice** the general-population incidence. RR **12.6 in the first week after diagnosis**, falling to **3.1 in the first year**. Higher with male sex, white race, older age, **poor-prognosis cancers** (gastro-oesophageal, hepatobiliary, lung) and **head and neck cancers**. 6–8% endorse PHQ-9 item 9; only **11–33% of these have intent**, so screen for both ideation and intent. |
| **Wish to hasten death** | Consensus definition (Balaguer 2016): a reaction to suffering with no way out other than accelerating death, distinct from accepting death. Linked to hopelessness, depression and physical distress. 17% near death vs 2% with prognosis over 6 months. |
| **Anxiety** | A normal response at crisis points (diagnosis, new treatment, recurrence, scans). **Fear of recurrence:** 73% have some, nearly 50% moderate to high; caregivers have more. In interview studies about **10%** have an anxiety disorder (specific phobia, panic, GAD most common). **Claustrophobia** can block MRI and radiotherapy; **needle phobia** can delay chemotherapy or surgery. **Conditioned (anticipatory) nausea** after emetogenic chemotherapy. |
| **Anxiety mimics** | **Akathisia** (antipsychotics, prochlorperazine, metoclopramide), **pulmonary embolism**, **hypoxia**, uncontrolled pain. |
| **PTSD** | About **5%** current, 15% lifetime. Under DSM-5 Criterion A, the **diagnosis or uneventful treatment of cancer alone does not qualify**; a sudden catastrophic medical event (e.g., waking during surgery, anaphylaxis) does. Risks: prior trauma, advanced disease, recent end of treatment, younger age, low support, poor staff interactions. |
| **Mania** | **Corticosteroids** are the commonest cause; risk is more than **4 times** higher (Fardet 2012). Insomnia and agitation are commoner than full mania. Higher dose and past psychiatric history increase risk. Monitor patients with bipolar disorder. |
| **Delirium** | **5–30%**; **25–50%** on admission; up to **85% terminally**. Often missed because of fluctuation and the hypoactive subtype, and mistaken for depression, dementia or anxiety. |

**Cancer-specific causes of delirium (Table 22–3):**
- primary or secondary brain tumours; leptomeningeal metastases;
- non-convulsive status; paraneoplastic **limbic encephalitis** (small-cell lung cancer); **PRES**; post-irradiation oedema;
- opportunistic infection; drug or substance withdrawal; opioids, steroids, anticholinergics, antineoplastics;
- **hypercalcaemia**; hyperviscosity; thromboembolism; **cerebral venous thrombosis** (asparaginase, tamoxifen, thalidomide, erythropoietin).

**Depression vs somatic symptoms:**
- An **inclusive** diagnostic approach "may be the most sensitive and reliable".
- Use the **pervasiveness and reactivity** of mood to tell disorder from normal adjustment.
- Rule out **hypoactive delirium**.

**Barriers to diagnosis:**
- doctors find it hard to separate somatic symptoms, and doubt that interventions work;
- patients fear medication, stigma and that "negativity" will harm the cancer;
- cultural differences in how distress is expressed.

### Screening [1]
- **North American guidelines:**
  - screen at the first visit and then periodically with the **ESAS-r, the Distress Thermometer or the PHQ-2**;
  - a score **above 4/10** triggers full assessment;
  - monitor with the PHQ-9, GAD-7, BDI, BSI, CES-D or HADS.
- **Critique (Meijer 2013):**
  - treatment effects are modest, and the only trial of screening (Maunsell 1996) showed no benefit;
  - so **routine screening is premature**.

### Psychosocial management (Applications 3: intervention) [1, 2]
**Principles:**
- Explore how the patient **understands the illness and prognosis**, symptoms, stressors and relationships, then build an **individualised plan**.
- **Collaborative care** embedded in oncology: nurse or social-worker care managers with specialist back-up (e.g., problem-solving therapy).
- **Stepped care** (Cancer Care Ontario/NICE) [2]:
  1. Everyone: assessment, support, psychoeducation, monitoring.
  2. Subthreshold or mild depression: low-intensity psychosocial help (no antidepressants, because risk outweighs benefit).
  3. Moderate to severe or non-responding: medication and/or high-intensity therapy, within collaborative care.
  4. Complex cases (suicide risk, psychosis, severe psychosocial risk): inpatient admission and/or brain stimulation therapies.

**Psychotherapy:** the choice depends on the type and severity of distress, stage, treatment status, function and **reflective capacity**.

| Phase | Therapies the source names |
| --- | --- |
| Recently diagnosed | **Psychoeducation, problem-solving, relaxation, CBT** |
| Advanced disease | **Existential, life-narrative, dignity-conserving, meaning-centred** therapies; **CALM** (Managing Cancer and Living Meaningfully) |
| Group | **Supportive–expressive group therapy** (Spiegel 1989): reduces isolation; addresses existential concerns. Kissane 2007: **protective against depressive disorders** in metastatic breast cancer |
| End of life | **Dignity therapy:** a 1-hour legacy-document interview reduces suffering and low mood (Chochinov 2005). **Meaning-centred group therapy** improved spiritual well-being and meaning compared with supportive groups (Breitbart 2010) |
| Adjunct | **Music therapy:** anxiety, pain, fatigue, QoL |

**Evidence for psychotherapy:**
- **Akechi 2008:** in advanced cancer, depression scores improved in **non-clinically depressed** samples but **not** in those diagnosed with depression.
- **Li 2017:** CBT, social support, problem-solving and brief psychodynamic therapy helped **up to 12 weeks only**.
- **Survival:** early positive studies (Spiegel 1989, Fawzy) were **not replicated** by more rigorous trials (Kissane 2007, Spiegel 2007). Aim for QoL and coping, not longer life.

**Pharmacotherapy:**
- **Choosing a drug:** consider past response, comorbidity, **interactions**, **route** (mucositis or bowel obstruction), how fast onset is needed given prognosis, symptom profile (pain, insomnia, hot flushes) and side effects.
- **Thrombocytopenia:** **avoid IM injections and restraints**.
- **Antidepressants:**
  - prescribed to **15.6%** worldwide (7.4% in Asia; 22.6% in breast cancer);
  - the evidence in depressed cancer patients is **limited and low quality**.
- **Specific agents:**
  - **mirtazapine** for anorexia–cachexia, nausea or vomiting;
  - **psychostimulants** in the terminally ill, because they act quickly;
  - **benzodiazepines** for acute anxiety, insomnia and to boost antiemetics.
- **Delirium:**
  - **methylene blue or thiamine** for ifosfamide encephalopathy;
  - in palliative delirium, **risperidone or haloperidol did worse than placebo (Agar 2017)**;
  - the commonest reason for **palliative sedation** is delirium, usually with continuous **midazolam**;
  - no cause is found in up to 50% of terminal delirium.

**Conclusion.** Psycho-oncology's value is in finding and treating the **large burden of depression, adjustment disorder, demoralization, delirium and suicide risk**, which peaks right after diagnosis. It also manages interactions between psychotropics and cancer treatment. [Certain, per the source] Psychological states have **not been shown to cause cancer**, and psychotherapy **does not prolong survival**. The goals are quality of life, coping, adherence and a dignified death, delivered through collaborative, stepped care.

## In depth

### Cancer-related symptoms that interact with mood [1]
**Pain:**
- present in **49–57%** with curable cancer and **56–75%** with advanced cancer;
- **depression affects pain more than pain affects depression** (Wang 2012, longitudinal);
- risk factors for **opioid misuse**: age 45 or younger, past substance misuse, mental illness, legal problems, family history of SUD;
- opioids are first-line for moderate to severe pain in active disease; consult addiction specialists for problem behaviours;
- **40% of survivors** have neuropathic or post-treatment pain; **TCAs, duloxetine, gabapentin and pregabalin** help as adjuvants;
- cannabinoids lack evidence;
- **hypnosis and CBT coping-skills training** reduce cancer pain.

**Cancer-related fatigue:**
- tiredness out of proportion to effort and **not relieved by rest**;
- affects up to **100%** during treatment and **30–40%** of survivors;
- **Mechanism:** proinflammatory cytokines.
- **Course:**
  - with **radiotherapy**, fatigue peaks near the end of treatment;
  - with **chemotherapy**, it worsens in the first week after each cycle and accumulates.
- **Treatment:**
  - first rule out anaemia, nutritional deficiency and drug causes;
  - **exercise, psychological interventions, or both** work; **methylphenidate, modafinil and paroxetine did not** (Mustian 2017);
  - reserve stimulants or low-dose steroids for **advanced disease, severe fatigue, or fatigue with depression**;
  - aim for **at least 150 minutes a week** of aerobic activity, supported by behaviour-change techniques.

### Drug interactions [1]
- **CYP3A4:** most chemotherapy is metabolised by CYP3A4.
  - **St John's wort and modafinil** induce it and may **reduce chemotherapy efficacy**;
  - **fluoxetine, sertraline, paroxetine and fluvoxamine** inhibit it and may **raise toxicity**.
- **Tamoxifen:** a prodrug activated to **endoxifen by CYP2D6**.
  - **Avoid strong CYP2D6 inhibitors** (paroxetine, fluoxetine, high-dose sertraline, bupropion, duloxetine).
  - The data are mixed: Chubak 2016 found no overall rise in recurrence, but there was a signal with paroxetine.
- **Hot flushes** (which threaten adherence to hormonal therapy):
  - SSRIs: **citalopram 10 [unit misprinted; see note] daily**, paroxetine 10 mg;
  - SNRIs: venlafaxine 75 mg, desvenlafaxine 100 mg;
  - gabapentin 900 mg daily, pregabalin 75 mg twice daily;
  - clonidine 0.1 mg a day (less effective); acupuncture.
  - ⚠️ The source prints "citalopram **10 g** daily". This is evidently a misprint for mg. Please confirm against the cited reference (Leon-Ferre 2017) rather than rely on my reading.

### Specific cancers (selected) [1]
- **Brain tumours:**
  - metastases are 2–3 times more common than primary tumours; glioblastoma median survival is **14 months**;
  - **frontal** tumours cause disinhibition, apathy and slowing (which mimics depression); **temporal** tumours affect memory and language;
  - use **benzodiazepines sparingly** (they worsen disinhibition);
  - late cognitive decline after radiotherapy.
- **Breast cancer:**
  - young women carry a disproportionate burden;
  - **pregnancy after treatment is not discouraged** (no difference in disease-free survival);
  - aromatase inhibitors cause more sexual dysfunction than tamoxifen;
  - depression is commoner than in the general population for more than 5 years after diagnosis;
  - **BRCA1/2:** 55–85% lifetime breast cancer risk; psychiatrists help with decisions on prophylactic surgery and fertility.
- **Prostate cancer:**
  - depression in 10–30% after diagnosis; **depression before diagnosis predicts less definitive treatment and worse survival** (Prasad 2014);
  - suicide risk rises when recommended treatment is not received;
  - **androgen deprivation therapy (ADT) raises depression risk with duration**; erectile dysfunction in 73–95%;
  - **couples-based** intervention.
- **Testicular cancer:** anxiety is commoner than depression; suicide risk is 20% higher.
- **Bladder and kidney cancer:** depression 77.5%, anxiety 69.3% and PTSD 25.2% (Yang 2016).
- **Pancreatic cancer:**
  - 5-year survival is 8%;
  - **depression often precedes diagnosis** (up to 50% have psychiatric symptoms 6 months before; OR 4.6 vs other GI cancers);
  - a direct tumour effect is suspected but unproven.
- **Head and neck cancer:**
  - suicide **3 times** the general rate (hypopharyngeal about **12 times**, laryngeal 5 times);
  - disfigurement and loss of speech or swallowing;
  - **alcohol and nicotine withdrawal** on admission;
  - guilt about substance use;
  - hypothyroidism after neck radiotherapy.

### Treatment phases [1]
- **Chemotherapy:**
  - most feared effects: nausea, vomiting, **alopecia** (fear of hair loss leads some to refuse treatment);
  - "chemo brain".
- **Radiotherapy:**
  - lying still for 5–10 minutes can be blocked by claustrophobia, panic or PTSD;
  - fatigue peaks after treatment;
  - brain radiotherapy causes more fatigue and late necrosis or leukoencephalopathy.
- **Haematopoietic stem-cell transplantation:**
  - **50% delirium** in the first 30 days (Fann 2011);
  - 31% depressed at 6–7 weeks;
  - **chronic GVHD treated with prednisone** predicts survivor distress.

### Survivorship [1]
- More than **14.5 million** US survivors.
- **Issues:**
  - fear of finishing treatment and of recurrence;
  - re-entry into normal life; guilt; body and role changes;
  - job and insurance discrimination; infertility.
- **Protective factors:** good cancer care, **active coping**, social support, **meaning and purpose** (Rowland and Massie 2010).
- **PTSD** in fewer than 10% of survivors.
- **Cognitive change** after chemotherapy or hormonal therapy in **15–61%**. The mechanisms are toxicity, cytokines and endocrine therapy. Cognitive and compensatory training has low-quality evidence.

### Physician-assisted death (context) [1]
- Legal in the Netherlands, Belgium, Luxembourg, Colombia (printed "Columbia" in the source), Switzerland, Canada and five US states.
- It accounts for **0.3–4.6%** of deaths there; **70% are cancer patients**.
- The motive is mainly **loss of autonomy and dignity, not pain**.
