## Exam answer

**Introduction.** The statement "the time has come to legalise cannabis in India" merges **three separate policy choices**, and a good answer separates them:
1. **Decriminalisation:** removing *criminal* penalties for personal possession and use while keeping sale and trafficking illegal. Examples: Portugal (2001, all drugs); several European states.
2. **Legalisation with regulated supply:** a licensed commercial or state market with age limits, taxation and product controls. Examples: Uruguay (2013, state-controlled); Canada (Cannabis Act, 17 October 2018); many US states.
3. **Medical and industrial (hemp) access:** prescription cannabinoids; low-THC hemp cultivation [1].

The international context has moved. In **December 2020 the UN Commission on Narcotic Drugs**, on WHO advice, removed cannabis from **Schedule IV** of the 1961 Single Convention, recognising medical value. India voted in favour [1; India vote: general knowledge].

### The Indian legal position
- **NDPS Act 1985:**
  - criminalises production, possession, sale and trafficking of **ganja (flowering tops) and charas (resin)**;
  - penalties are graded by "small" vs "commercial" quantity (small: ganja 1 kg; charas 100 g) [general knowledge; check];
  - **leaves and seeds are excluded** from the definition, so **bhang** sits outside it and is regulated by state excise laws [1].
- **Section 27:** consumption is punishable (up to 6 months for cannabis). **Section 64A** gives immunity to addicts who volunteer for treatment [general knowledge].
- **Hemp:** Uttarakhand (2018), Uttar Pradesh, Madhya Pradesh and Himachal Pradesh have frameworks or pilots for industrial and medical hemp [general knowledge; check status].
- **Cultural use:** religious bhang and ganja (Shivratri, Holi). The **Indian Hemp Drugs Commission (1893–94)** found "no evidence of any weight" of harm from moderate use [2].

### Burden in India
- **Magnitude of Substance Use in India (AIIMS–MoSJE, 2019):**
  - about **2.8% (3.1 crore) current cannabis users**;
  - about **0.66% (about 72 lakh) with problem use/dependence** [general knowledge; check].
- **Treatment gap** for substance use disorders above 70% (NMHS 2015–16).

### Arguments for (decriminalisation and/or regulation)
1. **Harms of criminalisation:**
   - arrests and criminal records fall disproportionately on poor and marginalised youth;
   - prison overcrowding (NDPS undertrials);
   - stigma, which deters people from seeking treatment [1].
2. **Public health framing:** treating use as a health issue (Portugal) increased treatment uptake and reduced HIV among people who inject drugs [1].
3. **Regulated supply** offers:
   - quality control (THC:CBD labelling);
   - an end to adulteration and synthetic substitutes;
   - age limits;
   - tax revenue for treatment [1].
4. **Medical use** has real indications, so research is easier under a regulated framework:
   - **CBD (Epidiolex)** for Dravet and Lennox–Gastaut syndromes;
   - **nabiximols** for MS spasticity;
   - **dronabinol and nabilone** for chemotherapy nausea and HIV cachexia;
   - chronic neuropathic pain (modest effect) [general knowledge].
5. **Historical and cultural legitimacy**; enforcement resources freed for trafficking.
6. **Inconsistency:** alcohol and tobacco are legal but cause far greater mortality.

### Arguments against
1. **Psychosis:** cannabis is a **component cause** of psychosis [2]:
   - **dose-dependent** (Swedish conscripts: OR 2.4 for ever-use, about 6 for heavy use; Zammit 2002 estimated **13% of schizophrenia cases preventable**);
   - **adolescent onset** carries greater risk (Dunedin: 10% vs 3% schizophreniform disorder at 26);
   - onset about **2.7 years earlier**;
   - **high-potency, low-CBD** products are worst (Di Forti 2014/2019).
2. **Dependence** in about **1 in 10 users** (1 in 6 adolescent starters); cannabis withdrawal syndrome; amotivation; cognitive impairment with early heavy use.
3. **Normalisation:** legalisation sends a message of harmlessness [2].
   - US data after commercial legalisation:
     - rising heavy and daily use;
     - higher THC potency;
     - more emergency visits for cannabis hyperemesis and paediatric edibles;
     - more cannabis-related psychosis and schizophrenia (Danish attributable fraction rose to about 15% in young men; Hjorthøj 2023) [general knowledge].
4. **Road traffic injuries** from impaired driving.
5. **India-specific risks:**
   - weak regulatory and enforcement capacity;
   - **very limited addiction treatment services**;
   - young population;
   - commercial lobbying (as with tobacco and alcohol);
   - poly-substance use;
   - early initiation [1].
6. **Commercialisation:** profit depends on heavy users. The tobacco and alcohol industries show how marketing expands harm.

### What should the national position be? (A balanced proposal)
1. **Decriminalise personal possession and use of small quantities.** Replace criminal penalties with **administrative diversion to assessment and treatment** (a Portugal-style "dissuasion commission"). Strengthen and apply **NDPS Section 64A**.
2. **Do not commercialise recreational supply now.** If states pilot it, use **state-controlled, not-for-profit** models (Uruguay-type) with:
   - THC caps;
   - CBD requirements;
   - **minimum age 21**;
   - **advertising ban**;
   - plain packaging;
   - high taxation earmarked for treatment.
3. **Develop medical cannabinoid regulation:**
   - CDSCO approval of evidence-based products (CBD for epilepsy);
   - prescription-only;
   - pharmacovigilance;
   - ICMR-funded RCTs.
4. **Allow industrial hemp** with THC below 0.3% under licensing.
5. **Prevent and treat:**
   - school and youth campaigns stressing **adolescent and psychosis risk**;
   - screening and brief interventions in primary care (Tele-MANAS, DMHP, NAPDDR);
   - expand **Drug Treatment Clinics**.
6. **Monitor:** a national surveillance system covering prevalence, potency, psychosis admissions, emergency visits, road traffic injuries and youth initiation, with sunset and review clauses.

**Conclusion.** **Decriminalisation of use is justified now** on public-health and justice grounds. **Commercial legalisation is not**, because the psychosis and dependence evidence and India's weak regulatory and treatment capacity make it premature. A **phased, health-led, evidence-monitored reform** is the defensible national position. It should treat cannabis like tobacco and alcohol, regulated for harm minimisation, not promoted.

## In depth

### Background
- **Cannabis sativa/indica.** Main compounds:
  - **Δ9-THC:** CB1 partial agonist; psychoactive; psychotogenic;
  - **CBD:** CB1 negative allosteric modulator and 5-HT1A agonist; anxiolytic, possibly antipsychotic [2].
- **History in India:**
  - Atharva Veda references;
  - Indian Hemp Drugs Commission 1893–94;
  - India resisted inclusion of cannabis in the 1961 Single Convention, securing a **25-year transition**, after which the NDPS Act 1985 followed under US pressure [general knowledge].
- **International treaties:** 1961 Single Convention; 1971 Psychotropic Substances Convention; 1988 Trafficking Convention. India is a signatory to all three.

### Epidemiology
- **World Drug Report:** about 220–230 million users worldwide; cannabis is the most widely used illicit drug [general knowledge; check].
- **India (2019 national survey):** 2.8% current users; Uttar Pradesh, Punjab, Sikkim, Chhattisgarh and Delhi highest; 0.66% problem users. Bhang use is common in some states [general knowledge].
- **Potency trend:** THC content in street cannabis in Europe and the US has risen several-fold since the 1990s (sinsemilla/skunk) [2].

### Classification of policy models
| Model | Example | Key features |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Prohibition | India (NDPS), most of Asia | Criminal penalties for use and possession |
| De-penalisation / toleration | Netherlands coffee shops | Illegal but not enforced |
| **Decriminalisation** | Portugal 2001; Czech Republic | Administrative sanctions; diversion to health |
| Medical-only | Germany (pre-2024), Thailand (2022 → partial rollback) | Prescription access |
| **State-regulated legalisation** | Uruguay 2013 | Government controls production, pharmacy sales, registry, monthly limits |
| **Commercial legalisation** | Canada 2018; US states | Licensed private retail; taxation |
| Partial legalisation (home grow, clubs) | **Germany 2024** | Possession up to 25 g; cannabis social clubs [general knowledge] |

### Mechanism: why cannabis harms the adolescent brain [2]
- The endocannabinoid system (CB1, CB2, anandamide, 2-AG) regulates:
  - neuronal proliferation and migration;
  - axon guidance;
  - interneuron positioning;
  - synaptogenesis.
- **Adolescent exposure** disrupts maturation of prefrontal and limbic circuits.
- **THC** disrupts retrograde endocannabinoid control of VTA dopamine neurons. This causes mesolimbic dopamine excess and **aberrant salience** (Kapur), and reduces frontal theta coherence.

### Clinical features of cannabis-related harm
- **Acute intoxication:** euphoria, conjunctival injection, tachycardia, appetite increase, impaired time perception, anxiety and panic, transient psychotic symptoms.
- **Cannabis use disorder;** withdrawal (irritability, insomnia, appetite loss, craving; days 1–14).
- **Cannabis-induced psychotic disorder;** earlier and worse schizophrenia.
- **Cognitive impairment:** memory, attention, IQ decline with persistent adolescent use (Dunedin, Meier 2012).
- **Cannabis hyperemesis syndrome.**
- **Amotivation.**

### Assessment: data a policymaker should demand
- Prevalence and incidence by age; potency testing; treatment demand.
- Psychosis first-episode incidence attributable to cannabis.
- Criminal-justice data (NDPS arrests for personal use).
- Cost–benefit modelling.

### Differential: how the debate differs from alcohol and tobacco
| Issue | Cannabis | Alcohol | Tobacco |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Mortality | Low direct mortality | High (liver, injury, cancer) | Very high |
| Psychosis risk | Clear, dose-dependent | Withdrawal and hallucinosis | None |
| Dependence | About 9–10% | About 15% | About 30–70% |
| Legal status in India | Prohibited (bhang legal) | Legal (prohibition states) | Legal (COTPA) |

### Management: treatment system needed alongside reform
- Screening (ASSIST, CUDIT-R) and brief intervention.
- **Psychosocial treatment:** MET plus CBT (MTP, Cannabis Youth Treatment trial); contingency management.
- **No approved pharmacotherapy.** Options under trial:
  - gabapentin;
  - N-acetylcysteine (adolescents);
  - nabiximols agonist replacement;
  - FAAH inhibitors.
- Psychosis care: early intervention services; cessation improves outcome.

### Evidence table
| Study | Finding |
| --- | --- |
| Andréasson 1987, *Lancet* | Swedish conscripts: OR 2.4 (ever), about 6 (heavy) for schizophrenia [2] |
| Zammit 2002, *BMJ* | Dose–response; 13% of schizophrenia preventable [2] |
| Arseneault 2002, *BMJ* (Dunedin) | Use by 15 years: 10% vs 3% schizophreniform disorder at 26 [2] |
| Moore 2007, *Lancet* | Meta-analysis: OR 1.4 for any psychotic outcome; dose–response; about 14% of UK psychotic outcomes attributable [2] |
| Di Forti 2019, *Lancet Psychiatry* (EU-GEI; general knowledge) | Daily high-potency use: about 5× odds of psychotic disorder; 30% of first-episode psychosis in London, 50% in Amsterdam, attributable |
| Hall & Lynskey 2016/2020 (general knowledge) | Commercial legalisation in the US: increased adult use, potency, emergency visits |
| Hughes & Stevens 2010 (Portugal) | Decriminalisation: no large rise in use; reduced HIV and imprisonment; increased treatment |

### Critical appraisal
- **Causation:** the psychosis association satisfies several Bradford Hill criteria:
  - strength (moderate);
  - consistency;
  - temporality;
  - dose–response;
  - biological plausibility;
  - experimental evidence (IV THC induces transient psychosis).

  Residual confounding and **reverse causation** (self-medication) are not fully excluded. Mendelian randomisation suggests a **bidirectional** effect [general knowledge].
- **Policy evidence is young:** Canadian and Uruguayan outcomes are only a few years old, and US state comparisons are confounded.
- **Decriminalisation and legalisation have different evidence bases.** Portugal shows decriminalisation is safe; commercial legalisation is less clear.
- **Source note:** the user-supplied decriminalisation essay is a secondary, unreferenced synthesis. Primary sources should be cited in the exam.

### Indian context
- The **Indian Psychiatric Society and AIIMS NDDTC** have voiced caution about legalisation [Likely; check].
- **Judicial activity:** a PIL in the Delhi High Court (Great Legalisation Movement, 2019) challenged cannabis criminalisation; not resolved [general knowledge; check].
- **NAPDDR (National Action Plan for Drug Demand Reduction)** and **Nasha Mukt Bharat Abhiyaan (2020)** are the government's demand-reduction frameworks.
- **NDPS amendments (2021)** dealt with drafting errors. No liberalisation of cannabis [general knowledge].

### Recent advances
- **Germany partially legalised** adult possession and home cultivation (April 2024) [general knowledge].
- **Thailand** delisted cannabis as a narcotic (2022) and then moved to re-restrict recreational use (2024–25) [general knowledge; check].
- **US federal rescheduling** of cannabis to Schedule III was proposed in 2024 [general knowledge; check status].
- **THC potency caps** are increasingly proposed as a public-health tool.
