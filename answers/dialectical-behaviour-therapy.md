## Exam answer

**Definition.** Dialectical behaviour therapy (DBT) is a **multimodal cognitive-behavioural treatment** developed by **Marsha Linehan** (University of Washington, late 1970s onwards) for chronically suicidal, self-harming women with **borderline personality disorder (BPD)** [1]. It combines behavioural change strategies with **acceptance and validation**, Eastern mindfulness and Western contemplative practice, held together by a **dialectical** philosophy [1]. It is the psychotherapy with the most empirical support for BPD [1].

### Theory

**1. Dialectics** — reality is interrelated and always changing; for every thesis there is an antithesis, and therapy seeks a **synthesis**. The fundamental dialectic is **acceptance versus change**: accept the patient as she is, while working relentlessly for change [1].

**2. Biosocial theory** — BPD is a disorder of the **emotion regulation system** [1]:

Emotional vulnerability (high sensitivity, high reactivity, slow return to baseline)
× **invalidating environment** (criticising, trivialising or punishing emotional expression; intermittently reinforcing extreme displays)
→ **pervasive emotion dysregulation**
→ self-harm, impulsivity, anger, dissociation, unstable relationships and identity — used to regulate emotion
→ further invalidation (a transactional cycle).

### Structure — four modes (all required for "DBT") [1]

| Mode | Function | Format |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Individual therapy** | Motivation; chain analysis; apply skills to the patient's problems | Weekly, 50–60 minutes |
| **Group skills training** | Acquire skills — a class, not process therapy | Weekly, about 2 hours, 4–10 patients, two co-leaders |
| **Telephone coaching** | Generalise skills to real life | Brief calls (under 10 minutes); **24-hour rule** — no coaching for 24 hours after self-harm |
| **Therapist consultation team** | Support therapists; maintain adherence; prevent burnout | Weekly, 1–2 hours |

Usual duration: **1 year** (patient agreement); therapy stops after **four consecutive missed sessions** [1].

### Four skills modules [1]

1. **Core mindfulness** — observe, describe, participate; non-judgemental, one-mindful, effective; integrate "emotion mind" and "reasonable mind" into **"wise mind"**.
2. **Distress tolerance** — survive crises without making them worse: distraction, self-soothing, improving the moment, **radical acceptance**.
3. **Emotion regulation** — identify and label emotions, reduce vulnerability, increase positive experiences, **opposite action**.
4. **Interpersonal effectiveness** — ask for what one needs, say no, keep relationships and self-respect.

### Treatment hierarchy and stages [1]

**Targets in stage I (in order):** (1) life-threatening and self-injurious behaviour → (2) therapy-interfering behaviour → (3) quality-of-life-interfering behaviour → (4) increasing behavioural skills.

**Stages:** I behavioural control → II emotional experiencing (including exposure for PTSD) → III ordinary problems of living → IV completeness and joy.

### Core techniques [1]

- **Diary cards** — daily ratings of mood, urges, self-harm; set the session agenda.
- **Chain (behavioural) analysis** — vulnerabilities → prompting event → links (thoughts, emotions, sensations, actions) → problem behaviour → consequences; then **solution analysis**.
- **Validation** — six levels, from attentive listening to radical genuineness.
- **Dialectical strategies** — devil's advocate, metaphor, "making lemonade out of lemons", extending.
- **Contingency management** — warmth for skilful behaviour, matter-of-fact response to problem behaviour.

### Evidence

- **First RCT 1991**: DBT superior to treatment as usual for women with BPD; manuals published 1993; second positive RCT 1999 made DBT the first "evidence-based" treatment for BPD [1].
- More than **10 RCTs** in several countries; **Cochrane review (2012)** — treatment of choice for BPD [1].
- Meta-analysis of **16 studies (8 RCTs)**: largest effects on **suicidal and self-injurious behaviour** [1].
- Adapted with evidence for substance use disorders, eating disorders (binge eating), depression in older adults, PTSD after childhood sexual abuse, and **adolescents (DBT-A)** [1].

**Conclusion.** DBT is a structured, principle-driven therapy that treats BPD as a disorder of emotion regulation, teaching skills within a validating relationship and a clear hierarchy of targets. It has the strongest evidence base of any psychotherapy for BPD, especially for reducing self-harm and suicidal behaviour, and has been adapted widely beyond BPD.

## In depth

### Background

- Linehan found that standard CBT's focus on change made suicidal patients feel invalidated, provoking anger, dropout and self-harm, while therapists burned out [1]. She added acceptance and validation, skills groups, phone coaching and a consultation team [1].
- **Behavioral Tech** (2004) disseminates training; DBT-Linehan Board of Certification certifies therapists; the revised skills manual was published in **2014** [1].
- DBT belongs to the "third wave" of behavioural therapies, alongside ACT and mindfulness-based approaches.

### Epidemiology of the target population

- Most people with BPD report non-suicidal self-injury and multiple suicide attempts; lifetime suicide mortality is about **50 times** the general population [1].
- 40–70% report childhood sexual abuse, but meta-analysis shows only a moderate association (r = 0.28) — abuse is neither necessary nor sufficient [1].

### DBT's mapping of BPD criteria [1]

| Domain | Criteria |
| --- | --- |
| Emotion dysregulation | Affective instability, anger |
| Behavioural dysregulation | Impulsivity, self-injury |
| Cognitive dysregulation | Dissociation, paranoia |
| Interpersonal dysregulation | Chaotic relationships, fear of abandonment |
| Self-dysregulation | Identity disturbance, emptiness |

### Assumptions and agreements [1]

**About patients:** they are doing the best they can; they want to improve; they need to do better and try harder; they must solve their problems even if they did not cause them; their lives are currently unbearable; they must learn new behaviours in all contexts.

**About therapy:** patients cannot fail in DBT — the therapy or therapist can fail the patient; therapists need support; the relationship is between equals.

**Consultation team agreements:** meet weekly; discuss cases by the treatment hierarchy; accept dialectical philosophy; consult with the patient rather than for her; therapists may differ and may observe their own limits; seek non-pejorative interpretations; all therapists are fallible [1].

### Levels of validation [1]

1. Listening and observing
2. Accurately reflecting experience
3. Articulating unverbalised emotions and thoughts
4. Validating in terms of past learning or biology
5. Validating in terms of the current context
6. Radical genuineness

### Opposite action — worked example

Every emotion has an action urge (anger → attack; fear → escape; sadness → withdraw; shame → hide; guilt → repair). If the emotion is **not justified** by the facts, act opposite to the urge (e.g. approach instead of avoid); if it is justified, use problem solving [1].

### Ethical issues [1]

- Risk disclosed in group — two leaders allow one to assess risk outside.
- Four-miss rule — the clinic stays responsible until the patient is referred elsewhere.
- Telephone coaching — confidentiality and limits of phone risk assessment.
- Consulting with other providers vs teaching the patient to advocate for herself.

### Adaptations [1]

| Adaptation | Features |
| --- | --- |
| DBT for adolescents (DBT-A) | Families in skills groups; target invalidating environment; 6 months; "walking the middle path" skills |
| DBT for substance use | Adds dialectical abstinence and attachment strategies |
| DBT for eating disorders | Binge eating, bulimia; **radically open DBT** for over-controlled anorexia |
| DBT for PTSD | Prolonged exposure integrated in stage II |
| DBT skills-only groups | Transdiagnostic emotion dysregulation (preliminary evidence) |

### Critical appraisal

- Whether all four modes are necessary is unproven; dismantling studies are limited [1].
- Many trials compare with treatment as usual; versus other structured treatments (MBT, TFP, general psychiatric management) effects are often similar.
- DBT is resource-intensive (team, phone coaching, training), which limits use in low-resource settings.
- Effects on self-harm are clearer than on global functioning, depression or diagnosis.

### Indian context

- Few certified DBT teams; skills groups and adapted, shorter programmes are more feasible in Indian hospitals and colleges.
- Families often join treatment; DBT-A's family skills model suits Indian practice.
- Mindfulness has cultural resonance with Indian contemplative traditions, which may aid acceptance.
- Telephone coaching needs clear boundaries and may be supplemented by Tele-MANAS (14416) for crises.

### Recent advances

- Standalone DBT skills training for transdiagnostic emotion dysregulation [1].
- Multi-site trial of DBT-A for preventing suicide attempts in high-risk youth (ongoing at the time of the textbook) [1].
- Digital and app-based DBT skills coaching are being evaluated (author's knowledge).
