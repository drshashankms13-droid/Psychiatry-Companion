## Exam answer

**Introduction.** Tobacco is the leading preventable cause of death worldwide. A lifelong smoker has about a **50% chance of dying prematurely** from a smoking-related disease [1]. In India:
- **GATS-2 (2016–17):** about **28.6% of adults (about 26.7 crore)** use tobacco. 10.7% smoke and **21.4% use smokeless tobacco** (gutka, khaini, zarda, betel quid). Use is far higher in men (42.4%) than women (14.2%).
- **NMHS 2015–16:** **tobacco use disorder 20.9%** of adults, with a **treatment gap of 91.8%** [2].
- Tobacco causes about 13 lakh deaths a year in India.

**COTPA** is the **Cigarettes and Other Tobacco Products (Prohibition of Advertisement and Regulation of Trade and Commerce, Production, Supply and Distribution) Act, 2003**. It is India's principal tobacco control law. It was enacted in 2003 and followed India's signing of the **WHO Framework Convention on Tobacco Control (FCTC)**, which India ratified in 2004 [3].

### Why tobacco is addictive (mechanism) [1]
- Inhaled nicotine reaches the brain in seconds and binds **α4β2\* nicotinic acetylcholine receptors** in the **ventral tegmental area**, causing **dopamine release in the nucleus accumbens shell** (reward).
- Glutamate release is enhanced. With chronic exposure, GABA inhibitory tone falls while glutamate excitation persists, which sensitises dopamine neurons.
- **Neuroadaptation:** receptor desensitisation leads to **up-regulation** of binding sites. During abstinence (e.g., overnight), desensitised receptors recover, causing **craving and withdrawal**, which smoking relieves.
- Other smoke constituents **inhibit MAO-A and MAO-B**, adding to addictiveness. **Conditioned cues** and stress maintain use.
- **CYP2A6** metabolism rate affects consumption.
- **Quit rates:** 70% want to quit and about 40% try each year, but only about **3% succeed** annually without help. 80% of self-quitters relapse within a month [1].

### Key provisions of COTPA 2003 [3]

| Section | Provision |
| --- | --- |
| **4** | **Prohibition of smoking in public places** (workplaces, hospitals, schools, public transport, restaurants, etc.). Designated smoking areas permitted under rules (Smoke-free Rules 2008). Fine up to **₹200** |
| **5** | **Ban on direct and indirect advertising, promotion and sponsorship** of tobacco products (exceptions at the point of sale and on packs). Penalty: imprisonment up to 2 years and/or fine up to ₹1,000 (first offence); up to 5 years and ₹5,000 (subsequent) |
| **6(a)** | **No sale to persons under 18**, and no sale by minors |
| **6(b)** | **No sale within 100 yards of educational institutions** (rules, 2009) |
| **7–10** | **Mandatory health warnings** on all tobacco packs: **pictorial warnings covering 85% of both principal display areas** (from 2016; rules 2014, amended 2022 with the Quitline number); specified language and size |
| **7(5)** | Nicotine and tar content display (not notified) [3] |
| **11–31** | Testing laboratories, enforcement, powers of entry and seizure, offences and penalties; offences by companies |

**Commencement** [3]:
- Sections 4, 5 and 6(a): 1 May 2004.
- Sections 7–10 (warnings): 2007–08, with pictorial warnings from 2009.
- Section 6(b) rules: 2009.

### The wider tobacco-control framework in India
- **WHO FCTC** (ratified 2004) and the **MPOWER** package:
  - **M**onitor use and prevention policies.
  - **P**rotect from smoke.
  - **O**ffer help to quit.
  - **W**arn about dangers.
  - **E**nforce advertising bans.
  - **R**aise taxes.
- **National Tobacco Control Programme (NTCP, 2007–08):**
  - State and district tobacco control cells.
  - Tobacco cessation centres (TCCs).
  - IEC; school programmes; enforcement squads; laboratory testing.
- **FSSAI regulation (2011):** banned tobacco and nicotine in food products, which led most states to ban **gutka and pan masala with tobacco**.
- **Prohibition of Electronic Cigarettes Act (PECA), 2019:** bans production, import, sale and advertising of e-cigarettes and heat-not-burn devices.
- **Taxation:** GST 28% + compensation cess (moving to 40% GST from 2025). Tax still accounts for a smaller share of retail price for bidis than WHO's recommended 75%.
- **Cessation services:** **National Tobacco Quitline (1800-11-2356)**; **mCessation** ("Quit Tobacco" SMS, 011-22901701); TCCs in medical colleges; NRT available over the counter.
- **Other measures:** "Tobacco-Free Educational Institutions" guidelines; OTT and film rules requiring anti-tobacco warnings and disclaimers (2012, 2023).

### Role of the psychiatrist
- **Screen** all patients (the 5 As: Ask, Advise, Assess, Assist, Arrange). Smoking rates in SMI are 2–3× those in the general population.
- **Treat tobacco dependence:**
  - **Pharmacotherapy:** NRT (patch + short-acting), **varenicline** (most effective; safe in psychiatric patients, as shown in EAGLES), **bupropion**.
  - **Behavioural support:** brief advice, counselling, MI, CBT.
  - **Combination** gives the best outcomes.
- **Drug interactions:** smoking induces **CYP1A2**. **Clozapine and olanzapine levels rise sharply on quitting** (reduce dose by about 25–50% and monitor levels). Fluvoxamine also matters.
- **Advocacy:** smoke-free psychiatric hospitals; enforcing COTPA in institutions.

### Implementation gaps
- **Weak enforcement:** Section 4 violations are common; fines are small (₹200); few challans except in some states (Karnataka, Kerala, Gujarat and Punjab account for about 75% of people fined).
- **Section 5:** surrogate advertising (pan masala, elaichi brands); point-of-sale advertising; OTT and film depictions.
- **Section 6:** sales to minors and near schools continue; **single-stick (loose) cigarette sales** are permitted.
- **Smokeless tobacco** is less regulated in practice, and gutka bans are evaded through twin packs.
- **COTPA Amendment Bill 2020 (draft)**, not yet enacted, proposed:
  - raising the legal age to 21;
  - banning loose sales and designated smoking areas;
  - higher penalties.
- **Cessation infrastructure** is limited compared with need.

**Conclusion.** COTPA 2003, with FCTC-aligned measures (smoke-free public places, advertising ban, youth access restrictions, and large pictorial warnings), is the backbone of India's tobacco control. Combined with NTCP, taxation, the gutka and e-cigarette bans and cessation services, it has contributed to a **fall in adult tobacco use from 34.6% (GATS-1, 2009–10) to 28.6% (GATS-2, 2016–17)**. Weak enforcement, low penalties, surrogate advertising, loose sales and a large smokeless-tobacco burden remain. Strengthening and passing the COTPA amendments, raising taxes, and **expanding cessation, including in psychiatric settings**, are the next steps.

## In depth

### Background
- **1975:** Cigarettes (Regulation of Production, Supply and Distribution) Act, which required a statutory warning.
- **2001:** the Supreme Court (*Murli S. Deora v. Union of India*) banned smoking in public places.
- **2003:** COTPA enacted.
- **2004:** India ratified the **WHO FCTC**.
- **2008:** Prohibition of Smoking in Public Places Rules (effective 2 October 2008).
- **2009 onward:** pictorial warnings; 85% from 2016 after litigation.
- **2007–08:** NTCP launched.
- **2011:** FSSAI food regulations (gutka bans).
- **2016:** plain-pack and large-warning debates.
- **2019:** PECA.

### Epidemiology
- **GATS-2 (2016–17):**
  - **28.6%** of adults use tobacco (about 26.7 crore).
  - Smokeless 21.4%; smoking 10.7%.
  - Men 42.4%, women 14.2%.
  - About 3 in 10 adults exposed to second-hand smoke at work.
- **GYTS-4 (2019):** tobacco use among students aged 13–15 was 8.5%.
- **NFHS-5 (2019–21):** tobacco use 38% in men and 9% in women (15+).
- **NMHS:** tobacco use disorder 20.9%; treatment gap 91.8% [2].
- **Psychiatric populations:** smoking prevalence is much higher in schizophrenia (about 60%) and bipolar disorder.

### Classification and criteria
- **ICD-11:** disorders due to use of nicotine (6C4A): harmful pattern, dependence, withdrawal.
- **DSM-5-TR:** tobacco use disorder; tobacco withdrawal.
- **Fagerström Test for Nicotine Dependence** (0–10); Fagerström for smokeless tobacco. NMHS used Fagerström [2].

### Mechanism in detail [1]
| Element | Detail |
| --- | --- |
| Receptors | α4β2\* (dependence); α3β4 (cardiovascular); α7 (cognition, sensory gating) |
| Reward | Dopamine in the NAcc shell; lowered reward threshold |
| Neuroadaptation | Desensitisation; receptor up-regulation; withdrawal when receptors resensitise |
| Other constituents | MAO inhibition by acetaldehyde condensation products |
| Metabolism | CYP2A6 (nicotine to cotinine). Slow metabolisers smoke less |
| Genetics | CHRNA5-A3-B4 variants (heaviness of smoking, lung cancer risk) |
| Conditioning | Cues, rituals, stress relief |

### Clinical features in detail
- **Withdrawal:** irritability, anxiety, low mood, poor concentration, restlessness, ↑ appetite and weight, insomnia, craving. Peaks in the first week and lasts 2–4 weeks.
- **Smokeless tobacco:** oral cancer (India has a very high burden), leukoplakia, cardiovascular disease, adverse pregnancy outcomes.

### Assessment in detail
- Quantity, type (smoked or smokeless), time to first use after waking (the strongest dependence marker), previous quit attempts, withdrawal, motivation (stages of change), psychiatric comorbidity, other substances.
- Biochemical verification: exhaled CO; cotinine.

### Differential: policy instruments and effectiveness
| Measure | Effect |
| --- | --- |
| Tax increases | Most effective: about a 4–5% fall in demand per 10% price rise in LMICs |
| Smoke-free laws | ↓ exposure; ↓ heart attacks; denormalisation |
| Advertising bans (comprehensive) | ↓ initiation |
| Graphic warnings | ↑ quit intentions |
| Cessation services | Increase quit rates |
| Youth access restrictions | Modest alone |

### Management details: cessation pharmacotherapy
- **NRT:** patch 21 mg (more than 10 cigarettes/day) tapering over 8–12 weeks, plus gum or lozenge 2–4 mg for cravings.
- **Varenicline:** α4β2 partial agonist. 0.5 mg daily ×3 days → 0.5 mg twice daily ×4 days → 1 mg twice daily for 12 weeks. Quit date at week 1–2.
- **Bupropion SR:** 150 mg daily ×3 days → 150 mg twice daily for 7–12 weeks. Contraindicated with seizures and eating disorders.
- **Smokeless tobacco:** behavioural support ± NRT or varenicline (weaker evidence).
- **Psychiatric patients:** EAGLES (2016, n≈8,000) found no significant increase in neuropsychiatric adverse events with varenicline or bupropion vs NRT or placebo. Varenicline was most effective. **Adjust clozapine and olanzapine doses** after quitting.

### Critical appraisal
- **COTPA strengths:** comprehensive in scope; FCTC-aligned; strong pictorial warnings (India ranks among the highest in warning size).
- **Weaknesses:**
  - Low penalties.
  - Designated smoking areas (not FCTC-compliant).
  - Loose-cigarette sales.
  - Poor enforcement capacity.
  - Industry interference and litigation.
  - Bidis and smokeless products under-taxed.
  - Weak coverage of digital and OTT advertising.
  - The amendment bill has been pending since 2020.
- **Evidence of impact:** GATS shows a fall in prevalence, though attributing it to COTPA specifically is difficult.
- **Psychiatry's blind spot:** tobacco is often ignored in psychiatric settings ("self-medication" myths). Quitting improves depression and anxiety (Taylor 2014 BMJ meta-analysis).

### Indian context
- **NTCP:** state and district tobacco control cells; TCCs; tobacco-free school and village campaigns; enforcement squads.
- **State action:** Karnataka, Kerala, Gujarat and Punjab lead COTPA fines. Several states ban gutka, hookah bars, or loose cigarettes.
- **NIMHANS** runs tobacco cessation clinics and has trained professionals (e.g., through the NIMHANS Tobacco Cessation Centre).

### Recent advances
- **Established:** 85% pictorial warnings with Quitline number (2022 rules); PECA 2019; mCessation.
- **Proposed or emerging:**
  - COTPA Amendment 2020 (age 21, ban on loose sales, end to designated smoking areas).
  - **Plain packaging.**
  - Tobacco endgame strategies.
  - Higher taxes under GST restructuring.
  - Cytisine (cheap, effective cessation drug; WHO-recommended 2024).
  - Digital cessation apps.
