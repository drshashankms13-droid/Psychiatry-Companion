## Exam answer

**Introduction.** ECT is **the most effective acute treatment for severe depression**, and older adults are the group in whom it works **best**. It is often **first-line rather than last-resort** in late life [1]. Severe late-life depression carries several risks:
- psychotic features (nihilistic and somatic delusions);
- **food and fluid refusal**;
- high suicide lethality;
- catatonia;
- medication intolerance.

All of these make a **rapid, reliable** treatment essential [1].

### Why ECT suits the elderly [1]
- **Efficacy:**
  - remission rates in older adults are typically **60–90%**;
  - in the CORE consortium, remission was higher in over-65s (about 90%) than in younger adults [general knowledge; O'Connor 2001].
  - Predictors of response that are common in old age: **psychotic depression**, psychomotor retardation, melancholic features, shorter episode duration.
- **Speed:** response often within **2–3 weeks**, which is life-saving when a patient refuses food or is suicidal.
- **It bypasses pharmacokinetic problems:** no hepatic or renal accumulation, no anticholinergic load, no hyponatraemia, no drug interactions.
- **Medication intolerance and treatment resistance** are common in the elderly.
- **Comorbid neurological disease:** ECT improves depression in **Parkinson's disease** (and transiently motor symptoms) and in **dementia** (also agitation and psychosis) [1].

### Indications in late life
| Primary (first-line) | Secondary |
| --- | --- |
| Psychotic depression | Failure of 2 or more antidepressant trials |
| Severe suicidality | Intolerance of medication |
| Food and fluid refusal, dehydration, poor physical state | Previous good response to ECT |
| Catatonia | Patient preference |
| Severe agitation or stupor | Depression in Parkinson's disease or dementia |
| Previous good response | Bipolar depression in older adults |

### Pre-ECT assessment
- **Medical:**
  - cardiovascular disease (recent MI, arrhythmia, heart failure, aortic stenosis);
  - **cerebrovascular disease** (recent stroke, aneurysm);
  - raised intracranial pressure (the only near-absolute contraindication);
  - respiratory disease; osteoporosis; dentition;
  - anticoagulation; diabetes; glaucoma.
- **Investigations:** CBC, electrolytes (Na, K), renal function, glucose, ECG, chest X-ray as indicated, **CT/MRI brain** (vascular lesions; space-occupying lesion), echocardiography if indicated.
- **Cognitive baseline:** MMSE/MoCA, plus a test of autobiographical memory.
- **Anaesthetic review:** ASA grade; drugs to stop or adjust (lithium dose, benzodiazepines, anticonvulsants, theophylline).
- **Consent and capacity:**
  - **MHCA 2017:** informed consent; nominated representative; advance directive;
  - **Section 95:** ECT **only with anaesthesia and muscle relaxant** (no direct ECT).

### Technique adaptations for older adults
- **Electrode placement:**
  - **right unilateral (RUL)** (d'Elia), at **about 6× seizure threshold** with **ultrabrief pulse (0.25–0.3 ms)**, to minimise cognitive effects [1];
  - **bitemporal** if urgent (life-threatening food refusal, catatonia) or if RUL fails.
- **Seizure threshold rises with age:** use titration or age-based dosing. Older patients may need higher energy; watch for short or inadequate seizures.
- **Anaesthesia:**
  - methohexital, propofol or etomidate (etomidate lengthens seizures);
  - **succinylcholine** (check for osteoporosis; use a full dose for fracture prevention);
  - **β-blocker (labetalol, esmolol)** for the hypertensive surge in cardiac patients;
  - glycopyrrolate.
- **Frequency:** twice weekly (rather than three times) reduces cognitive load.
- **Monitoring:**
  - cardiac (arrhythmias, ischaemia);
  - **post-ictal delirium** (more common in the elderly);
  - falls;
  - **cognition** (MoCA before and after; weekly during the course).

### Adverse effects
- **Cognitive:**
  - **acute confusion and post-ictal delirium**;
  - **anterograde amnesia** (resolves within weeks);
  - **retrograde autobiographical memory loss** (mostly recovers; small persistent gaps possible);
  - reduced by **RUL ultrabrief pulse, twice-weekly sessions, titrated dosing** [1];
  - **no evidence of long-term cognitive decline or increased dementia risk**. Some registry data even suggest lower dementia risk [general knowledge; Osler 2018].
- **Cardiovascular:** transient bradycardia then tachycardia and hypertension; arrhythmias; rare ischaemia.
- **Other:** falls, headache, muscle pain, aspiration, fractures (rare with relaxant), dental injury.
- **Mortality:** about 2 per 100,000 treatments, lower than for antidepressants in the frail [general knowledge].

### Continuation and maintenance (the key problem)
- **Relapse after successful ECT is about 50% within 6 months** without continuation treatment [general knowledge].
- **PRIDE** (Prolonging Remission in Depressed Elderly) [1]:
  - **phase 1:** RUL ultrabrief ECT plus venlafaxine produced about **62% remission**;
  - **phase 2:** **continuation ECT (4 sessions over 1 month, then as needed) plus venlafaxine and lithium** was better than medication alone at preventing relapse, with no excess cognitive harm.
- **Options:**
  - **continuation pharmacotherapy:** nortriptyline + lithium (Sackeim 2001), or venlafaxine + lithium;
  - **continuation or maintenance ECT** (weekly, then tapering to monthly) for patients who relapse on medication.

### Contraindications and cautions
- **No absolute contraindications**.
- **High-risk conditions:**
  - raised ICP;
  - recent MI (within 3 months) or stroke;
  - unstable angina;
  - aneurysm;
  - severe COPD;
  - retinal detachment;
  - phaeochromocytoma.

**Conclusion.** ECT is **safe and highly effective** in older adults with severe, psychotic, suicidal or treatment-resistant depression. It often works faster and better than in younger patients. **RUL ultrabrief pulse** technique, **careful medical work-up**, **cognitive monitoring** and **continuation treatment** (PRIDE strategy) maximise benefit and minimise harm. Age alone is never a contraindication.

## In depth

### Background
- ECT was introduced by Cerletti and Bini (1938). Its use in older adults rose as antidepressant intolerance became apparent.
- In the US and UK, people over 65 are **over-represented** among ECT recipients (about a third or more) [general knowledge].

### Epidemiology
- Severe late-life depression with psychotic features occurs in about 20–45% of hospitalised older depressed patients [general knowledge].
- **India:** ECT is widely used in tertiary centres; age-specific data are limited [Guessing].

### Classification of ECT parameters
- **Placement:** bitemporal, bifrontal, RUL.
- **Pulse width:** brief (0.5–1.5 ms), ultrabrief (below 0.5 ms).
- **Dosing:** titration (stimulus dosing at 1.5–2.5× threshold for bitemporal, 6× for RUL ultrabrief), or fixed or age-based.
- **Course:** acute (6–12 sessions); continuation (up to 6 months); maintenance (beyond 6 months).

### Mechanism
- Proposed mechanisms (general knowledge):
  - **neuroplasticity:** BDNF, hippocampal neurogenesis, **hippocampal volume increase** on MRI;
  - **anticonvulsant hypothesis** (rising threshold correlates with response);
  - neuroendocrine (HPA normalisation);
  - monoamine changes;
  - network connectivity changes (DMN).
- **Ageing brain:** a higher threshold and reduced plasticity, yet a robust response. The reason is unclear.

### Clinical features predicting response in the elderly
- Psychotic features; psychomotor retardation; older age; shorter episode; no comorbid personality disorder.
- **White-matter hyperintensities** may predict more post-ECT delirium but **not** poorer response [general knowledge; mixed].

### Assessment in detail
- **Delirium risk:** prior delirium, dementia, polypharmacy (anticholinergics), dehydration.
- **Medications:**
  - **lithium:** hold or lower the dose (risk of delirium and prolonged seizure);
  - **benzodiazepines and anticonvulsants:** reduce or withhold the night before (they raise threshold);
  - **theophylline:** status epilepticus risk;
  - **anticoagulants:** continue warfarin if INR is in range (DVT patients).

### Differential: alternatives to ECT
| Option | Comparison |
| --- | --- |
| rTMS | Well tolerated; less effective for psychotic or severe depression; slower [1] |
| Pharmacological augmentation | Slower; intolerance |
| Ketamine/esketamine | Rapid; limited elderly data; dissociation, BP |
| Magnetic seizure therapy | Experimental; cognitive advantage [1] |
| DBS | Last resort [1] |

### Management: special populations
- **Dementia with depression:** ECT is effective. Use twice weekly RUL with close delirium monitoring and caregiver involvement.
- **Parkinson's disease:** depression improves, motor symptoms often improve transiently; reduce levodopa if dyskinesia emerges [1].
- **Post-stroke depression:** usually wait about 3 months after stroke. Case series show efficacy.
- **Cardiac disease:** cardiology input; β-blockers; continue anti-anginal drugs.

### Evidence table
| Study | Finding |
| --- | --- |
| O'Connor 2001, CORE (general knowledge) | Remission about 90% in over-65s vs 70% in under-45s |
| Sackeim 2001, *JAMA* (general knowledge) | Continuation nortriptyline + lithium: relapse 39% vs placebo 84% at 24 weeks |
| Kellner 2006, CORE continuation (general knowledge; verify) | 6-month relapse: C-ECT about 37% vs C-Pharm (nortriptyline + lithium) about 32%; neither ideal |
| **PRIDE phase 1 (Kellner 2016, *Am J Psychiatry*)** | RUL UB ECT + venlafaxine: 62% remission, 70% response [1; numbers from general knowledge] |
| **PRIDE phase 2 (Kellner 2016)** | STABLE + C-ECT better than STABLE alone on HAM-D; no cognitive harm |
| Osler 2018, *Lancet Psychiatry* (general knowledge) | Danish registry: ECT not associated with increased dementia risk |

### Critical appraisal
- RCTs comparing ECT with medication in the elderly are few. Most evidence is open-label or registry-based.
- **Selection bias:** sicker patients receive ECT.
- Cognitive outcome measures vary, and autobiographical memory is hard to quantify.
- **Stigma and access** limit use more than evidence does.

### Indian context
- **MHCA 2017 Section 95:** **ECT without anaesthesia and muscle relaxant is prohibited.**
- **MHCA 2017 Section 94(3):** ECT may **not** be used as emergency treatment without consent [Likely; check wording].
- Consent is via the patient, or a nominated representative with Mental Health Review Board oversight if the patient lacks capacity [general knowledge; see `mhca-2017-admission-procedures`].
- **Practice issues:**
  - modified ECT is available in most medical colleges;
  - anaesthesia capacity limits access in districts;
  - family consent culture.

### Recent advances
- **Individualised dosing** using seizure threshold titration; EEG seizure-quality markers.
- **Ketamine anaesthesia** (no clear benefit).
- **Focal electrically administered seizure therapy (FEAST)** and **MST**.
- **Predictive biomarkers** (hippocampal volume change, EEG).
