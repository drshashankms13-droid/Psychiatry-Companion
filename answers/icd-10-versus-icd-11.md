## Exam answer

**Definition.** ICD-11 is the eleventh revision of the WHO International Classification of Diseases. It was adopted by the World Health Assembly in May 2019 and member states began reporting health statistics with it on 1 January 2022 [1]. Chapter 06, *Mental, behavioural or neurodevelopmental disorders*, replaces ICD-10 Chapter V(F), which had been in use for almost 30 years. Its clinical companion, the **Clinical Descriptions and Diagnostic Requirements (CDDR)**, replaces the ICD-10 "blue book" (Clinical Descriptions and Diagnostic Guidelines) [1].

### Why ICD-10 had to change

- **Decimal coding** (F0–F9) allowed only ten groupings, so unrelated disorders were forced together (e.g. anxiety disorders inside "neurotic, stress-related and somatoform disorders") [1].
- An **organic versus non-organic** split that no longer matched the evidence, especially for sleep and sexual disorders [1].
- **Inconsistent content** across disorder groupings in the blue book [1].
- **Artificial comorbidity** and little-used categories (e.g. ten specific personality disorders, of which only borderline and dissocial were recorded with any frequency) [1].
- Separate childhood chapter that ignored lifespan presentations.

### Organising principles of the revision

1. **Clinical utility** as the core principle: the classification must help clinicians identify and treat patients, or it will not be used faithfully [1, 2].
2. **Global applicability**: working groups included every WHO region, with many members from low- and middle-income countries [1].
3. **Scientific validity**: working groups reviewed the evidence for each change [1].
4. **Harmonisation with DSM-5**, permitting only justified conceptual differences [1].

### Salient structural differences

| Feature | ICD-10 (Chapter V, F00–F99) | ICD-11 (Chapter 06, 6A00–6E8Z) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Coding | Decimal; maximum 10 blocks | Alphanumeric; **21 groupings** in Chapter 06 [1, 2] |
| Order | Kraepelinian: organic first, then psychoses, neuroses, personality | **Developmental**: neurodevelopmental first, neurocognitive last [1] |
| Childhood disorders | Separate F80–F98 grouping | Distributed across groupings (**lifespan approach**) [1] |
| Diagnostic style | Operationalised guidelines, symptom counts and durations | **Essential features**; arbitrary cut-offs avoided unless empirically supported [1] |
| Content per disorder | Variable | Uniform template: essential features, boundary with normality, boundary with other disorders, course, developmental, culture- and gender-related features [1, 2] |
| Dimensions | Almost none | Severity of personality disorder; symptom qualifiers for psychosis [1] |
| Sleep disorders | Split "organic"/"non-organic" | New **Chapter 07 Sleep–wake disorders** [1, 2] |
| Sexual dysfunction, gender | Mental disorders chapter | New **Chapter 17 Conditions related to sexual health**; gender identity disorder → **gender incongruence** [1] |
| Format | Print | Electronic foundation, multilingual, API-linked [2] |

### Salient changes by grouping (high-yield)

| Grouping | Key change [1, 2] |
| --- | --- |
| Neurodevelopmental | "Mental retardation" → **disorders of intellectual development**; childhood autism + Asperger's → **autism spectrum disorder**; **ADHD** replaces hyperkinetic disorder |
| Psychotic | **Schizophrenia subtypes abolished**; six symptom domains rated (positive, negative, depressive, manic, psychomotor, cognitive); first-rank symptoms de-emphasised; ATPD restricted to the polymorphic form |
| Catatonia | New **independent grouping** (associated with mental disorder; substance-induced; secondary) |
| Mood | **Bipolar type II** added; depression needs 5 of 10 symptoms with hopelessness added and fatigue no longer an entry symptom; mania needs **increased activity or energy**; mixed depressive and anxiety disorder moved to depressive disorders |
| Anxiety | Phobic/other split removed; grouped by **focus of apprehension**; separation anxiety and selective mutism moved here |
| OCRD | New grouping: OCD, **body dysmorphic, olfactory reference, hypochondriasis, hoarding**, trichotillomania, **excoriation** |
| Stress | **Complex PTSD** and **prolonged grief disorder** added; PTSD tightened to 3 core features; acute stress reaction no longer a mental disorder |
| Dissociative | "Conversion" dropped; dissociative neurological symptom disorder; **partial DID**; trance and possession trance separated |
| Feeding/eating | **Binge eating disorder, ARFID** added; anorexia BMI threshold raised to 18 kg/m², endocrine criterion dropped |
| Bodily distress | **Bodily distress disorder** replaces somatoform disorders and neurasthenia; defined by distress and excessive attention, not by absence of medical explanation |
| Substances | Single episode of harmful use; harm to others; **gaming disorder** with gambling disorder in "addictive behaviours" |
| Impulse control | Kleptomania, pyromania, **intermittent explosive**, **compulsive sexual behaviour disorder** |
| Personality | Ten types replaced by **severity (mild, moderate, severe)** + **five trait domains** + optional borderline pattern; personality difficulty outside mental disorders |
| Paraphilias | Focus on **non-consenting others**; fetishism, sadomasochism, fetishistic transvestism removed |
| Neurocognitive | Organic disorders renamed; mild neurocognitive disorder; dementia coded with aetiology |

### Is ICD-11 superior? Arguments for

- **Clinical utility:** field studies in clinical settings in 13–14 countries rated utility high; clinicians found it easier to use [1].
- **Reliability:** clinic-based field studies found reliability **at least as high** as criteria-based systems [1].
- **Accuracy in case-vignette studies:** the Global Clinical Practice Network (nearly 15,000 clinicians, 155 countries) found more accurate and consistent diagnoses with ICD-11 guidelines for most tested changes [1].
- **Validity gains:** removal of schizophrenia subtypes and personality disorder types that lacked predictive validity; new categories (complex PTSD, prolonged grief, bipolar II, binge eating) supported by evidence of distinct course and treatment [1].
- **Reduced residual categories:** binge eating disorder and partial DID absorb many former "unspecified" diagnoses [1].
- **Destigmatisation:** gender incongruence removed from mental disorders; "mental retardation" retired [1].
- **Global and cultural fit:** flexible guidelines and culture-related guidance for every disorder [1].

### Arguments that it is not very different (or not yet proven superior)

- **Same paradigm:** still descriptive, syndromal and categorical, without biomarkers or aetiology for most disorders, so its *validity* rests on the same foundations as ICD-10 and DSM-5.
- **Most core concepts unchanged:** schizophrenia symptoms, depressive episode and many anxiety disorders are largely the same as in ICD-10 [1].
- **Reliability evidence is still limited:** most reliability data come from WHO's own field studies, often with case vignettes; independent, real-world studies are few.
- **Flexibility cuts both ways:** fewer explicit cut-offs may lower inter-rater agreement in untrained hands.
- **New categories are contested:** gaming disorder, compulsive sexual behaviour disorder and prolonged grief disorder risk medicalising normal behaviour.
- **Implementation burden:** retraining, software and legal changes (insurance, disability and mental health law) slow uptake, especially in LMICs.

### Conclusion

ICD-11 is a genuine structural advance over ICD-10: it fixes coding constraints, removes the organic/non-organic split, adopts a lifespan and dimensional approach, and adds evidence-based categories, with field trials showing better utility and at least equal reliability. It is not a change of paradigm. Its validity remains that of a descriptive syndromal system, and the claim of superior reliability rests mostly on WHO-led studies. "Superior in utility, equal or modestly better in reliability, unchanged in fundamental validity" is the defensible position.

## In depth

### Background

- ICD began as the International List of Causes of Death (1893). Mental disorders were first included in ICD-6 (1948). ICD-8 (1965) introduced a glossary; ICD-9 (1975) was used in India for many years; ICD-10 was approved in 1990 and the CDDG blue book published in 1992, with Diagnostic Criteria for Research (DCR-10) in 1993.
- The ICD-11 revision started in 2007. The mental disorders process ran in parallel with DSM-5 (published 2013), with overlapping working-group membership [1].
- Timeline: alpha browser (May 2011), beta draft open for public comment (May 2012), implementation version released (18 June 2018), World Health Assembly approval (May 2019), reporting from 1 January 2022 [1, 2].
- WHO coordinated four chapters relevant to psychiatry: mental disorders; sleep–wake disorders; diseases of the nervous system; and conditions related to sexual health [1].

### Versions of the classification

| Version | Purpose |
| --- | --- |
| **ICD-11 MMS** (Mortality and Morbidity Statistics) | Short definitions and codes for statistical reporting |
| **CDDR** (earlier draft name CDDG) | Clinical descriptions and diagnostic requirements for mental health professionals |
| **Primary care version** | Simplified guidance for primary care |
| **Research use** | Researchers may apply stricter thresholds |

The **foundation component** is the electronic database from which all linearisations are produced [2].

### The CDDR template for each disorder [1, 2]

1. Essential (required) features
2. Additional clinical features
3. Boundary with normality (threshold)
4. Boundary with other disorders and conditions (differential diagnosis)
5. Coded qualifiers and subtypes
6. Course features
7. Developmental presentations (children, adolescents, older adults)
8. Culture-related features
9. Sex- and gender-related features

This uniform structure answered a major criticism of ICD-10, where content varied widely between groupings [1].

### The 21 groupings of Chapter 06 [1]

Neurodevelopmental; schizophrenia and other primary psychotic; catatonia; mood; anxiety and fear-related; obsessive-compulsive and related; disorders specifically associated with stress; dissociative; feeding and eating; elimination; bodily distress and bodily experience; substance use and addictive behaviours; impulse control; disruptive behaviour and dissocial; personality; paraphilic; factitious; neurocognitive; mental or behavioural disorders associated with pregnancy, childbirth and the puerperium; psychological and behavioural factors affecting disorders classified elsewhere; secondary mental or behavioural syndromes associated with disorders classified elsewhere.

### Selected criteria comparisons

| Item | ICD-10 | DSM-5 | ICD-11 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Schizophrenia symptoms | One from (a)–(d) or two from (e)–(h) | 2 of 5, one must be delusions, hallucinations or disorganised speech | 2 of the core list, at least one core symptom (persistent delusions, persistent hallucinations, thought disorder, experiences of influence/passivity/control) |
| Schizophrenia duration | 1 month | 6 months | 1 month |
| Functional impairment | Not required | Required | Not required |
| Subtypes | Present | Abolished | Abolished; symptom qualifiers |
| Depressive episode | 4 of 10 incl. 2 of 3 typical (mood, interest, fatigue) | 5 of 9 incl. mood or interest | 5 of 10 incl. mood or interest; clusters: affective, cognitive, neurovegetative |
| Mania entry | Elevated mood + increased activity | Elevated/irritable mood + increased activity or energy | Euphoria/irritability/expansiveness **and** increased activity or subjective energy |
| Bipolar II | Absent | Present | Present |
| PTSD | Within 6 months of trauma; low threshold | 4 clusters, 20 symptoms | 3 core features; re-experiencing in the here and now |
| Dependence | 3 of 6 in 12 months | Use disorder, 2 of 11 | 2 of 3 central features (impaired control, increasing priority, physiological features) within 12 months, or continuous use for 1 month [2] |
| Anorexia BMI | 17.5 | Severity specifier | 18; fat phobia not required |

(ICD-10 and ICD-11 entries from [2]; DSM-5 entries from author's knowledge of DSM-5.)

### Reliability and validity in detail

**Reliability**

- *Case-vignette (GCPN) studies:* standardised cases manipulated to test key differences; clinicians across regions and in six languages (Chinese, English, French, Japanese, Russian, Spanish) diagnosed more accurately with ICD-11 for most changes; results analysed by region to detect cultural problems [1].
- *Clinic-based studies:* conducted in 14 countries in the local language, in natural settings, focused on the disorders with the greatest burden; inter-rater reliability for ICD-11 guidelines was at least as high as that achieved with criteria-based systems [1]. The published reliability study (Reed et al., 2018) reported kappa values from roughly 0.45 (dysthymic disorder) to 0.88 (social anxiety disorder) [4].
- *Limitations:* small samples for some disorders, joint interviews rather than test–retest in some sites, sponsor-led research, and little evidence yet from routine Indian practice.

**Validity**

| Type | ICD-11 position |
| --- | --- |
| Content/face validity | Improved by uniform templates and boundary-with-normality sections |
| Predictive validity | Improved by dropping subtypes that did not predict course or treatment (schizophrenia, OCD, personality) [1] |
| Construct validity | Groupings now reflect shared aetiology (stress disorders) or phenomenology and validators (OCRD) [1] |
| Aetiological validity | Unchanged; no biological criteria for primary mental disorders |
| Cross-cultural validity | Improved by culture-related guidance and global field testing [1] |

The honest summary: ICD-11 improves **utility** and probably **predictive and construct validity** for some groupings, while **aetiological validity** remains the shared weakness of all current classifications.

### Critical appraisal

- **Clinical judgement versus criteria:** Reed et al. argue that essential features conform to how clinicians actually diagnose; critics argue that loose guidelines will widen variability between clinicians and countries.
- **Divergence from DSM-5:** complex PTSD, prolonged grief disorder (now also in DSM-5-TR), the dimensional personality model and 1-month schizophrenia duration create real differences that complicate research comparisons.
- **Gender incongruence** remains in ICD-11 (in Chapter 17) to protect access to health care in countries where services need a diagnosis; some advocates want it removed entirely [1].
- **Gaming disorder** was included over objections that the evidence base was immature; supporters cite public-health need and treatment seeking [1].
- **Personality disorder model** has high utility for non-specialists but abandons decades of category-based research and therapy trials; the optional borderline pattern is a compromise [1].
- **Implementation:** India still largely uses ICD-10 in records, insurance and legal documents; mental health law, disability certification tools and medical education will need revision.

### Indian context

- Indian psychiatrists contributed to the revision (e.g. members from AIIMS New Delhi in the substance use and child working groups) [1].
- Several ICD-11 features suit Indian practice: culture-related guidance, separation of **trance** and **possession trance disorder**, recognition of **bodily distress disorder** (common in Indian primary care), and the primary care version for task-sharing.
- Acute and transient psychotic disorder, important in India, is retained but narrowed to the polymorphic form; non-polymorphic acute psychoses move to "other primary psychotic disorder" [1].
- Transition issues: hospital information systems, insurance coding, MHCA 2017 documentation, disability assessment (IDEAS) and postgraduate curricula are still based on ICD-10.

### Recent advances

- **CDDR published by WHO (2024)** as the official clinical guide for ICD-11 Chapter 06.
- Ongoing implementation studies of ICD-11 in primary care and in LMIC health systems.
- Proposals to integrate dimensional and transdiagnostic models (HiTOP, RDoC) into future revisions.
