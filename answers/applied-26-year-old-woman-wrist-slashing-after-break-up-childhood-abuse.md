## Exam answer

**Summary.**
- A **26-year-old woman** presents to emergency after **slashing her wrist following a break-up**.
- Since then she feels **worthless** and cries incessantly.
- **Frequent fights and anger outbursts** with her boyfriend over trivial issues, during which she "**zones out**" (dissociation).
- Work disrupted, with a **reprimand from her employer**.
- **Childhood sexual abuse by an uncle**, with **frequent nightmares** and **light, broken sleep**.

### Formulation
1. **Borderline personality pattern:**
   - self-harm after interpersonal loss;
   - intense, unstable relationship with anger outbursts;
   - feelings of worthlessness;
   - **stress-related dissociation**;
   - occupational impairment.
2. **Trauma-related disorder:** childhood sexual abuse with **re-experiencing (nightmares)** and **hyperarousal (waking easily)**. Consider **PTSD**. BPD has high comorbidity with PTSD [1].
3. **Depressive episode:** worthlessness and persistent crying since the break-up.
4. **Rule out** substance use, which is common in BPD [1], and assess physical injury.

### Emergency management
1. **Medical:** treat the wound and assess blood loss and tendon or nerve injury. Register as a **medico-legal case** [3].
2. **MHCA 2017 [3]:**
   - **s115:** presumed **severe stress**; she is **not to be tried or punished**;
   - the state must provide **care, treatment and rehabilitation**;
   - **mandatory psychiatric referral** after the attempt.
3. **Suicide risk assessment by clinical inquiry [2]:**
   - intent, what she expected would happen, whether she sought help, how she feels now;
   - ongoing ideation and plans;
   - distinguish self-harm from an **attempt to die**: both can coexist [3];
   - risk factors: **female sex (attempts more common)**, recent relationship loss, **trauma**, depression, impulsivity [2];
   - protective factors: work, social support, access to care [5].
   - **Remember:** more than **75% of people with BPD attempt suicide** and 2–5% die [1].
4. **Disposition:** admission vs outpatient depends on her current state, **family or social support** and access to outpatient care [2].
5. **Crisis medication**, only if needed and **for no more than 1 week** [1]:
   - **avoid benzodiazepines**: disinhibition, especially short-acting ones;
   - avoid **TCAs** (overdose toxicity);
   - **promethazine 25–50 mg** if sedation is needed.

### Trauma-informed approach [4]
- **Principles:** safety; trustworthiness and transparency; peer support; collaboration and mutuality; empowerment; attention to cultural, historical and **gender** issues. **Safety first.**
- **Discussing the abuse:**
  - in **private**, without family present;
  - listen generously; do not press for every detail;
  - **ask what she wants documented**;
  - acknowledge her strength and thank her for trusting you.
- **Examination of the wound:** explain, ask permission, use clinical language, offer a chaperone, stay in sight.
- **If she dissociates:**
  - stop the procedure;
  - help her reorient; offer water; change rooms;
  - stay with her and reassure her she is safe;
  - use **grounding** such as square breathing (inhale 4, hold 4, exhale 4, hold 4).

### Ongoing management
**1. Psychological treatment: the core**
- DBT and other psychological therapies have **much better evidence** than medication for BPD [1].
- For PTSD, **psychological approaches should be used before drug treatments** [1].

**2. Treat comorbid conditions** under their own guidance [1]:
- **PTSD (Box 3.8) [1]:**
  - **first line:** SSRIs (**paroxetine, sertraline or fluoxetine**) or **venlafaxine MR**;
  - **second line:** mirtazapine, phenelzine, olanzapine, quetiapine, risperidone. Antipsychotics help **intrusions and nightmares**, not avoidance or hyperarousal;
  - **prazosin 2–15 mg at night** for **nightmares and sleep disturbance**. Start at 1 mg; titrate slowly because of hypotension.
- **Depression:** usual treatment.

**3. BPD-specific medication: not routine** [1]:
- Drug treatment should not be used routinely for BPD itself (NICE).
- Possible targeted options:
  - anger: aripiprazole, topiramate;
  - **omega-3** for impulsivity and affective dysregulation;
  - **naltrexone** has very limited evidence for **dissociative** symptoms.
- **Avoid valproate** in a woman of child-bearing age.
- Caution with **fluoxetine**: one RCT found more suicide attempts when it was added to DBT. Weigh this if an SSRI is chosen for PTSD or depression.

**4. Occupational and social support:** address work problems; build supports; offer a crisis plan.

**5. Follow-up:** frequent review; reassess suicide risk each time [2].

**Conclusion.** This woman's impulsive self-harm after a break-up, unstable and angry relationships, worthlessness, stress-related dissociation and childhood sexual abuse with nightmares suggest **borderline personality disorder with probable comorbid PTSD and depression**. Steps:
1. Medical and medico-legal care, with MHCA protections.
2. Careful clinical suicide-risk assessment.
3. Trauma-informed engagement.
4. Psychological therapy as the core.
5. Treatment of PTSD (an SSRI or venlafaxine; prazosin for nightmares) and depression, with minimal, safe, time-limited medication for BPD itself.

## In depth

### Why benzodiazepines are a poor choice here [1]
- They cause **disinhibition** in BPD, especially short-acting ones.
- They are "relatively ineffective" in PTSD.
